The author argues that Constantine’s strategic foresight in establishing Constantinople proved prescient as the Huns invaded Europe around 372 A.D. [PDF p. 61]. The Huns first destroyed the Alans and subjugated the Ostrogoths before forcing the Visigoths to flee toward the Danube [PDF p. 61]. In 376, the desperate Visigoths, numbering nearly 200,000, petitioned Emperor Valens for asylum [PDF p. 62]. The author characterizes Valens as a cowardly and avaricious ruler who reluctantly permitted the Goths to cross the river as subject allies [PDF p. 62]. However, Roman officials, particularly Governor Lupicinus, betrayed the agreement by withholding food and accepting bribes to allow the Goths to retain their weapons [PDF p. 63]. This extortion led to starvation and the sale of children, sparking a violent revolt after Lupicinus attempted to assassinate Gothic leaders [PDF p. 64].

The conflict escalated into a year of fierce fighting along the Danube [PDF p. 64]. In 378, Emperor Valens launched a major offensive against the Goths near Adrianople [PDF p. 65]. The author notes that the Goths had adopted cavalry tactics, which proved decisive when their horsemen arrived to reinforce the infantry [PDF p. 66]. The Gothic cavalry crushed the Roman flanks, causing the imperial army to collapse into a suffocating melee [PDF p. 66]. Valens and forty thousand soldiers died in what the author describes as the most fearful defeat since Cannae [PDF p. 67]. Although the victorious Goths marched on Constantinople, they abandoned the siege upon seeing the city’s massive walls [PDF p. 67].
