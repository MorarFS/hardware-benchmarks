The author describes the Blues and Greens as factions that evolved from circus groups into dangerous political parties by 532 [PDF p. 101]. Justinian ordered the execution of riot leaders, but a botched hanging allowed two criminals to escape into sanctuary, sparking six days of rioting [PDF p. 102]. The factions united under the slogan "Nika," demanding the dismissal of unpopular ministers and the deposition of Justinian [PDF p. 102]. With the city largely undefended, the mob seized control, burning the Senate House and St. Sophia [PDF p. 103]. They crowned Hypatius as emperor, though he had initially sought refuge with Justinian [PDF p. 103]. Ministers urged the Emperor to flee, but the author notes that Empress Theodora refused to abandon the capital [PDF p. 105]. She delivered a speech arguing that imperial dignity was preferable to exile, a moment the author presents as decisive [PDF p. 105]. Spurred by her resolve, Justinian ordered a final assault led by Belisarius [PDF p. 105]. Imperial forces breached the Hippodrome, routing the rebels in a massacre that the author states killed thirty-five thousand people [PDF p. 106]. The author observes that while the factions continued to riot for fifty years, they never again threatened the regime so severely [PDF p. 106]. This narrative highlights the fragility of Justinian’s power and the critical role of Theodora’s leadership in preserving the throne during the Nika Sedition [PDF p. 105]. The events demonstrate how urban unrest could escalate into a near-revolution before being crushed by military force [PDF p. 106].
