The author argues that the Byzantine Empire ceased to be a vital bulwark for Christendom after 1370, asserting that its prolonged existence resulted from the Paleologi’s subservience to the Turks [PDF p. 358]. The text suggests that an earlier fall of Constantinople would have only marginally altered European history, potentially affecting Genoese and Venetian commerce or delaying the Renaissance [PDF p. 359]. John Paleologus endured a troubled reign marked by his son Andronicus’s rebellions, which ended with Andronicus’s blinding and the succession of Manuel II [PDF p. 359]. Manuel II, though capable, lacked the resources to resist Ottoman dominance until the 1402 defeat of Sultan Bayezid by Tamerlane created a power vacuum [PDF p. 360]. Manuel exploited this Turkish civil war to regain territories, but the opportunity was squandered because European powers like Hungary were distracted by internal conflicts with the Hussites [PDF p. 361-363]. When Murad II unified the Ottoman realm, he reclaimed the lost lands and besieged Constantinople, forcing Manuel to accept vassalage once more [PDF p. 363-365]. The author describes the subsequent decades as a period of unredeemed gloom, noting that the empire’s physical and economic decay was severe [PDF p. 365-366]. John VI. failed to recover Thessalonica, which was sold to Venice and annexed by the Ottomans [PDF p. 367]. Finally, John VIII. traveled to Italy to secure aid through religious union with Rome, but he received only minimal support because the papacy was weakened by internal schisms [PDF p. 367].
