The author argues that Latin decayed in the East due to practical separation from the West and barbarian devastation [PDF p. 170]. By the seventh century, Latin knowledge was rare among educated men [PDF p. 169]. Justinian was the last emperor to speak Latin natively [PDF p. 169]. The author attributes this linguistic shift to the division of the empire and the extermination of Latin-speaking populations by Slavs [PDF p. 170]. Only Africa and the Italian Exarchate retained significant Latin speakers [PDF p. 170].

Simultaneously, the empire became increasingly Christian [PDF p. 171]. The author notes that Christian influence on state machinery began slowly under Constantine [PDF p. 171]. Theodosius I initiated state suppression of moral sins [PDF p. 171]. The author credits the monk Telemachus with ending gladiatorial games through his martyrdom in 404 [PDF p. 171]. Christianity also ended infanticide and modified slavery by emphasizing the soul’s equality [PDF p. 173]. Justinian’s laws reflected this shift by permitting slave-free intermarriage [PDF p. 173].

However, the author evaluates the rise of monasticism as dangerous to the state [PDF p. 174]. He claims ascetics neglected civic duties and viewed barbarian invasions as divine judgments [PDF p. 175]. This apathy angered pagans who blamed Christianity for the empire’s ruin [PDF p. 176]. The author praises governing classes for maintaining Roman statecraft despite these pressures [PDF p. 176]. Paganism disappeared by the fifth century, though indifference persisted among educated Christians [PDF p. 176].
