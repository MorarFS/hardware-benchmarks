Around A.D. 372, the Huns moved westward, nearly exterminating the Alans and crushing the Ostrogoths [PDF p. 61]. The Visigoths, fleeing the Huns, reached the Danube and requested permission from the Emperor to cross the border [PDF p. 62]. The author characterizes the Eastern Emperor, Valens, as a stupid, cowardly, and avaricious prince [PDF p. 62]. Valens allowed the Goths to settle as subject-allies provided they surrendered their arms and gave hostages [PDF p. 63]. However, Roman officials accepted bribes to let the Goths retain weapons, and the governor Lupicinus engaged in shameless extortion by withholding food [PDF p. 63]. This desperation led to a violent affray at Marcianopolis, prompting Fritigern to lead the Goths in an armed revolt against Rome [PDF p. 63].

The conflict escalated into a year of fighting, eventually leading Valens to personally lead an army against the Goths in A.D. 378 [PDF p. 64]. During the Battle of Adrianople, Gothic cavalry charged the Roman flank, causing a catastrophic collapse of the Roman infantry [PDF p. 66]. The author describes this as the most fearful Roman defeat since Cannae, resulting in the death of Valens and forty thousand men [PDF p. 67]. Although the Goths approached Constantinople, they failed to attack the city walls [PDF p. 67].
