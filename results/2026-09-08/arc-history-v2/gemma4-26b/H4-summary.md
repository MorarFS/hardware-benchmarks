The author characterizes the final seventy-five years of the Byzantine Empire as a mere piece of local history that no longer serves as an important thread in Christendom [PDF p. 358]. The era began with the reign of John Paleologus, whose long life was vexed by the rebellion of his son, Andronicus [PDF p. 359]. After John died in 1391, he left the throne to his second son, Manuel II [PDF p. 359]. Although Manuel showed signs of capacity, he lacked the military strength to resist the Turks [PDF p. 360]. A temporary opportunity for liberation arose in 1402 when the conqueror Timour defeated Sultan Bayezid at Angora, causing the Ottoman Empire to fracture into civil war [PDF p. 360]. Manuel exploited this strife to sell his aid to Suleiman for a heavy price, regaining several territories [PDF p. 361]. However, the author notes that Europe failed to seize this unparalleled opportunity to expel the Ottomans [PDF p. 362].

The middle period saw the rise of Mohammed, a man of genius who reunited the Ottoman realm by 1421 [PDF p. 362]. This shift forced Manuel to cede his recovered lands back to the Sultan in 1422 [PDF p. 365]. Following Manuel's death, his son John VI presided over a period of unredeemed gloom [PDF p. 365]. The author describes a state of extreme exhaustion where the empire's population had shrunk and its wealth had passed to Italian merchants [PDF p. 366]. John VI attempted to secure Western aid by converting to Roman Catholicism at the Council of Florence in 1439, but the author evaluates this as a poor return, as the papacy was too discredited and preoccupied to provide significant help [PDF p. 367].
