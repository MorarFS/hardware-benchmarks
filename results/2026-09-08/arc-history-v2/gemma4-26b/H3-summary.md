The eighty years following the death of Basil the Macedonian were the most monotonous in the empire's history [PDF p. 241]. The author characterizes the era by the reigns of Leo the Wise and Constantine Porphyrogenitus, describing them as mild, inoffensive men of literature who were a strange issue from the adventurer Basil [PDF p. 241]. During the period of 880–960, the empire faced few foreign wars, experiencing only vexatious but non-dangerous piratical raids from Russians and Saracens [PDF p. 242]. Leo's twenty-six-year reign included an unsuccessful Bulgarian invasion and a disgraceful Saracen raid on Thessalonica in 904 [PDF p. 242].

Constantine VII ascended the throne at age seven under various guardians, including the ambitious Romanus Lecapenus, who usurped power and kept the rightful heir in the background [PDF p. 243]. Constantine eventually regained power after a popular uprising against the sons of Romanus [PDF p. 243]. The author evaluates Constantine as a somewhat weak, ineffective, but non-tyrannical ruler [PDF p. 243]. Both emperors' primary achievements were their books [PDF p. 244]. Leo produced a valuable manual on warfare [PDF p. 244], while Constantine wrote works on administration and court ceremonies [PDF p. 246]. The author concludes that these literary emperors signaled an end to previous intellectual destitution [PDF p. 247].
