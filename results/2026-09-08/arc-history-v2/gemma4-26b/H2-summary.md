The Latin language declined in the East as the Roman element vanished by the seventh century [PDF p. 169]. The author notes that while official titles like "patrician" survived, they became mere names [PDF p. 169]. This decay resulted from the division of the empire and the isolation of the East from the Latin West [PDF p. 170]. Furthermore, the author observes that the process was accelerated because the Latin-speaking provinces suffered most from barbarian incursions, such as those by the Visigoths, Ostrogoths, and Slavs [PDF p. 170]. By A.D. 620, less than one-tenth of the provincials east of the Adriatic spoke Latin [PDF p. 170].

Simultaneously, the empire became increasingly Christian [PDF p. 171]. The author evaluates this shift as having improved the conditions of vulnerable classes, such as infants and slaves, by emphasizing the importance of the individual soul [PDF p. 172, 173]. However, the author characterizes the rise of ascetic monasticism as "positively dangerous" because it led thousands to abandon civic duties [PDF p. 174]. The author judges that this apathetic attitude toward state calamities hampered the empire's fight against barbarians [PDF p. 175]. Ultimately, while paganism disappeared by the end of the fifth century, a widespread indifference prevailed among the educated classes [PDF p. 176].
