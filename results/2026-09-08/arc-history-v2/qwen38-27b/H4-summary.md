The author argues that the Byzantine Empire’s final decades constitute mere local history, as its role as a "bulwark of Christendom" shifted to the Servians and Hungarians after 1370 [PDF p. 358]. He contends that the Paleologi protracted the empire’s life through subservience to the Turks, even after justification for its existence had vanished [PDF p. 358]. The author suggests that an earlier fall in 1370 would have only modestly altered European history, primarily affecting Venetian and Genoese commerce and the timing of the Renaissance [PDF p. 358-359].

John Paleologus’s reign was marked by rebellion from his son Andronicus, who was blinded but retained enough sight to rebel again, leading John to pass the throne to Manuel II in 1391 [PDF p. 359]. Manuel II, though capable, lacked resources to resist the Ottomans until Tamerlane’s defeat of Sultan Bayezid in 1402 created a power vacuum [PDF p. 360]. Manuel exploited this civil war to recover Thessalonica and other territories, retaining them until 1421 when Mohammed II reunified the Ottoman realm [PDF p. 360-362]. The author notes that Europe missed this opportunity to expel the Ottomans because King Sigismund of Hungary was preoccupied with the Hussites, and Serb-Hungarian relations were hostile [PDF p. 362-363].

Murad II’s subsequent siege of Constantinople failed due to the city’s walls and a reported miraculous vision, resulting in a peace treaty that reduced the empire to vassalage [PDF p. 363-365]. Manuel II died in 1425, leaving behind a state of exhaustion and poverty, with commerce dominated by Italians and a small mercenary army [PDF p. 365-366]. His son John VI sold Thessalonica to Venice, prompting Ottoman annexation in 1430 [PDF p. 366-367]. Finally, John VI traveled to Italy in 1438 to seek aid, converting to Roman Catholicism at the Council of Florence, but received only modest financial and military support from Pope Eugenius IV [PDF p. 367].
