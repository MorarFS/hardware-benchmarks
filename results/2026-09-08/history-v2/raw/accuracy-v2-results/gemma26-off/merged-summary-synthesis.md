Around A.D. 372, the Huns moved westward from the Don and Volga, nearly exterminating the Alans and attacking the Goths [PDF p. 61]. The Ostrogoths were crushed into vassalage, while a remnant fled toward the Danube [PDF p. 61]. The Huns also attacked the Visigoths, who retreated to the Danube under Duke Fritigern [PDF p. 61]. The author judges that the Visigoths found surrender intolerable because they were more civilized and Christian than their eastern counterparts [PDF p. 62]. In A.D. 376, the Visigoths requested permission to cross the border [PDF p. 62]. The author characterizes the Eastern Emperor, Valens, as a stupid, cowardly, and avaricious prince [PDF p. 62]. Although Valens allowed the Goths to settle as subject-allies provided they surrendered arms and gave hostages, Roman officials accepted bribes to let the Goths keep weapons [PDF p. 63]. The governor, Lupicinus, further extorted the immigrants by withholding food [PDF p. 63]. This desperation caused a violent affray at Marcianopolis, leading Fritigern to take up arms against Rome [PDF p. 64]. In A.D. 378, Valens engaged the Goths near Adrianople [PDF p. 65]. The author observes that the Goths had learned to fight on horseback, which he calls a new development in warfare [PDF p. 65]. A Gothic cavalry charge caused confusion among the Roman infantry, resulting in the death of forty thousand men and the Emperor [PDF p. 66]. The author describes this as the most fearful Roman defeat since Cannae [PDF p. 67].

The author describes a decay of the Latin tongue in the East, noting that a large Latin-speaking population in the Balkans vanished by the seventh century [PDF p. 169]. This decline is attributed to the division of the empire and the decimation of Latin-speaking provinces by barbarians such as the Visigoths and Slavs [PDF p. 170]. By A.D. 620, less than one tenth of provincials east of the Adriatic spoke Latin [PDF p. 170]. Simultaneously, the empire became increasingly Christian [PDF p. 171]. The author evaluates Christianity as a force that improved the condition of vulnerable classes by emphasizing the importance of the individual soul [PDF p. 172, 173]. However, the author judges the rise of ascetic monasticism as positively dangerous because it led thousands to abandon civic duties [PDF p. 174]. This apathetic attitude caused many to view barbarian invasions as divine judgments rather than national calamities [PDF p. 175]. While the governing classes fought to preserve the state, the author notes that paganism disappeared by the end of the fifth century, replaced by widespread indifference among the educated classes [PDF p. 175, 176].

The eighty years following the death of Basil the Macedonian are characterized by the author as the most monotonous in the empire's history [PDF p. 241]. This era was defined by the reigns of Leo the Wise and Constantine Porphyrogenitus, whom the author describes as mild, inoffensive men of literature [PDF p. 241]. Although the period 880–960 was less troubled by foreign wars, Leo's reign was interrupted by a Bulgarian invasion and a Saracen raid on Thessalonica in 904 [PDF p. 242]. The author evaluates the loss of Thessalonica as a disgraceful incident for Leo's administration [PDF p. 242]. Constantine VII's reign began under guardians, including the emperor-regent Alexander and the ambitious admiral Romanus Lecapenus, who usurped power [PDF p. 243]. Constantine eventually regained power following a popular uprising against the sons of Romanus [PDF p. 243]. The author describes Constantine as a somewhat weak and ineffective ruler [PDF p. 243]. The author identifies the chief achievements of both emperors as their books [PDF p. 244]. Leo's military manual provides a detailed picture of the Byzantine army and fleet [PDF p. 244, 246], while Constantine's works demonstrate his skill as a methodical compiler [PDF p. 246].

The author characterizes the final seventy-five years of the Byzantine Empire as a mere piece of local history that no longer serves as an important thread in Christendom [PDF p. 358]. The author judges the Paleologi dynasty as having practiced a base subservience to the Turks, which he claims unnecessarily protracted the empire's existence [PDF p. 358]. During the reign of John Paleologus, the emperor was vexed by his son Andronicus, who rebelled and was blinded [PDF p. 359]. Consequently, John left the throne to his second son, Manuel II [PDF p. 359]. Manuel II experienced potential liberation when the Tartars defeated Sultan Bayezid at Angora in 1402 [PDF p. 360]. Manuel sold his aid to the pretender Suleiman, briefly regaining significant territories [PDF p. 361]. However, the rise of Mohammed reunited the Ottoman realm by 1421 [PDF p. 362]. After Manuel's death, his successor John VI presided over an empire in a state of exhaustion and debility [PDF p. 365]. Despite John's attempt to secure Western aid through conversion to Roman Catholicism at the Council of Florence, the author notes he received only a small sum of money and three hundred mercenaries [PDF p. 367].
