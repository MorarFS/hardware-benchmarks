The author characterizes the final seventy-five years of the Byzantine Empire as a mere piece of local history that no longer serves as an important thread in Christendom [PDF p. 358]. The author judges the Paleologi dynasty as having practiced a base subservience to the Turks, which unnecessarily protracted the empire's existence [PDF p. 358]. During the reign of John Paleologus, the emperor was vexed by the undutiful behavior of his son, Andronicus, who rebelled and was eventually blinded [PDF p. 359]. Consequently, John left the throne to his second son, Manuel II [PDF p. 359].

Manuel II experienced a period of potential liberation when the Tartars defeated Sultan Bayezid at Angora in 1402, causing Ottoman civil war [PDF p. 360]. Manuel sold his aid to the pretender Suleiman, briefly regaining significant territories [PDF p. 361]. However, the rise of Mohammed, a man of genius, reunited the Ottoman realm by 1421 [PDF p. 362]. After Manuel's death, his successor John VI presided over an empire in a state of exhaustion and debility [PDF p. 365]. Despite John's attempt to secure Western aid through conversion to Roman Catholicism at the Council of Florence, the author notes he received only a small sum of money and three hundred mercenaries [PDF p. 367].
