Around A.D. 372, the Huns moved westward from the Don and Volga, nearly exterminating the Alans before attacking the Goths [PDF p. 61]. The Ostrogoths were crushed and forced into vassalage, while a remnant fled toward the Danube [PDF p. 61]. The Huns then attacked the Visigoths, who, under Duke Fritigern, retreated to the Danube [PDF p. 61]. The author notes that the Visigoths found surrender intolerable because they were more civilized and Christian than their eastern brethren [PDF p. 62]. In A.D. 376, the Visigoths requested permission to cross the border [PDF p. 62]. The author characterizes the Eastern Emperor, Valens, as a stupid, cowardly, and avaricious prince [PDF p. 62]. Valens eventually allowed the Goths to settle as subject-allies if they surrendered their arms and provided hostages [PDF p. 63].

However, Roman officials accepted bribes to let the Goths keep their weapons, and the governor, Lupicinus, extorted the immigrants by withholding food [PDF p. 63]. This desperation led to a violent affray at Marcianopolis, prompting Fritigern to take up arms against Rome [PDF p. 64]. In A.D. 378, Valens engaged the Goths near Adrianople [PDF p. 65]. The author observes that the Goths had learned to fight on horseback, a new development in warfare [PDF p. 65]. A Gothic cavalry charge caused a fatal confusion among the Roman infantry, resulting in the death of forty thousand men and the Emperor himself [PDF p. 66]. The author describes this as the most fearful Roman defeat since Cannae [PDF p. 67]. Although the Goths approached Constantinople, they failed to attack the city walls and retreated [PDF p. 67].
