The author describes the decay of the Latin tongue in the East, noting that while a large Latin-speaking population existed in the Balkans, this element vanished by the seventh century [PDF p. 169]. The author attributes this decline to the division of the empire and the decimation of Latin-speaking provinces by barbarians like the Visigoths and Slavs [PDF p. 170]. By A.D. 620, less than one tenth of provincials east of the Adriatic spoke Latin [PDF p. 170].

Simultaneously, the empire became increasingly Christian [PDF p. 171]. The author evaluates Christianity as a force that improved the condition of vulnerable classes, such as infants and slaves, by emphasizing the importance of the individual soul [PDF p. 172, 173]. However, the author judges the rise of ascetic monasticism as "positively dangerous" because it led thousands to abandon civic duties to focus on personal salvation [PDF p. 174]. This "apathetic attitude" caused many to view barbarian invasions as divine judgments rather than national calamities [PDF p. 175]. While the author notes that the governing classes fought to preserve the state, paganism effectively disappeared by the end of the fifth century, replaced by a widespread indifference among the educated classes [PDF p. 175, 176].
