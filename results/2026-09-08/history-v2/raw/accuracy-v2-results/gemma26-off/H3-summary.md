The eighty years following the death of Basil the Macedonian were the most monotonous in the empire's history [PDF p. 241]. The author characterizes this era as being defined by the reigns of Leo the Wise and Constantine Porphyrogenitus, whom the author describes as mild, inoffensive men of literature [PDF p. 241]. While the author notes that the period 880–960 was less troubled by foreign wars than other eras, Leo's reign was interrupted by a Bulgarian invasion and a Saracen raid on Thessalonica in 904 [PDF p. 242]. The author evaluates the loss of Thessalonica as a disgraceful incident for Leo's administration [PDF p. 242].

Constantine VII's reign began under the tutelage of guardians, including the emperor-regent Alexander and later the ambitious admiral Romanus Lecapenus, who usurped power and kept the rightful heir in the background [PDF p. 243]. Constantine eventually regained power after a popular uprising against the sons of Romanus [PDF p. 243]. The author describes Constantine as a somewhat weak and ineffective ruler [PDF p. 243]. Ultimately, the author identifies the chief achievements of both emperors as their books [PDF p. 244]. Leo's military manual provides a detailed picture of the organized Byzantine army and fleet [PDF p. 244, 246], while Constantine's works, though sometimes focused on trifling topics like court ceremonies, demonstrate his skill as a methodical compiler [PDF p. 246].
