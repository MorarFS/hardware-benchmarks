The author describes the Blues and Greens as factions that evolved from circus groups into dangerous political parties by 532 [PDF p. 101]. Their unrest began when rioters demanded the execution of leaders, but a botched hanging allowed two criminals to escape into sanctuary [PDF p. 102]. This incident triggered six days of rioting where the factions united under the slogan "Nika" to demand the dismissal of unpopular ministers [PDF p. 102]. The mob refused to disperse and attempted to install Hypatius as emperor, exploiting the city’s lack of troops [PDF p. 102]. Belisarius led the Imperial Guard in a counterattack, but the fighting caused a massive fire that destroyed St. Sophia and other buildings [PDF p. 103]. Hypatius was eventually forced into the Hippodrome and crowned by the insurgents [PDF p. 103]. While ministers urged Justinian to flee, the author notes that Empress Theodora refused to leave, arguing that imperial dignity was preferable to exile [PDF p. 105]. Spurred by her resolve, Justinian ordered a final assault on the Hippodrome [PDF p. 105]. Belisarius’s troops breached the gates and slaughtered the rebels, resulting in approximately 35,000 deaths [PDF p. 106]. The author observes that despite this violence, the factions continued to riot for fifty years [PDF p. 106]. However, the text qualifies that they never again threatened the regime as severely as during the Nika Sedition [PDF p. 106]. The narrative concludes by highlighting the unique historical impact of this specific uprising compared to later disturbances [PDF p. 106].
