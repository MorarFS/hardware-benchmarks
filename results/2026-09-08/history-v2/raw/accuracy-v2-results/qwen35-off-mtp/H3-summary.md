The author characterizes the eighty years following Basil the Macedonian’s death as the most monotonous period in imperial history, dominated by the literary reigns of Leo VI and Constantine VII [PDF p. 241]. While these emperors were mild men of letters rather than military conquerors, the era experienced relative peace, with external threats limited to vexatious but non-dangerous piratical raids [PDF p. 242]. The author notes that the capture of Thessalonica by Saracens in 904 was a disgraceful administrative failure, though it occurred by surprise [PDF p. 242]. Constantine VII’s path to power was obstructed by regents, including his uncle Alexander and the ambitious admiral Romanus Lecapenus, until popular wrath removed the Lecapenus family, allowing Constantine to rule for twenty years [PDF p. 243]. The author evaluates their chief achievements as literary rather than political, highlighting Leo’s military manual as a valuable record of Byzantine organization and tactics [PDF p. 244]. This work reveals a highly disciplined army with specialized units and medical support, reflecting confidence in the empire’s defensive capabilities [PDF p. 245]. Constantine’s writings, particularly on court ceremonies, demonstrate his methodical nature and compensation for his lack of real power during his youth [PDF p. 247]. The author concludes that the literary output of these emperors signals the end of the intellectual dearth that had plagued Byzantium since the seventh century, marking a significant cultural revival [PDF p. 247].
