The author argues that Constantine’s strategic foresight in establishing Constantinople proved vital as the Huns displaced the Goths [PDF p. 61]. After crushing the Ostrogoths, the Huns forced the Visigoths to seek refuge across the Danube in 376 [PDF p. 62]. The Eastern Emperor Valens, described by the author as stupid and cowardly, permitted their entry under strict conditions [PDF p. 63]. However, Roman officials, particularly Governor Lupicinus, engaged in extortion and withheld food supplies, driving the starving Goths to desperation [PDF p. 64]. A banquet incident at Marcianopolis triggered open rebellion, leading to a year of fierce fighting along the Balkans [PDF p. 64]. The author notes that while the Goths had adopted some civilization, their barbarian spirit remained potent [PDF p. 64]. In 378, Emperor Valens launched a major offensive against the Gothic forces near Adrianople [PDF p. 65]. The author highlights a critical shift in military tactics, noting that the Goths had adopted cavalry warfare, which contrasted sharply with traditional Roman infantry dominance [PDF p. 65]. During the battle, Gothic cavalry arrived unexpectedly and routed the Roman flanks [PDF p. 66]. The resulting crush of infantry led to a catastrophic defeat, killing forty thousand soldiers and the Emperor himself [PDF p. 67]. The author characterizes this as the most fearful defeat since Cannae, effectively destroying the Eastern Roman army [PDF p. 67]. Although the Goths advanced toward Constantinople, they abandoned the siege due to the city’s formidable walls [PDF p. 67]. The author concludes that this event validated Constantine’s earlier prescience regarding the capital’s defensive importance [PDF p. 67].
