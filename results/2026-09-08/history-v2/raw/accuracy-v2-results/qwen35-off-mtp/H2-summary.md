The author argues that Latin decayed in the East due to practical separation from the West and barbarian devastation [PDF p. 170]. By the seventh century, Latin knowledge was rare, with Justinian being the last emperor to speak it natively [PDF p. 169]. The author attributes this linguistic shift to the division of the empire and the extermination of Latin-speaking populations by Slavs [PDF p. 170]. Simultaneously, the empire became increasingly Christian [PDF p. 171]. The author notes that Christian influence on state machinery began slowly, accelerating under Theodosius I [PDF p. 171]. The author highlights that gladiatorial games ended after a monk’s death, not state decree [PDF p. 171]. Christianity also abolished infanticide and modified slavery by emphasizing the soul’s equality [PDF p. 173]. The author credits these social improvements to the Christian belief in individual worth [PDF p. 174]. However, the author explicitly evaluates the rise of monasticism as dangerous [PDF p. 174]. The author claims monks neglected civic duties, harming the state during barbarian invasions [PDF p. 175]. The author describes this apathy as maddening to pagans [PDF p. 176]. The author concludes that governing classes preserved Roman traditions despite this religious shift [PDF p. 176]. The author observes that paganism vanished, though indifference persisted among educated Christians [PDF p. 176]. The author suggests that while Christianity improved social conditions, its ascetic elements posed significant political risks to the empire’s stability [PDF p. 174].
