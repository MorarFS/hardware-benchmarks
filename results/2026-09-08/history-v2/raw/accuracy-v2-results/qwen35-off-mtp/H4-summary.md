The author argues that the Byzantine Empire ceased to be a vital bulwark for Christendom after 1370, asserting that its prolonged existence resulted from the Paleologi’s subservience to the Turks [PDF p. 358]. The text suggests that an earlier fall of Constantinople would have only marginally altered European history, potentially affecting Italian commerce and the timing of the Renaissance [PDF p. 359]. During the reign of John Paleologus, internal strife weakened the state, as his son Andronicus rebelled and was subsequently blinded [PDF p. 359]. John’s successor, Manuel II, lacked the resources for rebellion but capitalized on the Ottoman civil war triggered by Tamerlane’s victory in 1402 [PDF p. 360]. Manuel secured temporary territorial gains by selling his support to Turkish pretenders, yet the author notes that Europe missed a rare opportunity to expel the Ottomans due to Hungarian conflicts with the Hussites and poor relations with Serbia [PDF p. 362]. When Murad II unified the Ottoman realm, he reclaimed the territories Manuel had gained, forcing the Byzantines back into vassalage [PDF p. 363]. The author describes the subsequent decades as a period of unredeemed gloom, characterized by the physical decay of Constantinople and its economic dependence on Italian merchants [PDF p. 365]. John VI. maintained peace but lost Thessalonica to the Ottomans after his brother sold it to Venice [PDF p. 367]. Finally, John VIII. traveled to Italy to secure aid through religious union with Rome, but the author evaluates this effort as futile because the papacy was too weakened by internal schisms to provide meaningful military support [PDF p. 367].
