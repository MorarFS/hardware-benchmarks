The author states that the last seventy‑five years of the Byzantine Empire (1370‑1453) are a piece of local history and no longer form an important thread in the history of Christendom [PDF p. 358]. He evaluates that a Turkish capture of Constantinople in 1370 would not have greatly altered Eastern‑European events because the empire had already ceased to serve as the “bulwark of Christendom” and that duty fell to the Serbians and Hungarians for the next one‑hundred‑and‑fifty years [PDF p. 358]. The author further judges that the Paleologi, by their subservience to the Turks, extended the empire’s life after its justification had disappeared [PDF p. 358].

John Paleologus’s long reign is described as troubled by his son Andronicus, who rebelled twice, briefly seized the throne, allied with a son of Murad I, and was eventually blinded in an ineffectual operation, after which he rebelled again [PDF p. 359]. On John’s death in 1391 the throne passed to his second son Manuel II, whose limited resources left him unable to challenge Ottoman power except during the crisis of 1402 when Timur’s invasion defeated Sultan Bayezid and threw the Ottoman realm into civil war [PDF p. 360‑361]. Manuel sold aid to the pretender Suleiman for a heavy price and, while the Ottoman princes fought each other, temporarily regained territories in the Straits, Thessalonica, the Strymon valley and Black‑Sea ports [PDF p. 362‑363]. After Mohammed unified the Ottoman remnants in 1421, Manuel’s gains vanished and he died in 1425, the last emperor to receive a “transient smile from fortune” [PDF p. 364‑365].

John VI inherited the throne, avoided conflict, and recognised that the empire required a bailiff rather than a statesman; he sought Western aid by converting to Roman Catholicism, traveling to Italy in 1438, attending the Councils of Ferrara and Florence, and being received into the Roman Church in 1439, but obtained only money and three hundred mercenaries from Pope Eugenius IV, a result the author presents as a poor return for such efforts [PDF p. 366‑367].
