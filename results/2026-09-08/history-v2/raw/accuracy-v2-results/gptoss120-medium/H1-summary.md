The author opens by declaring that Constantine’s decision to build a “great fortress‑capital” on the Balkan Peninsula proved “prescient” when the Huns entered Europe (author’s evaluation) [PDF p. 61]. Around A.D. 372 the Huns, described by a Gothic historian as “men with faces that can hardly be called faces…little in stature, but lithe and active, skilful in riding” [PDF p. 61], first devastated the Alans, then crushed the Ostrogoths, forcing the survivors toward the Danube delta; they then overran the Visigoths, who fled with families to the riverbank and begged the emperor to let them cross [PDF p. 61‑62]. In A.D. 376 the empire was split, Valens—whom the author labels “stupid, cowardly, and avaricious” [PDF p. 62]—was petitioned by the Goths. Valens allowed their crossing on the condition they surrender arms and give hostages; after they entered Moesia the governor Lupicinus hoarded food and sold it at “exorbitant prices,” driving the Goths to desperation and prompting a violent clash at Marcianopolis [PDF p. 63‑64]. A year of fighting culminated in the Battle of Adrianople (378 A.D.), where Gothic cavalry broke Roman lines, killing the emperor and most commanders; the loss was compared to Cannae by Ammianus Marcellinus [PDF p. 66‑67]. The victorious Goths briefly threatened Constantinople, but, “their courage failed them” when they saw the city’s walls and withdrew to Thrace [PDF p. 67].
