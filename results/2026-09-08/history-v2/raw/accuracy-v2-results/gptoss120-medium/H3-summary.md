The author claims that the eighty years after Basil the Macedonian’s death were uneventful in imperial history, dominated by the reigns of Leo VI the Wise and his grandson Constantine VII, whom he describes as “mild, strange issue” devoted to literature rather than warfare [PDF p. 241]. He argues that, although the period offered military opportunities, the emperors stayed “quiet, unenterprising” in the palace and seldom took the field [PDF p. 242]. Leo’s twenty‑six‑year rule was marked only by a failed Bulgarian invasion and a Saracen raid that briefly captured Thessalonica in 904; the author notes the city fell by surprise and was retaken when the attack was delayed a few weeks [PDF p. 242]. After Leo, Constantine VII inherited the throne at seven and spent his minority under guardians, first his uncle Alexander and then the usurper Romanus Lecapenus, who kept him “in the background” [PDF p. 243]. When Romanus’ sons attempted to seize the throne, popular revolt restored Constantine, who ruled for twenty years and was described as “somewhat weak and ineffective,” yet not tyrannical [PDF p. 243‑244]. The author praises Leo’s literary output, especially his military manual describing themes, cavalry and fleet, and notes Constantine’s treatises on the themes, imperial administration and court ceremonies, calling him a compiler rather than an original thinker [PDF p. 244‑247]. Finally, the author concludes that the successive literary emperors signal the end of the “intellectual dearth” that plagued Byzantine culture from the sixth to eighth centuries, marking a renewal of literary activity by the ninth century [PDF p. 247].
