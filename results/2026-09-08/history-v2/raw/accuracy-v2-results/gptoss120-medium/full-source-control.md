**The first excerpt (PDF pp. 61‑66) describes how the Huns shattered the security of the Balkan Peninsula in the late fourth century.** Around A.D. 372 the “enormous Tartar horde…burst into the lands north of the Euxine,” first almost exterminating the Alans and then crushing the Ostrogoths, who were forced into vassalage (p. 61). The Visigoths fled to the Danube, begged the emperor for passage, and were described by a contemporary writer as a “multitude…stretching out their hands with loud lamentations” (p. 62). Emperor Valens, portrayed as “stupid, cowardly, and avaricious,” reluctantly allowed them to cross on the condition that they surrender weapons and give hostages (p. 63). Corruption by the governor Lupicinus, who hoarded grain and sold food at exorbitant prices, provoked famine, barter of slaves for bread, and finally a violent confrontation that sparked a year‑long war (p. 64). The conflict culminated at Adrianople in 378, where the Gothic cavalry’s flank attack broke the Roman lines, leading to “the most fearful defeat…since Cannae” and the death of the emperor (pp. 66‑67).

**The second excerpt (PDF pp. 169‑170) concentrates on the linguistic decline of Latin in the eastern Mediterranean.** The author notes that even a Greek‑born writer such as Ammianus Marcellinus wrote in Latin, yet by the seventh century “the Roman element was rapidly vanishing” (p. 169). The loss is linked to the political split after Constantine’s sons and the later disintegration of the western empire, which cut off the “Latin‑speaking West” from the east (p. 170). Barbarian invasions accelerated the shift: the Visigoths, Ostrogoths, and later Slavs devastated the Latin‑speaking Balkan provinces, reducing the proportion of Latin speakers from “a quarter…in A.D. 400” to “not a tenth” by A.D. 620 (p. 170). The passage stresses that only isolated enclaves—African cities and the Italian Exarchate—still preserved the language, while the rest of the Balkans had become “Slavonic principalities” (p. 170).

**The third excerpt (PDF pp. 241‑247) presents the “literary emperors” Leo VI and Constantine VII, emphasizing their cultural rather than military pursuits.** The author characterizes them as “easy‑going, inoffensive men of literature” who “wrote no annals with their sword” and instead produced useful works for Byzantine historiography (p. 241). Leo’s “Tactica” is highlighted for its detailed description of the army’s organization, cavalry themes, and logistical support, portraying a professional force “with none of that variety in arms…that was the bane of medieval armies” (pp. 244‑245). Constantine’s prolific output—treatises on the themes, administration, and court ceremonies—is described as “careful and methodical…gifted with the powers of a compiler” (p. 247). The passage argues that the presence of two literary emperors signals the end of the “intellectual dearth” that had plagued the empire from 600 to 750, noting a revival of Byzantine literature by the ninth century (p. 247).

**The final excerpt (PDF pp. 358‑367) follows the ultimate decline of the Byzantine state in the late‑medieval period.** After 1370 the empire “ceased to exercise its old function of ‘bulwark of Christendom’” (p. 358), becoming a vassal of the rising Ottoman power. John Paleologus’s long reign is recounted, including his son Andronicus’s rebellions and the succession of Manuel II, who briefly hoped to exploit the Ottoman civil war after 1402 (pp. 360‑362). Manuel’s opportunistic treaties with rival Turkish claimants allowed him to regain some territories, yet the Ottoman fragmentation soon ended, and Murad II’s siege of Constantinople in 1422 was repelled only by a “miraculous vision of the Virgin” (p. 365). The narrative concludes with the death of Manuel II, the poverty of Constantinople—its population reduced to “about a hundred thousand souls” living in “great poverty,” the dominance of Italian merchants, and an army of merely “four thousand mercenary troops” (p. 366). John VI’s brief reign is noted for the loss of Thessalonica to the Ottomans and his futile appeal to the West, illustrating the empire’s final “unredeemed gloom” (pp. 367).
