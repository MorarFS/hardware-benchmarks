The author opens by declaring that Constantine’s decision to build a “great fortress‑capital” on the Balkan Peninsula proved “prescient” when the Huns entered Europe (author’s evaluation) [PDF p. 61]. According to a Gothic historian, the Huns were “men with faces that can hardly be called faces…little in stature, but lithe and active, skilful in riding” [PDF p. 61]; they first devastated the Alans, then crushed the Ostrogoths, forcing the survivors toward the Danube delta, and finally overran the Visigoths, who fled with families to the riverbank and begged the emperor to let them cross [PDF p. 61‑62]. In A.D. 376 the empire was split, and Valens—whom the author labels “stupid, cowardly, and avaricious”—was petitioned by the Goths. Valens allowed their crossing on the condition that they surrender arms and give hostages; after they entered Moesia the governor Lupicinus hoarded food and sold it at “exorbitant prices,” driving the Goths to desperation and prompting a violent clash at Marcianopolis [PDF p. 63‑64]. A year of fighting culminated in the Battle of Adrianople (378 A.D.), where Gothic cavalry broke Roman lines, killing the emperor and most commanders; the loss was compared to Cannae by Ammianus Marcellinus [PDF p. 66‑67]. The victorious Goths briefly threatened Constantinople, but, “their courage failed them” when they saw the city’s walls and withdrew to Thrace [PDF p. 67].

In the next section the author notes that the decay of Latin was already evident in the eastern Mediterranean: Ammianus Marcellinus, although born in Greece, wrote in the language of the ruling race, and a large Latin‑speaking population existed on the Balkan peninsula and along the Thracian coast, “except Greece proper, Macedonia, and a scattered line of cities” [PDF p. 169]. By the seventh century this Roman element was rapidly vanishing; already in the sixth century a knowledge of Latin was unusual even among educated men, as shown by Johannes Lydus’s claim that his civil‑service advancement depended on this rare skill and by Procopius’s ignorance of basic Latin [PDF p. 169]. The author evaluates Justinian as the last emperor whose mother tongue was Latin, while his successors were better skilled in Greek [PDF p. 169]. The gradual disuse of Latin, the author explains, resulted from the practical solution of continuity between Rome and the East after the empire’s division and Odoacer’s coronation in 476, which cut the Eastern provinces off from the Latin‑speaking West and exposed them to Greek dominance [PDF p. 170]; Gothic and later Slavic invasions reduced the Latin‑speaking populace from about a quarter of the provincials east of the Adriatic in 400 to less than a tenth by 620, leaving only Dalmatian ports, Africa and the Italian Exarchate as notable Latin enclaves, and the last native‑Latin author was the African poet Flavius Corippus [PDF p. 170]. Turning to religion, the author reports that under Constantine the governmental machinery remained essentially pagan, with many high officials still pagans, and that the Christian spirit did not fully penetrate the material sphere until forty years after Constantine’s death [PDF p. 171]. Theodosius I’s moral crusade, including the spectacular death of the monk Telemachus in 404, ended gladiatorial games in Constantinople [PDF p. 171]; Christian influence also abolished infanticide (Constantine’s state responsibility, Valentinian I’s capital punishment) and profoundly altered slavery: the Church taught slaves were souls equal to masters, and Justinian’s legislation permitted intermarriage, declared children of mixed unions free, and punished the prostitution of slaves [PDF p. 173‑174]. The author judges these reforms as stemming from the Christian belief in the individual soul, yet warns that ascetic monasticism became “positively dangerous” because thousands of men withdrew from civic duties, weakening the empire’s ability to resist barbarian invasions; only Cassiodorus later gave monks a productive role by ordering manuscript copying [PDF p. 174‑175]. He concludes that paganism had practically disappeared by the end of the fifth century, that Justinian closed the Athenian schools in 529, and that many sixth‑century elites were nominal Christians who remained indifferent to the faith, citing Procopius as an example [PDF p. 176].

The author claims that the eighty years after Basil the Macedonian’s death were uneventful in imperial history, dominated by the reigns of Leo VI the Wise and his grandson Constantine VII, whom he describes as “mild, strange issue” devoted to literature rather than warfare [PDF p. 241]. He argues that, although the period offered military opportunities, the emperors stayed “quiet, unenterprising” in the palace and seldom took the field [PDF p. 242]. Leo’s twenty‑six‑year rule was marked only by a failed Bulgarian invasion and a Saracen raid that briefly captured Thessalonica in 904; the author notes the city fell by surprise and was retaken when the attack was delayed a few weeks [PDF p. 242]. After Leo, Constantine VII inherited the throne at seven and spent his minority under guardians, first his uncle Alexander and then the usurper Romanus Lecapenus, who kept him “in the background” [PDF p. 243]. When Romanus’ sons attempted to seize the throne, popular revolt restored Constantine, who ruled for twenty years and was described as “somewhat weak and ineffective,” yet not tyrannical [PDF p. 243‑244]. The author praises Leo’s literary output, especially his military manual describing themes, cavalry and fleet, and notes Constantine’s treatises on the themes, imperial administration and court ceremonies, calling him a compiler rather than an original thinker [PDF p. 244‑247]. Finally, the author concludes that the successive literary emperors signal the end of the “intellectual dearth” that plagued Byzantine culture from the sixth to eighth centuries, marking a renewal of literary activity by the ninth century [PDF p. 247].

In the final excerpt the author states that the last seventy‑five years of the Byzantine Empire (1370‑1453) are a piece of local history and no longer form an important thread in the history of Christendom [PDF p. 358]. He evaluates that a Turkish capture of Constantinople in 1370 would not have greatly altered Eastern‑European events because the empire had already ceased to serve as the “bulwark of Christendom” and that duty fell to the Serbians and Hungarians for the next one‑hundred‑and‑fifty years [PDF p. 358]; the author further judges that the Paleologi, by their subservience to the Turks, extended the empire’s life after its justification had disappeared [PDF p. 358]. John Paleologus’s long reign is described as troubled by his son Andronicus, who rebelled twice, briefly seized the throne, allied with a son of Murad I, and was eventually blinded in an ineffectual operation, after which he rebeled again [PDF p. 359]. On John’s death in 1391 the throne passed to his second son Manuel II, whose limited resources left him unable to challenge Ottoman power except during the crisis of 1402 when Timur’s invasion defeated Sultan Bayezid and threw the Ottoman realm into civil war [PDF p. 360‑361]; Manuel sold aid to the pretender Suleiman for a heavy price and, while the Ottoman princes fought each other, temporarily regained territories in the Straits, Thessalonica, the Strymon valley and Black‑Sea ports [PDF p. 362‑363]. After Mohammed unified the Ottoman remnants in 1421, Manuel’s gains vanished and he died in 1425, the last emperor to receive a “transient smile from fortune” [PDF p. 364‑365]. John VI inherited the throne, avoided conflict, and recognised that the empire required a bailiff rather than a statesman; he sought Western aid by converting to Roman Catholicism, traveling to Italy in 1438, attending the Councils of Ferrara and Florence, and being received into the Roman Church in 1439, but obtained only money and three hundred mercenaries from Pope Eugenius IV, a result the author presents as a poor return for such efforts [PDF p. 366‑367].
