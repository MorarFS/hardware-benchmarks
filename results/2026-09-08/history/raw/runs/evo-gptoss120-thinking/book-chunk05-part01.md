The narrative opens with the disastrous aftermath of Justinian II’s Saracen campaign, after which the emperor began imprisoning officers and decimating his troops, a situation the author likens to the peril of serving under Robespierre [PDF p. 201]. In 695 the cup of Justinian’s iniquities overflowed, and the officer Leontius received the command of the theme of Hellas, fearing imminent execution [PDF p. 201]. A monk named Paul urged him to strike against Justinian, promising popular and military support [PDF p. 201]; this counsel is presented as direct testimony. Leontius obeyed, freed hundreds of political prisoners, seized the Cathedral of St Sophia, and captured Justinian with his ministers, ordering the emperor’s nose to be slit and his exile to Cherson [PDF p. 201]. The mob executed Theodore and Stephanus by burning [PDF p. 201].  

The author characterizes the subsequent twenty‑year period as “the first anarchy,” noting the new emperor’s lack of ability, his three‑year reign, and relentless revolts at home and defeats abroad [PDF p. 202]. The Asiatic frontier suffered raids by Abdalmalik, while a Saracen army from Egypt seized Carthage in 697, overturning Belisarius’s earlier restoration [PDF p. 202].  

Leontius’s downfall is described on the next page: officers fleeing Carthage plotted with Tiberius Apsimarus, who commanded the Aegean fleet, and invited him to claim the throne [PDF p. 203]. Their forces opened Constantinople’s gates, captured Leontius, slit his nose, and confined him to a monastery [PDF p. 203]. Tiberius III, though initially successful against the Saracens and in northern Syria, soon lost his throne to the same mob‑driven instability that doomed his predecessors [PDF p. 203].  

Justinian’s exile to Cherson is recounted, including his daring escape to the Khazar Khan, marriage to the Khan’s sister, and the bribery attempt by Tiberius III to recover him [PDF p. 204]. Justinian’s violent retaliation against the bribing Khazar chiefs, his escape in a fishing boat, and his storm‑laden voyage to the Bulgarians are narrated in detail [PDF p. 204‑205]. The emperor returned to Constantinople with a Bulgarian army, entered the city at night, and retook the throne without battle, the author noting that the populace had largely forgotten his prior tyranny [PDF p. 205].  

The author then argues that Justinian’s revenge involved mutilating his rivals—Leontius and Tiberius—displaying their bodies as footstools before beheading them, followed by a reign of terror marked by executions, eye‑mutilations, drowning of citizens, and sack of Cherson [PDF p. 206]. This brutality, the author claims, made Justinian “comparatively popular” only in his first reign [PDF p. 206].  

The narrative shifts to the six‑year chaotic interregnum after Justinian’s death, describing Philippicus’s brief rule, the rise of Anastasius, and the accession of Theodosius III, whose ineffective governance allowed continued Saracen and Bulgarian incursions [PDF p. 207‑208].  

Leo the Isaurian emerges as a capable general of the Anatolic theme, repelling the Saracen siege of 717–718 by breaking the blockade with fire‑ships, exploiting harsh winter conditions, and defeating Moslemah’s army, an event the author portrays as decisive for Christendom’s survival [PDF p. 211‑214]. Leo’s accession in spring 717 is presented as a response to the assembly’s request for a strong ruler [PDF p. 209‑210].  

Turning to the Iconoclast controversy, the author describes a cultural decline in the seventh‑century empire, emphasizing superstition, relic worship, and the degradation of Roman jurisprudence [PDF p. 216‑218]. Leo’s 725 decree to remove images incited riots; subsequent imperial executions and the forced removal of icons are detailed, as are the resistance of monks and accusations of collusion with Jews and the Caliph [PDF p. 219‑222].  

Later emperors continued the dispute: Constantine V pursued harsher measures, including mutilations, while Michael II adopted a moderate policy, restoring exiled monks but refusing full restoration of icons [PDF p. 222‑233]. Theophilus intensified persecutions, prompting Saracen retaliation that culminated in the sack of Amorium, after which his death in 842 led to Empress Theodora’s rapid restoration of icons and a compromise limiting future image worship to paintings and mosaics [PDF p. 235‑238].  

The final pages reflect on artistic decline from the sixth to eighth centuries, noting rare surviving mosaics and the revival of classical painting in the ninth‑century, a revival spurred by Iconoclast persecution [PDF p. 248‑250].  

The source provides a richly detailed, author‑driven interpretation of a tumultuous era; however, it offers limited corroboration for many specific anecdotes, and the reliance on dramatic testimonies (e.g., Leontius’s prophecy) leaves several events ambiguously sourced.
