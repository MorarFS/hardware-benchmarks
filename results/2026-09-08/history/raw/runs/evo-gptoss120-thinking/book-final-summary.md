The author opens by condemning Gibbon’s negative view of Byzantium and praising Finlay and Bury’s revisions, declaring his purpose to follow their spirit rather than Gibbon’s skepticism [PDF p. 13‑14]. The narrative begins with the legendary founding of Byzantium by a Megarian fleet in the late seventh‑century BC, when settlers erected a stockade across the headland and called the colony Axeinos, “inhospitable” [PDF p. 27‑28]. The name later changed to Euxeinos, “hospitable,” as the harbour expanded and trade increased [PDF p. 29]. Its position between Europe and Asia created a valuable military frontier and commercial hub [PDF p. 30‑31]. Byzantium then enjoyed three centuries of relative independence before a brief thirty‑year Persian domination [PDF p. 31‑32]. Darius’s pontoon bridge across the Bosphorus and the Ionic Revolt are recorded as key events, and the city’s leaders extracted tolls from passing ships, provoking local resentment [PDF p. 32]. The city minted iron coins, a practice shared only with Sparta [PDF p. 32]. The author depicts Byzantine luxury as indulgent, noting feasting on tunny fish, yet cautions that such excesses may reflect jealous neighbor testimonies rather than fact [PDF p. 33]. Philip II of Macedon captured the city for the fifth time in 339 BC; a sudden light, interpreted as divine aid, repelled the assault and inspired a later crescent‑star emblem [PDF p. 34]. Byzantium later allied with Rome, receiving the status civitas libera et foederata before Vespasian stripped its privileges, reducing it to a provincial town [PDF p. 34‑35]. The city endured repeated sackings by Maximinus Daza in AD 314 and Gallienus in AD 263, yet recovered swiftly each time [PDF p. 36‑37]. Constantine decided in AD 328‑330 to refound Byzantium as Constantinople, citing geography, defense, and a symbolic break with Rome, and contrasted alternative sites such as Naissus, Sofia, and Nicomedia, arguing Byzantium offered the optimal harbour and fortifications [PDF p. 39‑44]. He ceremonially laid out the city’s boundaries, erected the Milion milestone flanked by statues of himself and Helena, and cleared a 150‑acre site for an imperial palace, creating the marble‑paved Augustaeum as the civic heart [PDF p. 44‑48]. The Hippodrome, surrounded by the Senate House, Baths of Zeuxippus, and other structures, became central to public life, and the author interprets it as a barometer of civic tension and a unifying imperial space [PDF p. 49‑50]. He notes limited source material for early events, acknowledging bias in later chroniclers [PDF p. 32‑33]. Surviving central monuments of the Hippodrome now stand in the Atmeidan, preserving three spina dedications, while the Milion marked the empire’s heart and later bore statues, including a repurposed porphyry column [PDF p. 51]. North of the Hippodrome, Constantine erected a basilica‑shaped church dedicated to Divine Wisdom, the first Hagia Sophia, which later burned, and a wooden gallery linked the palace’s Royal Gate to the square, allowing imperial avoidance of the market street [PDF p. 53]. The palace, Hippodrome, and cathedral formed Constantinople’s core, while peripheral structures such as the Church of the Holy Apostles, granaries, the Golden Gate, and a bronze equestrian statue of Constantine dominated the urban landscape [PDF p. 54‑55]. On 11 May 330, Constantine celebrated the dedication festival, with Christian bishops blessing the unfinished palace and holding the first service in Hagia Sophia, although the emperor remained unbaptized; pagan temples were spared as artworks, and statues of gods in the Baths and Senate House remained decorative [PDF p. 55]. To populate the capital, Constantine granted land, Senate seats, and free grain from Egypt, while African corn was reserved for older Rome [PDF p. 56]. After Constantine’s death in 337, rapid murders and civil wars concentrated power under Constantius II, who nevertheless secured a brief internal peace despite barbarian pressures on the Rhine and Euphrates [PDF p. 57‑58]. The Goths, long raiders of the Balkan provinces, were forced across the Danube after a war from 328‑332 that secured hostages and peace terms for Constantine [PDF p. 59]; by the mid‑fourth century many settled as farmers along the Theiss and Pruth rivers, trading with Moesian towns and adopting Christianity through Ulfilas’s translation of the Bible [PDF p. 60]. Around 372, the Huns swept from the Don and Volga, annihilating the Alans and crushing the Ostrogoths, who became Hunnic vassals; the Visigoths then fled to the Danube seeking imperial refuge [PDF p. 61‑62]. In 376, the Visigoths begged Valens to cross the Danube; he allowed passage on condition of hostages and disarmament, yet officials accepted bribes and failed to supply promised grain, leading to starvation [PDF p. 63‑64]. A banquet at Marcianopolis turned violent when Lupicinus ordered the murder of the Gothic leader Fritigern, sparking open war [PDF p. 64]. After a year of skirmishes, the Goths defeated Valens at Adrianople in 378, annihilating the Roman army and killing the emperor, an event compared to Cannae [PDF p. 66‑67]; they briefly besieged Constantinople’s Golden Gate but withdrew after confronting the city’s formidable walls [PDF p. 68]. Theodosius succeeded Valens, restoring order in the Balkans and securing peace with the Goths through land grants and military commissions, eventually fielding forty‑thousand Gothic cavalry in his service [PDF p. 69‑70]. After Theodosius’s death in 408, his son Theodosius II ruled under the regency of his sister Pulcheria and praetorian prefect Anthemius, who rebuilt the Danube fleet, reorganized corn supplies, and revived the north‑west Balkan region; Pulcheria governed as a chaste Augusta for thirty‑six years, establishing a virtuous court [PDF p. 70‑72]. Successors Leo I, Zeno, and Anastasius maintained Eastern stability, contrasting with the West’s rapid turnover of puppet emperors manipulated by Germanic generals; Leo I introduced Isaurian troops, and Zeno later pacified the Ostrogoths by offering them the Italian throne, leading to Theodoric’s conquest of Italy and the eventual withdrawal of Germanic forces from the Balkans [PDF p. 75‑78]. Justinian, nephew of Justin I, ascended in 527 after a brief reign by his uncle, marrying former actress Theodora and facing opposition from his mother and the emperor‑designate; contemporary sources describe Theodora as extraordinarily beautiful but morally suspect, her influence growing to rival Justinian’s while she promoted charitable institutions for fallen women [PDF p. 79‑84]. Justinian’s reign combined ambitious building, codification of law, and military campaigns, yet his financial policies relied on heavy taxation and extortion, weakening the empire’s fiscal health for future generations [PDF p. 95‑99]. His foreign policy began with a four‑year war against Persian king Kobad over frontier fortresses; the conflict ended indecisively after a Roman victory at Dara in 530, introducing the talented general Belisarius [PDF p. 100]. The author notes that many early details derive from later chroniclers and may reflect bias [PDF p. 32‑33]. The Persian war concluded in 532, while another threat rose and fell the same year: the Nika riots, in which the “Blues” and “Greens” factions became powerful political parties supporting different emperors and theological positions [PDF p. 101]. In January 532, Justinian ordered execution of both factions’ leaders; seven rioters were executed, four beheaded at St Conon’s monastery, and three hanged, though the rope failed, prompting the mob to rescue the half‑hung men and place them in a nearby monastery [PDF p. 102]. The mob demanded removal of finance minister John of Cappadocia and prefect Eudemius, whom Justinian promised to dismiss, and called for his deposition and for Hypatius, Anastasius’s nephew, to become Caesar while most troops were away fighting Persia [PDF p. 102]. Justinian could rely only on four thousand Imperial Guard, a few German auxiliaries, and five hundred cataphracts under Belisarius, who commanded the whole force [PDF p. 103]. The main battle unfolded in the Augustaeum between the palace and the Hippodrome, where rebels set fire to the Senate’s Brazen Porch, spreading to St Sophia, destroying the cathedral on the third day, and also consuming the hospital of Sampson and St Irene’s church [PDF p. 103]. While the blaze halted fighting, insurgents controlled most of the city but could not locate Hypatius, who had taken refuge with the emperor; Justinian eventually drove Hypatius out, fearing his presence, and the rebels later seized him, placing him on the royal Kathisma seat and crowning him with his wife’s gold chain [PDF p. 103]. Empress Theodora, refusing to flee, urged Justinian to attack the rebels, a speech recorded by Procopius; prompted by her words, Justinian ordered a final assault, and Belisarius attacked all three Hippodrome gates, routing the rebels after side entrances fell despite resistance at the Kathisma gate [PDF p. 106]. Approximately thirty‑five thousand men died during the six‑day Nika sedition, yet the Blues and Greens continued rioting for fifty more years without again threatening imperial rule [PDF p. 106]. After the Persian truce and Nika suppression, Justinian pursued his long‑delayed western reconquest, exploiting the weakness of the Germanic kingdoms of Italy and Africa six years after his accession; in Africa, King Hilderic had been overthrown by his cousin Gelimer, and in Italy, Theodoric’s grandson Athalaric died, leaving the kingdom to his mother Amalasuntha, whose marriage to Theodahad destabilized Ostrogothic rule [PDF p. 108]. Justinian declared war on Gelimer in 533, using the unlawful deposition of Hilderic as pretext, and sent Belisarius with ten thousand foot soldiers and five thousand horsemen to Tripoli, which quickly surrendered; Belisarius advanced unopposed, captured Carthage after a hard fight, and ended the Vandal kingdom after 104 years of rule, treating Gelimer kindly and returning him to Constantinople with Vandal treasures [PDF p. 110‑112]. In 535, Belisarius invaded Sicily, captured Palermo after a brief siege, and took the whole island within six months; Theodahad’s incompetence led the Goths to kill him, and Witiges succeeded but could not stop Belisarius, who seized Naples and then Rome without resistance; the Gothic siege of Rome in 537 lasted over a year, yet the Goths failed to breach the walls and the imperial garrison held out [PDF p. 115‑116]. On May 540, the starving Gothic army abandoned Ravenna; Belisarius entered the city, ending the Gothic war, but a new Persian conflict erupted when Chosroes I invaded Antioch in 540, sacking the city after a brief battle; Justinian redirected troops east, appointing Belisarius commander, and after two years of inconclusive fighting a peace treaty in 545 restored limited territories to Persia [PDF p. 125‑126]. In AD 542, a devastating plague struck the empire, killing up to five thousand people daily in Constantinople and halting normal life; the disease spared no class, yet seemed to avoid the most wicked, according to chroniclers, and Justinian, though he survived, never fully recovered his former vigor [PDF p. 127‑128]. In later years he devoted increasing attention to theological disputes, especially Monophysitism, seeking compromise through edicts while forcing Pope Vigilius to sign his demands in AD 554, alienating the Western Church and deepening East‑West estrangement [PDF p. 129]; the death of Empress Theodora in AD 548 removed a bold political partner, leaving Justinian increasingly distrustful and isolated, and even Belisarius was repeatedly deprived of troops, recalled only during emergencies such as the Hunnic incursion of 556, which he repelled with a small, undisciplined force [PDF p. 130‑131]. The author notes that Justinian’s final military success was the defeat of the Huns, after which he died in AD 565 [PDF p. 131].
