The empire’s maritime commerce had been devastated by Saracen piracy during the preceding century, leaving Byzantine naval power as the sole conduit for Eastern goods to Italy and France, which passed through Bosphorus warehouses and endured a harsh yet ineffective tax system【PDF p. 251】. Under Constantine Porphyrogennetos, the eastern frontier faced a succession of formidable foes—first the Sassanian Persians, then the Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates—until the Abbasid state collapsed in 951 A.D., when the Buhawid prince Imad‑ud‑din seized Baghdad and reduced the caliphs to powerless captives【PDF p. 252】. The new Muslim polities, including the emirs of Aleppo and Mosul, were weak and mutually hostile, creating a strategic opening for the empire to restore its borders beyond the Taurus line【PDF p. 253】.

Nicephorus Phocas, a scion of a great Cappadocian land‑owning family and a noted military writer, was chosen by Romanus II to lead a massive expedition against the pirate haven of Crete. In 961 A.D. his fleet, numbered by the thousand, captured Chandax, seized a century’s worth of loot, and returned the island to Byzantine control【PDF p. 254】. Emboldened, Phocas advanced into Cilicia, stormed Anazarbus, forced the Amanus Pass, and laid siege to Aleppo; after a costly stalemate he withdrew with booty and sixty captured forts in 962 A.D.【PDF p. 255】. The unexpected death of Romanus II that same year left his young sons Basil and Constantine under regency; Phocas claimed their guardianship, married the dowager empress Theophano, and ruled jointly from 963 to 969, maintaining a reputation for loyalty to the imperial heirs【PDF p. 256】.

During 964‑965 Phocas seized the Cilician strongholds of Adana, Mopsuestia and Tarsus, sent their bronze gates to Constantinople, and reclaimed Cyprus after seventy‑seven years of Saracen rule【PDF p. 257】. His domestic policies, however, provoked hostility: clashes with Patriarch Polyeuctus, suppression of public spectacles, and the redirection of state revenues to the war budget earned him the popular epithets “niggard” and “extortioner”【PDF p. 258】. In a winter siege of Antioch he ignored orders, stormed a tower, and subsequently dismissed both the disobedient commander Burtzes and the overly obedient general Peter【PDF p. 259】. Phocas’s reign ended abruptly when Theophano conspired with her lover John Zimisces; on a December night in 969 the emperor was murdered by a dozen assassins, a deed that allowed Zimisces to seize the throne【PDF p. 260】.

John Zimisces, a former cavalry officer, repelled two Russian incursions—first in 907, then a larger raid in 941—by destroying their light boats at sea【PDF p. 261】. In 970 he confronted a massive Russian army of sixty thousand under King Swietoslav, defeating it at Silistria; the defeated sultan swore never to molest the empire again【PDF p. 263】. After Zimisces’s death, the Macedonian line ended with Basil II (976‑1025), whose reign was dominated by continuous warfare, notably the thirty‑four‑year Bulgarian wars that culminated in the 1014 victory at Kleidion and the near‑annihilation of the Bulgarian elite【PDF p. 267】.

The Byzantine fortunes waned after Basil’s death. Alexios Comnenus, who ascended in 1081, faced the dual threats of Seljuk incursions in Asia Minor and Norman ambitions in the west. He suffered defeat at Durazzo in 1081 but later repelled the Normans at Larissa in 1083【PDF p. 286】. The First Crusade (1095‑1099) offered temporary relief: Alexios reclaimed much of western Asia Minor after the Crusaders captured Nicaea, yet the crusading armies pillaged Byzantine territories and established independent principalities, limiting Alexios’s long‑term gains【PDF p. 291】. The crusading traffic diverted commercial activity from Constantinople to Italian ports, causing a severe decline in imperial revenue—perhaps a loss of one‑third to one‑half of trade over the next fifty years【PDF p. 294】. Alexios’s generous concessions to Venice and Genoa, exchanged for military support, further eroded the empire’s fiscal base【PDF p. 295】.

The narrative concludes that the empire’s deterioration was accelerated by successive rulers who either mismanaged finances, pursued reckless wars, or granted excessive privileges to foreign merchants, leaving the state vulnerable to internal unrest and external conquest【PDF p. 300】. The source provides a detailed chronological account but offers limited quantitative data on trade volumes or army sizes, and the author’s moral judgments about individual emperors are interwoven with factual description, requiring careful separation when using this material for further analysis.
