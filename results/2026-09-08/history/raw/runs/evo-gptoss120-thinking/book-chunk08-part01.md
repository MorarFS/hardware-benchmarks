The Janissary corps, first formed in the fourteenth‑sixteenth centuries, originated as “new soldiery” and entered European service under Orkhan’s later reign [PDF p. 351]. After Andronicus III died in 1341, his nine‑year‑old son John III inherited a weakened empire, exposing it to civil strife [PDF p. 351]. The author portrays John Cantacuzenus, prime minister of the deceased emperor, as a clever but unsteady courtier who aspired to imitate Michael VIII’s rise [PDF p. 351]. Cantacuzenus bribed officials, built a faction, and prepared a coup, yet procrastination delayed his actions [PDF p. 351]. Empress‑dowager Anne of Savoy eventually raised opposition, forcing Cantacuzenus to proclaim himself emperor while he could only muster an army at the city walls [PDF p. 351‑352].

Unable to capture Constantinople, Cantacuzenus resorted to foreign aid, summoning Stephen Dushan of Servia and Turkish princes Amour and Orkhan—an unprecedented Byzantine appeal to external powers [PDF p. 352‑353]. Dushan entered Macedonia and Thrace, capturing all territory except Thessalonica and a few towns, and compelled the despot of Epirus to pay homage [PDF p. 353‑354]. The Byzantine government retained merely the capital, Adrianople, and Thessalonica; most of the Balkans passed permanently to Servian control, suggesting a looming Servian empire in the peninsula [PDF p. 354]. Dushan’s death in 1355 and the swift assassination of his son Urosh fragmented the Servian realm, allowing numerous princes to scramble for its remnants [PDF p. 354].

Cantacuzenus leaned heavily on Turkish cavalry, permitting them to ravage the countryside and sell captives at Smyrna and Broussa [PDF p. 354‑355]. His “depth of degradation” culminated in giving his daughter Theodora to Orkhan as a harem member [PDF p. 355]. After six years of war, Empress Anne recognized Cantacuzenus as co‑ruler and guardian of the rightful heir in 1347, leaving a dilapidated realm limited to Constantinople, Adrianople, Thessalonica, and the Peloponnese [PDF p. 355‑356]. The author ranks Cantacuzenus among the empire’s great destroyers, alongside Isaac and Alexios Angelos [PDF p. 356].

For seven more years Cantacuzenus shared power with John Palaiologos, waging an unsuccessful war against Servia in hopes of regaining Dushan’s conquests [PDF p. 355‑356]. In 1354 the twenty‑four‑year‑old emperor John V asserted himself, prompting Cantacuzenus to summon Orkhan’s son‑in‑law forces, who crossed into Thrace and expelled Palaiologan garrisons [PDF p. 356]. A night surprise allowed John Palaiologos to seize Constantinople and capture Cantacuzenus, who was tonsured, spared blindness, and spent his remaining years composing a personal history [PDF p. 356‑357].

Turkish auxiliaries, however, remained; Suleiman, Orkhan’s son, seized Gallipoli, populated it with Turkish families, and established the first Ottoman foothold in Europe, though initially isolated [PDF p. 357‑358]. Orkhan died in 1359; his successor Murad I crossed into Europe, decisively defeating the Byzantines at Adrianople in 1361 and reducing the empire to a “head without a body” [PDF p. 358‑359]. Murad refrained from besieging Constantinople, whose walls and Genoese‑Venetian naval protection remained formidable, and instead focused on campaigns against Servians, Bulgarians, and Seljuk emirs [PDF p. 359‑360].

John V, lacking resources, could not reverse Ottoman advances; his attempts to solicit papal aid proved futile [PDF p. 360‑361]. Manuel II, succeeding in 1391, inherited a realm confined to Constantinople, Thessalonica, and the Peloponnese, and recognized the empire’s inability to act as Christendom’s bulwark after 1370 [PDF p. 361‑362]. The author speculates that an earlier fall in 1370 would have modestly altered Genoese‑Venetian trade and delayed the Renaissance, yet overall European history would have remained largely unchanged [PDF p. 362‑363].

The narrative concludes that the Ottoman empire, under Murad II and later Mehmed II, finally captured Constantinople in 1453, ending a centuries‑long decline [PDF p. 363‑367]. The author notes that many details—particularly Ottoman motives and Byzantine internal politics—remain uncertain, leaving questions about the precise causes of the empire’s final collapse [PDF p. 368‑370].
