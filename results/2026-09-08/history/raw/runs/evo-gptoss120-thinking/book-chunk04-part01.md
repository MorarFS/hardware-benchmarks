The Latin‑speaking Thraco‑Illyrian population, proud of its native tongue, suffered devastating invasions by Slavs and Avars; the Goths who had preceded them were at least Christian and semi‑civilized【PDF p.151】.  Between 570 and 600 the older inhabitants of northern Balkan lands were almost exterminated, leaving only Dalmatian islanders and scattered “Vlachs” among the new Slav settlers【PDF p.151】.  Contemporary chronicler John of Ephesus records that in 581 the “accursed people called Slavonians” overran Greece, Thessalonica and all Thrace, capturing cities, burning forts and enslaving the population【PDF p.151‑152】.  The open countryside was swept bare, while fortified towns resisted because neither Slav nor Avar excelled in siege warfare【PDF p.152】.  General Priscus, appointed by Maurice, held the Danube line, crossed the river and burned enemy villages, yet could not protect unarmed civilians; the Danube fortresses soon guarded only a desolate region populated sparsely by Slavs【PDF p.152】.  

Maurice’s unpopularity grew as he failed to pay a ransom demanded by the Avar chagan for 15 000 prisoners; the chagan massacred the captives, further eroding Maurice’s standing【PDF p.152‑153】.  In 602 his order that the Danube army winter north of the river was refused, the troops elected the centurion Phocas as commander and marched on Constantinople【PDF p.153】.  Maurice fled to the Asiatic provinces but was captured at Chalcedon and executed with his five sons【PDF p.153】.  Phocas, described by the author as a brutal, ignorant soldier, initiated a reign of cruelty, including the execution of Constantina, widow of Maurice, and her three daughters, and the burning alive of the able general Narses after he had come under safe conduct【PDF p.154‑155】.  

During Phocas’s rule the Persian king Chosroes launched a war that penetrated north‑Syria, reached the sea, turned north into Asia Minor and, in 614, reached the gates of Chalcedon【PDF p.155‑156】.  Although Phocas retained Constantinople, he was eventually deposed by Heraclius, the exarch of Africa, whose fleet arrived at the Dardanelles and whose forces entered the capital after Phocas’s palace guards mutinied【PDF p.156‑157】.  Heraclius was crowned on 5 October 610 and inherited an empire with an empty treasury, a depleted army and provinces overrun by Persians, Avars and Slavs【PDF p.158】.  

In 613 the Persian commander Shahrbarz seized Central Syria, captured Damascus and, in 614, Jerusalem, taking the “True Cross” to Persia; this loss sparked panic in the East, with many believing the fate of Christendom hung in the balance【PDF p.158‑159】.  The Church loaned its treasures to fund Heraclius’s war effort and the civic corn dole was abolished, allowing the treasury to finance a new army【PDF p.160】.  After a series of setbacks, including the 617 Persian capture of Chalcedon, Heraclius negotiated a temporary peace with the Avars and, in 622, finally marched from the Balkans to confront the Persians directly【PDF p.160‑161】.  

Heraclius framed his campaigns as a holy crusade; religious zeal motivated his troops as they fought to recover the “True Cross”【PDF p.161‑162】.  Between 622 and 627 he conducted six campaigns, the first clearing Asia Minor by landing in Cilicia and forcing the Persian army to withdraw from the Balkans【PDF p.162‑163】.  In 626 a combined Avar‑Persian siege of Constantinople was repelled; the Roman fleet sank Avar rafts and defeated the attackers, after which the Chagan retreated across the Danube【PDF p.163‑164】.  The 628 peace left both empires exhausted【PDF p.184】.  

Meanwhile, Muhammad sent invitations to Heraclius and Chosroes in 628; both gave only perfunctory replies, prompting the Prophet to deem them unworthy and foretelling mutual destruction【PDF p.185‑186】.  Arab raids began in 629, and by 634 they captured Bostra; a decisive battle at the Hieromax tributary in 634 allowed the Arabs to defeat the Byzantine field army and capture Damascus in 635【PDF p.188‑189】.  Heraclius could not halt the Arab advance; a revolt led by Baanes further destabilized the empire, and in 637 Jerusalem fell, after which Caliph Omar built a mosque on the Temple Mount while sparing Christian sanctuaries【PDF p.190‑191】.  Heraclius died in 641, his son Constantine failing at the 638 Syrian campaign and losing Egypt to the Arabs in 641【PDF p.191‑192】.  

Heraclius’s second marriage to his niece Martina produced a succession crisis: Constantine III died, Heracleonas and his half‑brother Constantine vied for the throne, and after violent intrigue Martina and her son were mutilated; Constans II ultimately ruled from 642 to 668, fighting Saracens and later Lombards, before being assassinated in 666【PDF p.191‑196】.  Constantine IV repelled a major Arab siege (674‑678) using “Greek‑fire,” securing a celebrated victory, yet the Bulgars settled in the Balkans in 679, establishing a new kingdom that the empire could not dislodge【PDF p.196‑198】.  

The author interprets the reorganization of the empire into “themes” as a pragmatic response to the Saracen threat, describing the military‑civilian fusion of authority that replaced earlier Roman structures【PDF p.193‑195】.  

**Source limitations.**  The narrative relies heavily on later chroniclers such as John of Ephesus and on the author’s own interpretive judgments; many events, especially the details of the sieges of Constantinople and Arab battles, lack contemporary documentation, leaving portions of the account uncertain.
