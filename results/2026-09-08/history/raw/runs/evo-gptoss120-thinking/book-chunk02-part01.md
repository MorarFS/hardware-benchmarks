The Hippodrome’s surviving central monuments stand in the open space the Turks call Atmeidan, preserving three dedications that once flanked the “spina” [PDF p. 51].  Along the eastern wall of the Hippodrome, near the Augustaeum, the Milion—Constantine’s central milestone—marked the empire’s heart, while later emperors added statues, including a porphyry column repurposed from a Hellenic Apollo into an imperial image [PDF p. 51].  North of the Hippodrome, Constantine erected an earlier, basilica‑shaped church dedicated to Divine Wisdom (the first Hagia Sophia), which burned in the fifth and sixth centuries, leaving no trace of its original form [PDF p. 53].  A wooden gallery from its western door crossed the square to the palace’s “Royal Gate,” allowing the emperor to avoid the Chalcoprateia market street [PDF p. 53].  The palace, Hippodrome, and cathedral formed Constantinople’s core, while peripheral structures such as the Church of the Holy Apostles, granaries, the Golden Gate, and a bronze equestrian statue of Constantine also dominated the urban landscape [PDF p. 54‑55].

On 11 May 330, Constantine celebrated the dedication festival of the new capital; Christian bishops blessed the partially finished palace and held the first service in Hagia Sophia, although the emperor remained unbaptized [PDF p. 55].  Pagan temples were spared as artworks rather than objects of worship, and statues of gods in the Baths and Senate House remained decorative [PDF p. 55].  To populate the city, Constantine invited Roman senators, wealthy Greeks and Asians, court officials, artisans, and merchants, granting them land, Senate seats, and free grain distributions drawn from Egypt, while African corn was reserved for older Rome [PDF p. 56].  After Constantine’s death in 337, his realm passed to his sons and nephews, but rapid murders and civil wars concentrated power under Constantius II, who nevertheless managed a brief period of internal peace despite barbarian pressures on the Rhine and Euphrates [PDF p. 57‑58].

The Goths, long raiders of the Balkan provinces, were forced across the Danube after a war from 328‑332 that secured hostages and peace terms for Constantine [PDF p. 59].  By the mid‑fourth century many Goths had settled as farmers along the Theiss and Pruth rivers, traded with Moesian towns, and adopted Christianity through Ulfilas’s translation of the Bible [PDF p. 60].  Around 372, the Huns swept from the Don and Volga, annihilating the Alans and crushing the Ostrogoths, who became Hunnic vassals; the Visigoths then fled to the Danube, seeking imperial refuge [PDF p. 61‑62].  In 376, the Visigoths begged Emperor Valens to cross the Danube; he allowed passage on the condition of hostages and disarmament, yet officials accepted bribes and failed to provide promised grain, leading to starvation and desperation [PDF p. 63‑64].  A banquet at Marcianopolis turned violent when Lupicinus ordered the murder of the Gothic leader Fritigern, sparking open war [PDF p. 64].  After a year of skirmishes, the Goths defeated Valens at Adrianople in 378, annihilating the Roman army and killing the emperor, an event compared to Cannae [PDF p. 66‑67].  The Goths briefly besieged Constantinople’s Golden Gate but withdrew after confronting the city’s formidable walls [PDF p. 68].

Theodosius succeeded Valens, restoring order in the Balkans and securing peace with the Goths through land grants and military commissions, eventually fielding forty thousand Gothic cavalry in his service [PDF p. 69‑70].  After Theodosius’s death in 408, his son Theodosius II, a child, ruled under the regency of his sister Pulcheria and the capable praetorian prefect Anthemius, who rebuilt the Danube fleet, reorganized corn supplies, and revived the north‑west Balkan region [PDF p. 70‑71].  Pulcheria, a chaste Augusta, governed as a nun‑empress for thirty‑six years, establishing a virtuous court [PDF p. 71‑72].  Successors Leo I, Zeno, and Anastasius maintained the Eastern Empire’s stability, contrasting with the West’s rapid turnover of puppet emperors manipulated by Germanic generals [PDF p. 73‑74].  Leo I introduced Isaurian troops; Zeno later pacified the Ostrogoths by offering them the Italian throne, leading to Theodoric’s conquest of Italy and the eventual withdrawal of Germanic forces from the Balkans [PDF p. 75‑78].

Justinian, nephew of the short‑lived emperor Justin I, ascended in 527 after a brief reign by his uncle.  He married the former actress Theodora, overturning a Senate law prohibiting such unions, and faced political opposition from his mother and the emperor‑designate [PDF p. 79‑81].  Contemporary sources describe Theodora as extraordinarily beautiful but morally suspect; her influence grew to rival Justinian’s, though she also promoted charitable institutions for fallen women [PDF p. 82‑84].  Justinian’s reign combined ambitious building, codification of law, and military campaigns, yet his financial policies relied on heavy taxation and extortion, weakening the empire’s fiscal health for future generations [PDF p. 95‑99].  His foreign policy began with a four‑year war against Persian king Kobad over frontier fortresses; the conflict ended indecisively after a Roman victory at Dara in 530, introducing the talented general Belisarius [PDF p. 100].

The source provides a detailed narrative of Constantinople’s foundation, early imperial monuments, the transformation from pagan to Christian city, the Gothic and Hunnic invasions, and the successive administrative reforms that preserved the Eastern Empire.  Gaps remain regarding precise dates of certain construction phases, the extent of Gothic settlement patterns, and the reliability of anecdotal episodes such as the apple scandal involving Empress Eudocia.  Further primary evidence would be needed to resolve these uncertainties.
