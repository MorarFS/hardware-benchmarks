The work opens with a preface in which the author denounces Edward Gibbon’s negative portrayal of Byzantium and praises recent scholarship by Finlay (1856) and Bury (1889) for restoring the empire’s reputation; he declares his aim to follow their spirit rather than Gibbon’s skepticism [PDF p. 13‑14].  

The narrative proper begins by recounting the legendary founding of Byzantium in the late 7th century BC, when a Megarian fleet established a modest settlement on the Golden Horn, building a stockade across the headland [PDF p. 27‑28]. The author notes that the colony’s name changed from Axeinos (“the inhospitable”) to Euxeinos (“the hospitable”) as its ports grew friendly [PDF p. 29]; the site’s strategic position between Europe and Asia made it an ideal military frontier and commercial hub [PDF p. 30‑31].  

Early autonomy is described: Byzantium survived three centuries of relative independence, briefly falling under Persian control for thirty years before regaining freedom [PDF p. 31‑32]. The author cites Darius’s pontoon bridge across the Bosphorus and the later Ionic Revolt, noting that the city’s leaders extracted tolls from passing ships, provoking local resentment [PDF p. 32]. He also records that the city’s coinage used iron instead of copper, a unique practice shared only with Sparta [PDF p. 32].  

The section on “Byzantine luxury” portrays the populace as indulgent, frequenting inns and feasting on tunny‑fish to the point of satirical exaggeration; the author suggests these tales may reflect jealous neighbors rather than factual excess [PDF p. 33]. He then recounts the city’s fifth capture by Philip II of Macedon in 339 BC, when a sudden light—interpreted as divine aid—repelled the assault and inspired the crescent‑and‑star emblem later adopted by Ottoman sultans [PDF p. 34].  

Subsequent centuries see Byzantium alternately allied with Rome, receiving the status of civitas libera et foederata, and later stripped of privileges by Emperor Vespasian, becoming a provincial town [PDF p. 34‑35]. The author emphasizes the city’s resilience during the tumult of the third century, noting its repeated sackings by usurpers Maximinus Daza (AD 314) and Gallienus (AD 263) and its swift recovery each time [PDF p. 36‑37].  

The narrative culminates with Constantine’s decision (AD 328‑330) to refound Byzantium as Constantinople. The author evaluates Constantine’s motives—geographic centrality, defensive strength, and symbolic break with Rome—citing his pragmatic assessment of imperial logistics and threats from the Danube frontier and Persia [PDF p. 39‑42]. He contrasts proposed sites (Naissus, Sofia, Nicomedia) with Byzantium, arguing that the latter offered the optimum combination of harbor, fortifications, and personal familiarity to the emperor [PDF p. 43‑44].  

Construction details follow: Constantine’s ceremonial laying out of the city’s boundaries, the erection of the Milion as a central milestone flanked by statues of the emperor and his mother Helena, and the clearing of a 150‑acre imperial palace site [PDF p. 44‑45]. The Augustaeum, a marble‑paved forum, is described as the civic heart, surrounded by the Senate House, the Baths of Zeuxippus, and the Hippodrome [PDF p. 46‑48].  

The Hippodrome’s role in public life is emphasized; its “Blues” and “Greens” factions extended beyond sport to politics, sometimes inciting riots such as the 523 AD uprising in which the mob crowned Hypatius emperor, and later serving as the backdrop for Justin I’s triumphal displays after the reconquest [PDF p. 49‑50].  

The author’s interpretation stresses Constantinople’s deliberate design as a unifying imperial capital and portrays the Hippodrome as a barometer of civic tension. He acknowledges limited source material for certain early events, noting that many details derive from later chroniclers and may reflect bias [PDF p. 32‑33].  

**Source limitations:** The narrative relies heavily on later Byzantine chroniclers (e.g., Procopius, Theophanes) and ancient historians, whose accounts are sometimes contradictory; the author admits uncertainty about internal administrative structures and the precise motives behind some political decisions. No archaeological evidence is directly cited.
