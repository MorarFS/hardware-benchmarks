The emperor’s reign ended with the Persian War’s conclusion in AD 532, while another threat rose and fell in the same year [PDF p. 101].  The author notes that the “Blues” and “Greens” – long‑standing circus factions – had become powerful political parties by the fifth century [PDF p. 101].  “Greens” supported the late emperor Anastasius and Monophysitism; “Blues” backed Justinian’s house and orthodoxy [PDF p. 101].  Their popular origins remained evident in frequent rioting and rough behaviour [PDF p. 101].  In January 532, serious street riots erupted; Justinian, usually favoring the Blues, ordered both sides’ leaders executed [PDF p. 101].  Seven rioters were executed, four beheaded before a crowd at St Conon’s monastery [PDF p. 102].  The remaining three were to be hanged, but the rope failed, allowing both a Blue and a Green to survive [PDF p. 102].  The mob rescued the half‑hung men, placing them in a nearby monastery [PDF p. 102].  This incident sparked six days of desperate rioting, during which Blues and Greens united under the cry “Nika, conquer” [PDF p. 102].  They demanded the removal of finance minister John of Cappadocia and prefect Eudemius, who had overseen the executions [PDF p. 102].  The ordinary city police could not control them, and Justinian promised to dismiss the officials [PDF p. 102].  The mob, incited by supporters of Anastasius’s house, called for Justinian’s deposition and for Hypatius, Anastasius’s nephew, to become Caesar [PDF p. 102].  Most troops were away fighting Persia, leaving the capital nearly defenseless [PDF p. 102].  Justinian could rely only on four thousand Imperial Guard, a few German auxiliaries, and five hundred cataphracts under Belisarius, fresh from the war [PDF p. 103].  Belisarius commanded the whole force, confronting the rioters who fought as fiercely as the Goths had a century earlier [PDF p. 103].  The main battle unfolded in the Augustaeum square between the palace and the Hippodrome [PDF p. 103].  Rebels set fire to the Senate’s Brazen Porch; the flames spread to St Sophia, destroying the cathedral on the third day [PDF p. 103].  The fire also consumed the hospital of Sampson and St Irene’s church [PDF p. 103].  While the blaze halted fighting, insurgents controlled most of the city, yet could not locate Hypatius, who had taken refuge with the emperor [PDF p. 103].  Justinian eventually drove Hypatius out, fearing his presence, and the rebels later seized him [PDF p. 103].  On the sixth day they carried Hypatius to the Hippodrome, placed him on the royal Kathisma seat, and crowned him with his wife’s gold chain [PDF p. 103].  Empress Theodora, refusing to flee, urged Justinian to attack the rebels, a speech recorded by Procopius [PDF p. 105].  Prompted by her bold words, Justinian ordered a final assault; Belisarius attacked all three Hippodrome gates [PDF p. 106].  Though the gate to the Kathisma resisted, the side entrances fell, and the rebels were routed [PDF p. 106].  Approximately thirty‑five thousand men died during the six‑day “Nika” sedition [PDF p. 106].  The author stresses that despite this slaughter, the Blues and Greens continued rioting for fifty more years, though never again threatening imperial rule [PDF p. 106].  

After the Persian truce and the suppression of the Nika revolt, Justinian finally pursued his long‑delayed program of western reconquest [PDF p. 107].  Six years after his accession, the Germanic kingdoms of Italy and Africa had become weak, enabling Justinian’s ambitions [PDF p. 107].  In Africa, King Hilderic had been overthrown by his cousin Gelimer; in Italy, Theodoric’s grandson Athalaric died, leaving the kingdom to his mother Amalasuntha, whose ill‑chosen marriage to the cruel Theodahad destabilized Ostrogothic rule [PDF p. 108].  Both Vandals and Goths were numerically small and spread thinly, making them vulnerable to a determined neighbor [PDF p. 108].  Justinian declared war on Gelimer in 533, using the unlawful deposition of Hilderic as pretext [PDF p. 110].  Belisarius sailed with ten thousand foot soldiers and five thousand horsemen, landing at Tripoli, which quickly surrendered [PDF p. 110].  He advanced unopposed, and after a hard fight near Carthage, captured the city the next day [PDF p. 111].  Gelimer’s final attempt near Carthage failed, ending the Vandal kingdom after 104 years of rule [PDF p. 111].  Gelimer fled to the Atlas Mountains but soon surrendered to Belisarius, who treated him kindly and returned him to Constantinople with the Vandal treasures [PDF p. 112].  In 535, Belisarius invaded Sicily with a modest force, capturing Palermo after a brief siege and taking the whole island within six months [PDF p. 114].  Theodahad’s incompetence led the Goths to kill him; Witiges succeeded but could not stop Belisarius, who seized Naples and then Rome without resistance [PDF p. 115].  The Gothic siege of Rome in 537 lasted over a year; despite numerical superiority, the Goths failed to breach the walls, and the imperial garrison held out [PDF p. 115‑116].  On May 540, the starving Gothic army abandoned Ravenna; Belisarius entered the city, ending the Gothic war [PDF p. 117].  However, the empire soon faced a new Persian conflict; Chosroes I invaded Antioch in 540, sacking the city after a brief battle [PDF p. 125‑126].  Justinian redirected troops east, appointing Belisarius as commander; after two years of inconclusive fighting, a peace treaty in 545 restored limited territories to Persia [PDF p. 126].  In AD 542, a devastating plague struck the empire, killing up to five thousand people daily in Constantinople and halting normal life [PDF p. 127‑128].  The chronicler records that the disease spared no class, region, or season, yet seemed to avoid the most wicked [PDF p. 128].  Justinian contracted the disease, survived, but never fully recovered his former vigor [PDF p. 128].  In his later years, he devoted increasing attention to theological disputes, especially Monophysitism, seeking compromise through edicts while forcing Pope Vigilius to sign his demands in AD 554 [PDF p. 129].  The author argues that this policy alienated the Western Church and deepened East‑West estrangement [PDF p. 129].  The death of Empress Theodora in AD 548 removed a bold political partner, leaving Justinian increasingly distrustful and isolated [PDF p. 130].  Even Belisarius, his most trusted general, was repeatedly deprived of troops and later recalled only during emergencies, such as the Hunnic incursion of 556, which he repelled with a small, undisciplined force [PDF p. 130‑131].  The author notes that Justinian’s final military success was the defeat of the Huns, after which he died in AD 565 [PDF p. 131].  

Beyond warfare, Justinian left a monumental architectural legacy; he rebuilt St Sophia within forty days of its Nika‑era destruction, employing Anthemius of Tralles as chief architect [PDF p. 133‑134].  The new cathedral combined a Greek cross plan with a massive central dome, becoming a model for later Byzantine churches [PDF p. 134‑135].  He also constructed countless churches, forts, hospitals, and public buildings across the empire, many still standing today [PDF p. 132‑133].  His codification of Roman law, supervised by Tribonian, produced the Institutes and Pandects, forming the foundation of European legal tradition [PDF p. 138‑139].  

After Justinian’s death, his successors – Justin II, Tiberius II, and Maurice – inherited a weakened state, exhausted by the plague, heavy taxation, and continuous wars [PDF p. 140‑141].  The Lombards crossed the Alps in AD 568, rapidly occupying northern Italy, sparing only Pavia, which fell in 571 and became their capital [PDF p. 142‑143].  Subsequent Lombard dukes established independent duchies, notably Spoleto and Benevento, while the remaining imperial territories were reduced to central and southern Italy [PDF p. 143‑144].  The papacy gained increasing autonomy, with Pope Gregory I acting independently of the exarch in Ravenna, negotiating directly with Lombard leaders [PDF p. 145‑146].  In the Balkans, new threats emerged: the Avars, nomadic horsemen from the steppes, began raiding Moesia after AD 562, demanding tribute yet repeatedly breaking agreements [PDF p. 148‑149].  More dangerous were the Slavs, who migrated into the Danube frontier after the Lombards’ departure, employing guerrilla tactics such as hiding in water to surprise Roman forces [PDF p. 149‑150].  Their incursions intensified after Justinian’s death, contributing to the empire’s gradual loss of the Balkan provinces [PDF p. 150].  

The source does not provide precise casualty figures for many battles, and relies heavily on Procopius’ accounts, which may reflect authorial bias.  Uncertain details include the exact numbers of troops involved in the Nika riot and the extent of Slav‑Avar cooperation, leaving aspects of the narrative open to further investigation.
