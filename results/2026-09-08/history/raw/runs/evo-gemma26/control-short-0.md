To reconstruct the past, historians must act as forensic linguists and detectives, meticulously parsing the layers of any given text. The ability to distinguish between an author’s account, quoted testimony, and inference is not merely a technical skill; it is the fundamental mechanism that prevents historical distortion. If a historian treats an opinion as a fact or a quote as an original observation, the resulting narrative becomes a fabrication rather than an analysis.

An **author’s account** refers to the direct narrative, interpretation, or descriptive framework provided by the writer of a document. This is the "voice" of the source. It includes the author's synthesis of events and their subjective framing of reality. For example, imagine a hypothetical diary entry from a soldier in 1914: *"The morning was heavy with a sense of impending doom as we marched toward the front lines."* Here, the statement about "impending doom" is the author’s account. It is not necessarily an objective fact that "doom" existed in the atmosphere; rather, it is the author's personal perception and emotional framing of the event. The historian must note this as a subjective perspective—a window into the soldier's psyche rather than a meteorological or tactical certainty.
