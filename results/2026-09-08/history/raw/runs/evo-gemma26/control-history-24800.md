This excerpt contains a Preface, letters from Wendell Phillips and William Lloyd Garrison, an autobiographical introduction, and the first nine chapters of Frederick Douglass’s *Narrative*.

**Prefatory Material**
In a Preface, Wendell Phillips recounts meeting Douglass at an anti-slavery convention in Nantucket in August 1841. He describes Douglass as a "fugitive slave" whose eloquent speech deeply moved the audience and clarified the horrors of slavery. Phillips notes that while Douglass was initially hesitant to enter public life, fearing he would do more harm than good, he eventually became a successful lecturing agent for anti-slavery societies.

William Lloyd Garrison, writing on May 1, 1845, defends the veracity of the *Narrative*. He asserts that Douglass’s account is truthful and not exaggerated. Garrison highlights the legal impossibility of slaves testifying against white men and notes that religious professions among Southern masters often serve as a "palpable imposture" to mask cruelty. He concludes with a call for "no compromise with slavery."

**Biographical Summary**
Douglass was born in Maryland, likely between 1817 and 1818. After escaping slavery in 1838, he moved to New York, married Anna Murray, and changed his name. He became an influential orator and abolitionist, later serving in various official capacities, including U.S. Minister to Haiti.

**Chapter Summaries**
*   **Chapter I:** Douglass explains that slaves are kept ignorant of their ages. He estimates he is 27 or 28. He describes the common practice of separating infants from their mothers to destroy natural affection; he saw his mother, Harriet Bailey, only a few times at night before her death when he was seven. He notes the "odious" reality that children of enslaved women follow the condition of their mothers, often resulting in masters fathering children with slaves. He recalls his first witness to extreme violence: his Aunt Hester being brutally whipped by his master, Captain Anthony, for disobeying orders regarding a young man named Ned Roberts.

*   **Chapter II:** Douglass describes life on Colonel Lloyd’s "Great House Farm." The plantation was a massive operation involving hundreds of slaves and various overseers. He details the meager food and clothing allowances provided to slaves and the harsh living conditions, including sleeping on cold floors. He contrasts the cruel overseer, Mr. Severe, with the more moderate Mr. Hopkins. Douglass notes that the "wild songs" of the slaves were not signs of happiness but expressions of deep sorrow and a prayer for deliverance.

*   **Chapter III:** The text describes Colonel Lloyd’s wealth, including a fine garden and an expensive riding equipage managed by Barney and his son. Douglass recounts how Lloyd’s extreme irritability led to arbitrary punishments, such as whipping an elderly man, Barney, for the perceived state of his horses. He also illustrates the danger of speaking the truth: a slave who told Lloyd he was treated poorly was subsequently sold to a Georgia trader.

*   **Chapter IV:** Douglass introduces Austin Gore, a highly efficient but "artful, cruel, and obdurate" overseer. Gore murdered a slave named Demby by shooting him after Demby refused to come out of a creek during a whipping. Gore’s actions were not investigated, as slaves could not testify against whites. Douglass notes that in Talbot County, killing a Black person was rarely treated as a crime.

*   **Chapter V:** Douglass describes his own childhood on the plantation, noting he had more leisure than others but suffered from extreme hunger and cold. He recalls the "ecstasy" of being sent to Baltimore to live with Hugh Auld, viewing it as a providential move toward freedom.

*   **Chapter VI:** In Baltimore, Douglass’s mistress, Sophia Auld, initially treated him with kindness and began teaching him to read. However, her husband, Hugh Auld, forbade this, arguing that education would make a slave "unmanageable." This revelation provided Douglass with the realization that literacy was the pathway to freedom. He describes how he taught himself by befriending white children and using scraps of paper and bread as tools for learning.

*   **Chapter VII:** Douglass details his seven years in Baltimore, noting how slavery transformed Sophia Auld from a kind woman into a "demon" through the exercise of irresponsible power. He explains his stratagems for learning to write by observing ship carpenters and copying letters from his master’s son’s copybooks.

*   **Chapter VIII:** Following the deaths of Captain Anthony and Lucretia Auld, Douglass was valued alongside livestock during the division of the estate. He expresses horror at being ranked with "horses, sheep, and swine." He recounts the ingratitude shown to his grandmother, who had served the family faithfully but was left to die in lonely poverty.

*   **Chapter IX:** In March 1832, Douglass was sent to live with Thomas Auld in St. Michael’s. He describes Auld as a "mean" and inconsistent man who provided insufficient food. After Auld’s religious conversion in August 1832, his cruelty intensified, as he used his faith to justify his slaveholding.
