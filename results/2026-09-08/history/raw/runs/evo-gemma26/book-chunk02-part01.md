The Hippodrome contained several ancient monuments, including a golden tripod dedicated at Delphi in 479 B.C. [PDF p. 51]. While the vast walls had crumbled, central decorations like the square bronze column remained [PDF p. 51]. Along the eastern wall stood various statues and the Milion, the empire's central milestone [PDF p. 51]. Constantine added a porphyry column featuring an image originally representing the Apollo of Hierapolis [PDF p. 51]. North of the Hippodrome sat the original, humbler Hagia Sophia, which was later destroyed by fire [PDF p. 52]. A wooden gallery connected this church to the Royal Gate of the palace [PDF p. 52].

Constantine chose Byzantium as his capital around A.D. 328 or 329 [PDF p. 54]. He held a consecration festival on May 11, 330, featuring Christian services [PDF p. 54]. To populate the city, Constantine offered privileges to senators, merchants, and artisans [PDF p. 55]. He also transferred the Egyptian wheat-tribute to Constantinople to ensure food supplies [PDF p. 55]. Although an edict named the city New Rome, people primarily called it Constantinople [PDF p. 56]. After Constantine died in A.D. 337, civil wars eventually left Constantius II as sole ruler [PDF p. 57].

The empire faced threats from the east and north [PDF p. 58]. While Sapor of Persia challenged the eastern frontier, the Goths posed a northern danger [PDF p. 58]. By A.D. 376, the Huns had driven the Visigoths toward the Roman Danube border [PDF p. 61]. The Emperor Valens allowed the Goths to enter as subject-allies [PDF p. 62]. However, corruption by officials like Lupicinus led to starvation and revolt [PDF p. 63]. This culminated in the disastrous Battle of Adrianople in A.D. 378, where Valens was killed [PDF p. 65, 67]. Theodosius later stabilized the region by enlisting Gothic war-bands into the Roman army [PDF p. 68].

The author argues that this policy of using barbarian auxiliaries under their own chiefs was dangerous [PDF p. 70]. Following Theodosius' death in A.D. 395, factional wars erupted between German and Roman parties [PDF p. 73]. Alaric led the Visigoths in revolt, eventually invading Italy [PDF p. 74, 75]. Meanwhile, Gainas attempted to seize power in Constantinople but was defeated by a local mob [PDF p. 76, 77]. The empire stabilized under the reorganization of Leo I, Zeno, and Anastasius [PDF p. 85]. These rulers utilized native troops, such as Isaurians, to reduce barbarian influence [PDF p. 87].

Justinian ascended the throne in A.D. 527 after his uncle Justinus died [PDF p. 91]. He married Theodora, a former actress, which caused significant social scandal [PDF p. 92, 93]. The author describes Justinian as a hard, suspicious, and tireless ruler [PDF p. 95]. His foreign policy aimed to reunite the Western Mediterranean provinces with the East [PDF p. 99]. Early in his reign, he fought an indecisive war against the Persian King Kobad [PDF p. 100]. This conflict highlighted the military merit of his general, Belisarius [PDF p. 100].

The text does not provide information regarding the ultimate outcome of Justinian's wars or his later reign.
