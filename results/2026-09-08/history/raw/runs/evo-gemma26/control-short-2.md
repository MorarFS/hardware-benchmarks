To reconstruct the past, historians must act as forensic linguists and detectives, meticulously parsing the layers of information found in primary sources. The ability to distinguish between an author’s account, quoted testimony, and inference is not merely a technical skill; it is the fundamental mechanism that prevents historical distortion. If a historian treats an opinion as a fact or a quote as an original observation, the resulting narrative becomes unreliable.

An **author’s account** refers to the direct observations, interpretations, or narratives provided by the person writing the document. This is the "voice" of the source itself. When an author writes, they are providing their subjective reality—what they saw, what they felt, and how they framed the events. For example, imagine a diary entry from a soldier during the American Civil War: *"The heat was suffocating, and the morale of my regiment seemed to wither under the relentless sun."* Here, the author is offering an account. The "suffocating heat" and the "withering morale" are the author’s direct perceptions and qualitative assessments. They are not necessarily objective truths, but they are the primary data points of that specific individual's experience.
