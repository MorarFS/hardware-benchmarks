C. W. C. Oman argues that the Byzantine Empire was neither decadent nor monotonous [PDF p. 13]. He contends the empire played a vital role in resisting Saracens and preserving learning [PDF p. 13]. The history begins with Greek colonists from Megara founding Byzantium approximately 2,558 years ago [PDF p. 27]. These settlers established their colony on a headland near the Golden Horn [PDF p. 27]. Legend suggests the Delphic oracle instructed them to build opposite Chalcedon [PDF p. 29]. The city's location provided significant military and commercial advantages for controlling Black Sea trade [PDF p. 30-31]. After falling to Alexander the Great, Byzantium eventually became a free confederate city under Rome [PDF p. 34]. However, Emperor Vespasian later stripped the city of these privileges [PDF p. 34].

Emperor Constantine the Great captured the city in A.D. 323 after besieging Licinius [PDF p. 37-38]. Oman interprets Constantine as a practical leader seeking a convenient administrative center [PDF p. 39-41]. He chose Byzantium for its superior military and commercial positioning [PDF p. 42-43]. Constantine established the new capital around A.D. 328 or 329 [PDF p. 54]. He held a consecration festival on May 11, 330, featuring Christian services [PDF p. 54]. The topography of Constantinople included a central marketplace and an imperial palace [PDF p. 44-45]. Public life centered on the Augustaeum and the Hippodrome [PDF p. 45-49]. Chariot racing in the Hippodrome fueled intense factional rivalries between the Blues and Greens [PDF p. 49]. These factions often caused public disorder and political instability [PDF p. 49-50].

The empire faced threats from Sapor of Persia and various northern groups [PDF p. 58]. By A.D. 376, Huns had driven the Visigoths toward the Roman Danube border [PDF p. 61]. Emperor Valens allowed the Goths to enter as subject-allies [PDF p. 62]. Corruption by officials like Lupicinus led to starvation and revolt [PDF p. 63]. This resulted in the Battle of Adrianople in A.D. 378, where Valens died [PDF p. 65, 67]. Theodosius later stabilized the region by enlisting Gothic war-bands into the Roman army [PDF p. 68]. Oman argues that using barbarian auxiliaries under their own chiefs was dangerous [PDF p. 70]. Following the death of Theodosius in A.D. 395, factional wars erupted between German and Roman parties [PDF p. 73].

Justinian ascended the throne in A.D. 527 after the death of his uncle [PDF p. 91]. His marriage to the former actress Theodora caused significant social scandal [PDF p. 92, 93]. Oman describes Justinian as a hard, suspicious, and tireless ruler [PDF p. 95]. Internal stability was shaken by the Nika riots in A.D. 532 [PDF p. 101]. These riots were fueled by the Blue and Green circus factions [PDF p. 101]. During this chaos, fires destroyed much of the city [PDF p. 103]. Empress Theodora refused to flee, prompting a final assault led by Belisarius [PDF p. 105]. This assault killed approximately 35,000 people in the Hippodrome [PDF p. 105-106]. Suppression of this dissent allowed Justinian to pursue foreign conquests [PDF p. 107].

Belisarius conquered the Vandal kingdom in Africa by A.D. 534 [PDF p. 111-112]. He also invaded Italy to challenge the Ostrogoths [PDF p. 114]. Although he captured Rome and Ravenna, the conquest was not permanent [PDF p. 115, 117]. Oman interprets this failure as a result of religious and racial tensions [PDF p. 109]. Justinian's later reign was marred by a devastating plague starting in A.D. 542 [PDF p. 127]. Simultaneously, Chosroes of Persia launched an invasion that sacked Antioch [PDF p. 125]. Following Justinian's death, the empire faced threats from Lombards, Avars, and Slavs [PDF p. 141]. The Slavs used unconventional tactics like hiding in water to ambush Romans [PDF p. 149].

The failure to stem these invasions contributed to the fall of Emperor Maurice [PDF p. 152]. In 602, his troops elected Phocas as leader and marched on Constantinople [PDF p. 153]. Oman characterizes Phocas as a brutal usurper whose reign caused imperial decay [PDF p. 154]. Heraclius took the throne in 610 after overthrowing Phocas [PDF p. 156]. He inherited an empire in chaos with provinces overrun by Persians and others [PDF p. 157]. Heraclius eventually defeated the Persians through skillful strategy [PDF p. 161]. However, his recovery was short-lived as the Saracen invasion began [PDF p. 166]. The Arabs captured Syria and Egypt, including Jerusalem in 637 [PDF p. 189].

Justinian II ascended the throne as a reckless tyrant who depleted the treasury [PDF p. 199]. In 695, Leontius seized power and deposed him [PDF p. 201]. This usurpation initiated twenty years of anarchy characterized by constant revolts [PDF p. 202]. Justinian II eventually returned to reclaim his throne in 705 [PDF p. 205]. His restoration triggered a reign of terror involving many executions [PDF p. 205]. Leo the Isaurian rose to power in 717 after defeating Theodosius III [PDF p. 208]. Leo successfully defended Constantinople against a massive Saracen invasion [PDF p. 211]. He also initiated the Iconoclastic movement to combat image-worship [PDF p. 215].

The Macedonian dynasty began with Basil the Macedonian [PDF p. 240]. The empire experienced a literary revival under later rulers like Leo VI [PDF p. 241]. Strength was maintained through a commercial monopoly on the Bosphorus [PDF p. 251]. Nicephorus Phocas exploited Caliphate fragmentation to conquer Crete in 961 A.D. [PDF p. 254]. John Zimisces later defeated a Russian invasion led by King Swiatoslaf in 971 [PDF p. 260, 261]. Basil II conducted a thirty-four year war against King Samuel [PDF p. 266, 267]. The Macedonian dynasty ended with the death of Theodora in 1057 [PDF p. 274]. This extinction triggered civil war and the Seljouk Turk invasions [PDF p. 275].

Alexius Comnenus stabilized the empire through diplomacy and treachery [PDF p. 283]. However, the Crusades caused a terminal decline in commercial wealth [PDF p. 293, 294]. The empire passed to the incompetent Isaac and Alexius Angelus [PDF p. 300]. These rulers relied on rapacious favorites who dispensed patronage for bribes [PDF p. 302]. In 1203, the Fourth Crusade arrived driven by Venetian interests [PDF p. 305]. The Crusaders established a Latin Empire that remained perpetually unstable [PDF p. 318]. Michael Paleologus finally reconquered the empire in 1261 [PDF p. 330]. However, the restored state faced permanent commercial and territorial decay [PDF p. 334].

The decline continued through the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries [PDF p. 351]. John Cantacuzenus attempted to supplant his master, John III [PDF p. 351]. He summoned Turkish princes for aid, which Oman argues destroyed the empire [PDF p. 353]. Ottoman forces established a permanent foothold at Gallipoli under Suleiman [PDF p. 355]. The narrative concludes with the fall of Constantinople on May 29, 1453 [PDF p. 373]. Mohammed II led seventy thousand troops in an assault against Constantine XI [PDF p. 372, 373]. The Emperor died during a final Janissary assault [PDF p. 374].

This synthesis is limited by the absence of information regarding Justinian's later reign [PDF p. 122], the ultimate outcome of his wars [PDF p. 122], and the fate of the Angelus brothers [PDF p. 300]. It also lacks details on Leo the Isaurian's administrative reforms [PDF p. 220] or the long-term fate of Greek enclaves [PDF p. 350].
