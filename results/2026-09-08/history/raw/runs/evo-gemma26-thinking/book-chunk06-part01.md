The Byzantine Empire derived strength from its monopoly over European commerce [PDF p. 251]. This wealth supported the empire despite its harsh taxation systems [PDF p. 251]. On the eastern frontier, the Caliphate had fractured into several weak states [PDF p. 252]. This fragmentation allowed Nicephorus Phocas to lead successful military campaigns [PDF p. 253]. Phocas conquered Crete in 961 and advanced into Northern Syria [PDF p. 254]. He later became emperor and continued these eastern conquests [PDF p. 256]. Phocas captured many fortresses in Cilicia and North Syria [PDF p. 255]. However, Phocas was unpopular at home due to his fiscal policies [PDF p. 256]. He was eventually murdered by John Zimisces and Theophano in 969 [PDF p. 259].

Zimisces achieved a great victory against the Russians in 971 [PDF p. 261]. He died shortly after his reign, perhaps by poison [PDF p. 265]. Basil II then ruled as a stern and effective emperor [PDF p. 266]. He fought a long war against the Bulgarian King Samuel [PDF p. 267]. Basil achieved a decisive victory and expanded the imperial borders [PDF p. 268]. He died in 1025, leaving the throne to his brother [PDF p. 270]. The Macedonian dynasty eventually ended with the death of Theodora [PDF p. 274].

The extinction of this dynasty triggered civil wars and Seljuk invasions [PDF p. 275]. Constantine Ducas failed to maintain the imperial army's strength [PDF p. 276]. Romanus Diogenes faced the Seljuks but lost at Manzikert in 1071 [PDF p. 280]. He was captured by Alp Arslan and later blinded [PDF p. 281]. Alexius Comnenus eventually restored order through diplomacy and warfare [PDF p. 283]. He fought the Normans and negotiated with the first Crusaders [PDF p. 285-289]. While the Crusades pushed back the Turks, they harmed commerce [PDF p. 292-293]. The Italians gained privileges that depleted the imperial treasury [PDF p. 294]. These privileges included free access to most Byzantine ports [PDF p. 294].

John II ruled with honesty and expanded the eastern frontiers [PDF p. 295]. His son Manuel I was a brave but reckless soldier [PDF p. 296]. Manuel's wasteful wars eventually weakened the empire's financial stability [PDF p. 297]. The dynasty ended after the tyrannical reign of Andronicus Comnenus [PDF p. 299]. The empire then fell to the incompetent Angelus brothers [PDF p. 300]. They ruled during a period when the empire was salvageable [PDF p. 300].
