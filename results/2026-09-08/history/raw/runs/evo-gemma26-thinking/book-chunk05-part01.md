The era commenced with the usurpation of Leontius in 695 [PDF p. 201]. He overthrew the mutilated Justinian II and ruled for three years [PDF p. 201-202]. Tiberius III subsequently seized the throne with help from Admiral Apsimarus [PDF p. 203]. Justinian II returned to power in 705 with Bulgarian support [PDF p. 205]. His restored reign was defined by extreme vengeance and widespread terror [PDF p. 205-206]. The tyrant was eventually overthrown by Philippicus in 711 [PDF p. 206]. This event initiated years of anarchy under several ephemeral rulers [PDF p. 207]. Leo the Isaurian eventually seized the throne in 717 [PDF p. 209]. He successfully defended Constantinople against a massive Saracen siege [PDF p. 211]. The siege failed due to famine and exceptionally harsh winters [PDF p. 212]. The author attributes the salvation of Christendom to Leo's victory [PDF p. 214].

Leo initiated the Iconoclastic movement to oppose image worship [PDF p. 215]. His son Constantine V intensified these persecutions against the monks [PDF p. 222-223]. He was a true persecutor who used scorpions for punishment [PDF p. 222]. His harsh religious policies earned him the nickname Copronymus [PDF p. 222]. The dynasty continued through Leo IV and the regent Empress Irene [PDF p. 223-224]. Irene blinded her son Constantine VI to maintain her authority [PDF p. 225]. Her reign caused a final breach between Rome and Constantinople [PDF p. 226]. Nicephorus I later took the throne and maintained iconoclast views [PDF p. 229]. Subsequent rulers included Michael I and Leo V, the Chameleon [PDF p. 230-231]. Michael the Amorian ruled after a conspiracy against Leo V [PDF p. 232-233].

Theophilus was a vehement iconoclast who lost the city of Amorium [PDF p. 235-236]. Theodora ended the iconoclastic era by restoring images in 842 [PDF p. 237]. Basil the Macedonian then founded a long and stable dynasty [PDF p. 240]. His successors, Leo VI and Constantine VII, were literary emperors [PDF p. 241]. They focused on writing significant works regarding war and administration [PDF p. 243-246]. The author suggests these men ended a long period of decay [PDF p. 247]. Byzantine art and literature also experienced a significant resurgence [PDF p. 248-250].

The author notes that many details of Leo's reforms remain unknown [PDF p. 220]. Monkish chroniclers often obscured his positive achievements in their writings [PDF p. 220].
