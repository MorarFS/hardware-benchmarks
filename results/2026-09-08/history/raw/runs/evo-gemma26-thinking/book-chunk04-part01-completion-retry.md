The invasion of Slavs and Avars devastated the Latin-speaking Thracian population [PDF p. 151]. This process nearly exterminated the original population north of the Balkan mountains [PDF p. 151]. Contemporary chronicler John of Ephesus described the 581 invasion as very severe [PDF p. 151]. General Priscus struggled to hold the Danube frontier against these nomadic invaders [PDF p. 152]. The inability to stop these incursions contributed to Emperor Maurice's eventual fall [PDF p. 152]. He became unpopular after refusing to pay a large ransom in 599 [PDF p. 152]. In 602, the Danube army revolted and elected Phocas as their emperor [PDF p. 153]. Phocas subsequently executed Maurice and all five of his young sons [PDF p. 153]. The author characterizes Phocas as a brutal and incompetent imperial ruler [PDF p. 154].

Phocas's reign triggered a major war with Chosroes of the Persian Empire [PDF p. 155]. The Persians ravaged North Syria and eventually entered into Asia Minor [PDF p. 155]. In 610, Heraclius overthrew Phocas and claimed the vacant imperial throne [PDF p. 156, 157]. The empire faced chaos and lost Jerusalem to the Persians in 614 [PDF p. 157]. Shahrbarz captured the "True Cross" and carried it into Persia [PDF p. 158]. This loss inspired a new sense of national and religious feeling [PDF p. 159]. Heraclius led several successful campaigns to reclaim these important holy places [PDF p. 161]. He finally secured peace and returned the Holy Wood in 628 [PDF p. 164, 165].

The exhaustion from the Persian war left the empire quite vulnerable [PDF p. 184]. The Saracen invasion then struck the weakened Roman and Persian powers [PDF p. 185]. The Arabs rapidly conquered much of the provinces in Syria and Egypt [PDF p. 189, 190]. During this era, the empire reorganized into military districts called Themes [PDF p. 193, 194]. These units combined military command with provincial and civil administration [PDF p. 195]. Constantine IV successfully defended Constantinople from a massive Arab siege [PDF p. 196, 197]. However, the later reign of Justinian II proved to be very difficult [PDF p. 199]. The author describes him as a selfish and very reckless tyrant [PDF p. 199]. He used extortionate ministers to fund his various unsuccessful wars [PDF p. 200].

The text notes that Syrian conquest details are not well preserved [PDF p. 188]. It also suggests that the origins of the Themes remain puzzling [PDF p. 193].
