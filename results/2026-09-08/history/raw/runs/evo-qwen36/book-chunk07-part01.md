The reigns of Isaac II and Alexius III Angelus marked a period of profound decline for the Byzantine Empire [PDF p. 301]. The emperors prioritized personal luxury over statecraft, leaving the military and financial systems in disarray [PDF p. 301]. The empire had lost its native recruiting grounds in Asia Minor to Seljouk invasions [PDF p. 301]. Consequently, it relied heavily on mercenaries who lacked discipline and loyalty [PDF p. 301]. Civil administration was equally corrupt, with offices sold to the highest bidders [PDF p. 302]. Isaac Angelus exposed administrative abuses by auctioning posts to officials who extorted profits from provincials [PDF p. 302]. Alexius III promised merit-based appointments but surrounded himself with rapacious favorites [PDF p. 302].

Isaac II lost Bulgaria and Cyprus during his rule [PDF p. 303]. Bulgarian rebels exploited the empire’s internal weakness to declare independence [PDF p. 303]. Isaac appointed incompetent generals, allowing the rebellion to grow until a civil war erupted [PDF p. 303]. He bribed Conrad of Montferrat to defeat the usurper Branas, but Bulgaria remained lost [PDF p. 303]. Cyprus was seized by Isaac Comnenus, who established an independent kingdom there [PDF p. 304]. Richard Coeur-de-Lion later conquered the island from Isaac Comnenus after a personal dispute [PDF p. 304]. Alexius III deposed his blind brother Isaac in a palace coup and continued the disastrous policies of neglect [PDF p. 305].

The Fourth Crusade diverted toward Constantinople due to Venetian financial interests and Byzantine political intrigue [PDF p. 306]. The Crusaders, unable to pay Venice, attacked Zara before being persuaded by the exiled Alexius Angelus to restore his father Isaac II [PDF p. 307]. They captured the city in 1203 after breaching the sea walls with floating towers [PDF p. 310]. However, the restored emperors failed to meet their financial obligations to the Crusaders [PDF p. 311]. This led to a popular revolt in January 1204, resulting in the death of Isaac II and the murder of Alexius Angelus [PDF p. 312].

Alexius Ducas, known as Murtzuphlus, assumed power but lacked resources or military strength [PDF p. 313]. He attempted to reform the army and fortify the walls against a second Crusader assault [PDF p. 314]. The Crusaders breached the sea wall on April 12, 1204, after a fierce naval battle [PDF p. 315]. Alexius Ducas fled the city, leading to its complete fall and a three-day sack of Constantinople [PDF p. 316]. The Crusaders plundered churches, destroyed ancient art, and divided the empire among themselves [PDF p. 317]. Baldwin of Flanders became Latin Emperor, while Boniface of Montferrat received Thessalonica [PDF p. 318].

The Latin Empire proved unstable from its inception, losing territory to Bulgarian and Greek forces [PDF p. 320]. Baldwin I was captured and killed by the Bulgarians in 1205 [PDF p. 321]. Meanwhile, Theodore Lascaris established the Empire of Nicaea in Asia Minor [PDF p. 324]. He successfully defended against both Latin and Seljouk threats, securing his realm’s independence [PDF p. 325]. Another Greek state emerged in Epirus under Michael Angelus, challenging Latin dominance in Greece [PDF p. 326]. John Ducas of Nicaea eventually united the Nicene and Thessalonican territories by 1246 [PDF p. 327].

Michael Paleologus usurped the throne from the young John IV Lascaris in 1259 [PDF p. 329]. He blinded his predecessor and consolidated power through ruthless political maneuvering [PDF p. 330]. In 1261, Alexius Strategopulus recaptured Constantinople for Michael while the Latin garrison was absent [PDF p. 331]. The restored Byzantine Empire was significantly smaller and economically weakened compared to its former self [PDF p. 334]. Trade monopolies had shifted to Italian republics, depriving Constantinople of its commercial centrality [PDF p. 336].

Michael Paleologus disbanded native militias out of suspicion, inadvertently opening the frontier to Ottoman Turks [PDF p. 339]. His successor Andronicus II hired the mercenary "Grand Company" to fight the Turks [PDF p. 342].
