The Slavic and Avar invasions devastated the Latinized Thraco-Illyrian population between 570 and 600 [PDF p. 151]. John of Ephesus described how these groups overran Greece, Thessalonica, and Thrace, enslaving inhabitants and settling by force [PDF p. 152]. General Priscus defended the Danube line but could not protect the open countryside from devastation [PDF p. 152]. Emperor Maurice faced unpopularity due to his inability to stop these invasions and a callous refusal in 599 to ransom prisoners [PDF p. 153]. In 602, Maurice ordered the Danube army to winter north of the river, prompting a mutiny led by Centurion Phocas [PDF p. 153]. Maurice fled but was captured and executed along with his sons at Chalcedon [PDF p. 154].

Phocas proved a brutal ruler who executed rivals, including General Narses, violating safe conduct [PDF p. 155]. King Chosroes of Persia declared war under the pretext of avenging Maurice [PDF p. 155]. Persian forces ravaged Syria and Asia Minor, reaching Chalcedon by 608 [PDF p. 155]. Heraclius, exarch of Africa, revolted against Phocas with support from Constantinople’s citizens [PDF p. 156]. He captured the city in 610, executing Phocas and assuming the throne [PDF p. 157]. The empire faced Persian occupation of Syria, Egypt, and parts of Asia Minor [PDF p. 158]. In 614, Persians captured Jerusalem and took the True Cross to Ctesiphon [PDF p. 159]. Chosroes sent a blasphemous letter demanding Heraclius’s submission [PDF p. 160].

Heraclius launched a crusade-like campaign in 622, driven by religious enthusiasm and Church funding [PDF p. 161]. He cleared Asia Minor of Persians through strategic maneuvering in Cilicia [PDF p. 162]. Subsequent campaigns invaded Media, burning fire-temples and forcing Persian retreats [PDF p. 163]. In 626, Avars and Slavs besieged Constantinople while Shahrbarz threatened the Bosphorus [PDF p. 164]. The city’s defenses held, aided by the Roman fleet preventing enemy coordination [PDF p. 165]. Heraclius defeated the main Persian army near Nineveh in 627 [PDF p. 166]. Chosroes was overthrown by his son Siroes, who sued for peace [PDF p. 167]. The True Cross was restored to Jerusalem in 628 [PDF p. 168].

The period from Constantine to Heraclius saw the gradual de-Romanization of the East [PDF p. 169]. Latin usage declined as Greek became dominant among educated classes and administrators [PDF p. 170]. Christianity transformed social norms, ending infanticide and modifying slavery practices [PDF p. 173]. Monasticism grew but sometimes diverted manpower from state duties [PDF p. 175]. The author rejects Lecky’s view of Byzantine cowardice, citing military successes against Persians and Saracens [PDF p. 180]. Treachery existed but did not prevent stable succession until Phocas [PDF p. 183].

The exhaustion from the Persian war allowed the rise of Islam under Mahomet [PDF p. 185]. Arab forces defeated Roman armies in Syria starting in 634 [PDF p. 187]. Jerusalem fell to Caliph Omar in 637 after a prolonged siege [PDF p. 189]. Egypt was conquered by 641, shortly before Heraclius’s death [PDF p. 190]. Persia also collapsed under Arab attacks at Kadesia and Nehauend [PDF p. 191]. Internal strife followed Heraclius’s death, involving his wife Martina and sons Constantine III and Heracleonas [PDF p. 192]. Constans II succeeded them after deposing Heracleonas in 642 [PDF p. 193].

Constans reorganized the empire into military themes to manage defense against Saracens [PDF p. 194]. He campaigned in Italy before being assassinated in 668 [PDF p. 196]. Constantine IV defended Constantinople against a massive Arab siege from 674 to 678 using Greek fire [PDF p. 197]. The Bulgarians settled between the Danube and Balkans around 679 [PDF p. 198]. Justinian II succeeded his father in 685, displaying tyrannical tendencies early in his reign [PDF p. 199]. He provoked war with Persia over coinage and suffered military defeats [PDF p. 200]. His ministers Theodotus and Stephanus raised funds through extortion and cruelty [PDF p. 200].
