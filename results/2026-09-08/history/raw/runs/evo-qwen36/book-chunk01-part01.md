C.W.C. Oman’s preface challenges the traditional view that Byzantium represented corruption and decadence [PDF p. 13]. He argues that earlier historians like Gibbon dismissed the East-Roman Empire as monotonous and depressing [PDF p. 13]. Two works by Finlay and Bury helped correct this negative perception among English readers [PDF p. 13]. Oman intends to write in the spirit of these later scholars rather than following Gibbon’s lead [PDF p. 14]. He acknowledges his debt to both the veteran of Greek Independence and the Dublin professor [PDF p. 14]. The author claims acquaintance with chroniclers like Ammianus, Procopius, and Anna Comnena justifies his task [PDF p. 14].

The narrative begins by describing the founding of Byzantium two thousand five hundred years prior to the text’s writing [PDF p. 27]. Greek colonists from Megara established a settlement on a headland between the Bosphorus and the Golden Horn [PDF p. 27]. They sought advice from the oracle at Delphi, which told them to build opposite the city of the blind [PDF p. 29]. The settlers interpreted this as a command to choose the superior site over Chalcedon on the Asiatic shore [PDF p. 29]. This location offered military impregnability and control over Black Sea trade routes [PDF p. 30]. Byzantium maintained its independence for centuries, though it faced Persian domination for thirty years [PDF p. 31]. It played a role in the Ionic Revolt against Persia under Histiaeus [PDF p. 31]. The city eventually threw off Persian rule after the Greek victories at Salamis and Mycale [PDF p. 31].

Athens later dominated Byzantium, capturing it twice through surrender and treachery during the fifth century B.C. [PDF p. 32]. The city suffered under Roman Emperor Severus in A.D. 196 after refusing to submit to his authority [PDF p. 35]. Severus destroyed its walls and confiscated citizen property as punishment for its resistance [PDF p. 35]. Gothic raids in the third century further devastated the population, nearly exterminating the original Megarian race [PDF p. 36]. The city recovered under strong Illyrian emperors who provided peace and stability [PDF p. 36]. Licinius used Byzantium as a key fortress against his rival Constantine during their civil war [PDF p. 37]. Constantine besieged the city for months before forcing its surrender in A.D. 323 [PDF p. 38].

Constantine then chose Byzantium to become the new capital of the Roman Empire, renaming it Constantinople [PDF p. 41]. He selected this site because Rome was too distant from the empire’s critical northern and eastern frontiers [PDF p. 42]. The new city would serve as an administrative center closer to the Balkans and Asia Minor [PDF p. 42]. Constantine marked out vast boundaries for the new capital, including all seven hills of the peninsula [PDF p. 43]. He constructed a grand palace complex near the sea and established the Augustaeum forum [PDF p. 45]. The Senate House and Baths of Zeuxippus were built to mirror the institutions of Old Rome [PDF p. 46]. Constantine also erected the Hippodrome, which became central to civic life and factional rivalry [PDF p. 48]. The spina of the Hippodrome featured an Egyptian obelisk and a bronze serpent monument [PDF p. 50].

The supplied text ends abruptly during the description of these monuments in the Hippodrome [PDF p. 50]. The narrative does not cover the subsequent history of the empire beyond this foundational period [PDF p. 50]. Consequently, the summary cannot address later events such as the reign of Justinian or the fall of Constantinople [PDF p. 17]. The source material provided is incomplete regarding the full scope of Oman’s historical account [PDF p. 50].
