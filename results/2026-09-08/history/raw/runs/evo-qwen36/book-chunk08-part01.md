The text describes the Revolt of Cantacuzenus as a pivotal moment in the decline of the Byzantine Empire [PDF p. 351]. John Cantacuzenus, the prime minister to Andronicus III, sought to usurp the throne from the nine-year-old heir, John V [PDF p. 351]. He possessed literary talents but lacked military or statesmanlike abilities [PDF p. 351]. His ambition mirrored that of Michael VIII, who had similarly supplanted a young ruler in 1258 [PDF p. 351]. Cantacuzenus attempted to replicate this success through bribery and intrigue [PDF p. 351]. However, his fatal procrastination allowed the Empress Dowager Anne of Savoy to organize resistance [PDF p. 352]. When he finally declared himself emperor in 1347, he failed to seize Constantinople immediately [PDF p. 352].

To secure his position, Cantacuzenus invited foreign allies into Byzantine territory [PDF p. 353]. He summoned Stephen Dushan of Serbia and the Turkish princes Orkhan and Amour [PDF p. 353]. These allies prevented his immediate defeat but devastated the empire in the process [PDF p. 353]. Stephen Dushan conquered Macedonia, Thrace, and Thessaly, establishing a Serbian empire that threatened to dominate the Balkans [PDF p. 353]. The author suggests this outcome might have benefited Christendom by creating a single power capable of resisting the Turks [PDF p. 353]. However, Dushan died in 1355, and his realm quickly fragmented among rival princes [PDF p. 354].

Cantacuzenus relied more heavily on the Turkish mercenaries provided by Orkhan [PDF p. 354]. These troops ravaged Thrace and sold Byzantine subjects into slavery [PDF p. 354]. The depth of Cantacuzenus’s degradation was marked when he gave his daughter Theodora to Orkhan for his harem [PDF p. 354]. After six years of war, a hollow peace recognized Cantacuzenus as co-emperor and guardian of John V [PDF p. 354]. The civil war had left Macedonia and Thessaly in Serbian hands and Thrace ruined by Turkish incursions [PDF p. 354]. The author characterizes Cantacuzenus as one of the great destroyers of the Eastern Empire alongside Isaac and Alexius Angelus [PDF p. 354].

Cantacuzenus continued to rule jointly with John V for seven years, failing to reclaim Serbian territories [PDF p. 355]. In 1354, the young emperor asserted his authority and attempted to dethrone his guardian [PDF p. 355]. Cantacuzenus called upon Orkhan’s troops, but a surprise naval attack allowed John V to capture Constantinople [PDF p. 355]. The usurper was tonsured and confined to a monastery, where he wrote a history of his times [PDF p. 355]. However, the Ottoman Turks remained in Thrace as principals rather than auxiliaries [PDF p. 355]. Suleiman, son of Orkhan, seized Gallipoli, establishing the first permanent Ottoman foothold in Europe [PDF p. 355].

Murad I succeeded Orkhan and rapidly conquered Thrace from sea to sea by 1361 [PDF p. 356]. The decisive battle at Adrianople reduced the Byzantine Empire to a mere head without a body [PDF p. 356]. Murad I chose not to besiege Constantinople, focusing instead on wars against Serbia and Bulgaria [PDF p. 356]. John V became a humble vassal, enduring humiliations such as being compelled to help siege Philadelphia [PDF p. 356]. The last Christian stronghold in Asia surrendered after seeing the imperial banner among Turkish forces [PDF p. 357].

The author argues that after 1370, the empire ceased to function as a bulwark for Christendom [PDF p. 358]. The defense of Europe fell to Serbia and Hungary for the next century and a half [PDF p. 358]. Manuel II Paleologus briefly regained territories during the Ottoman civil war following Tamerlane’s defeat of Bayezid at Angora in 1402 [PDF p. 359]. However, this opportunity was not taken by European powers due to internal conflicts like the Hussite wars [PDF p. 362]. Mohammed I reunified the Ottoman Empire by 1421, and Murad II subsequently besieged Constantinople with cannon [PDF p. 362-365].
