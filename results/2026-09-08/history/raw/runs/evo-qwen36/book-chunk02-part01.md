North of the Hippodrome stood an earlier, humble church dedicated to Divine Wisdom [PDF p. 53]. A wooden gallery connected this church directly to the palace [PDF p. 53]. These structures formed the heart of the imperial city [PDF p. 54]. Other notable buildings included granaries and the Golden Gate [PDF p. 54]. An equestrian statue of Constantine remained a famous landmark until the fifteenth century [PDF p. 54].

Constantine chose Byzantium as his capital in A.D. 328 or 329 [PDF p. 55]. He consecrated the new city on May 11, 330 [PDF p. 55]. The city was established as Christian from its inception [PDF p. 55]. Constantine invited senators and wealthy proprietors to settle there [PDF p. 55]. He granted them positions in the senate and land for homes [PDF p. 55]. Artisans were enticed by special privileges [PDF p. 55]. The distribution of free corn from Egypt attracted merchants and seamen [PDF p. 56]. An edict in 330 A.D. officially named the city New Rome [PDF p. 56]. However, the populace continued to call it Constantinople [PDF p. 56].

Constantine died in 337 A.D., leaving his realm to his sons and nephews [PDF p. 57]. Civil wars eventually concentrated power under Constantius II [PDF p. 57]. The empire faced threats from Persia in the east [PDF p. 58]. However, the primary danger emerged from the north [PDF p. 58]. The Goths had been settled beyond the Danube for a century [PDF p. 59]. They were gradually adopting Christianity and Roman customs [PDF p. 60]. In A.D. 372, the Huns invaded Gothic territories [PDF p. 61]. The Ostrogoths were crushed by the Hunnic horde [PDF p. 61]. The Visigoths fled under Duke Fritigern toward the Roman border [PDF p. 61].

In A.D. 376, Emperor Valens allowed the Visigoths to cross into Moesia [PDF p. 62]. Roman officials mistreated the immigrants and withheld food supplies [PDF p. 63]. This extortion drove the Goths to rebellion under Fritigern [PDF p. 64]. The Battle of Adrianople in 378 A.D. resulted in a catastrophic defeat for Rome [PDF p. 65]. Valens died, and his army was nearly annihilated [PDF p. 67]. The Goths failed to sack Constantinople due to its strong walls [PDF p. 67]. Emperor Theodosius later made peace with the Goths [PDF p. 68]. He enlisted them as auxiliaries in large numbers [PDF p. 68]. This policy placed significant military power in German hands [PDF p. 69].

After Theodosius died, factionalism weakened both halves of the empire [PDF p. 73]. Alaric led the Visigoths in revolt against Arcadius [PDF p. 74]. He eventually moved into Italy and sacked Rome [PDF p. 75]. In the East, Gainas commanded Gothic troops but was killed by Huns [PDF p. 76]. Patriarch John Chrysostom clashed with Empress Eudoxia over court morality [PDF p. 78]. He was deposed and exiled in 404 A.D [PDF p. 79].

Theodosius II succeeded Arcadius as a child under the regency of Pulcheria [PDF p. 81]. She ruled effectively for many years [PDF p. 81]. The empire faced Hunnic raids but maintained stability [PDF p. 83]. Marcianus and Pulcheria ended the tribute to Attila [PDF p. 85]. Emperors Leo I, Zeno, and Anastasius reorganized the army using native Isaurians [PDF p. 87]. This reduced reliance on German auxiliaries [PDF p. 87]. Zeno sent the Ostrogoths to Italy under Theodoric in 488 A.D [PDF p. 90].
