The text describes the usurpation of Justinian II by Leontius in 695 [PDF p. 201]. Justinian had become tyrannical after military failures against the Saracens [PDF p. 201]. A monk named Paul advised Leontius to rebel, leading to the capture and mutilation of Justinian [PDF p. 201]. Leontius ruled for three years amidst constant revolts and external defeats [PDF p. 202]. The Saracens captured Carthage in 697 after a long siege [PDF p. 202]. Tiberius Apsimarus then deposed Leontius with fleet support [PDF p. 203]. He slit Leontius’s nose and exiled him to Cherson [PDF p. 203]. Tiberius III achieved some military successes but lacked popular loyalty [PDF p. 203]. Justinian escaped from exile by killing two Khazar chiefs [PDF p. 204]. He fled through a storm to Bulgaria, where King Terbel supported his restoration [PDF p. 204-205]. Justinian returned in 705 and executed Leontius and Tiberius III with extreme cruelty [PDF p. 205-206]. His reign became a terror marked by widespread executions and mutilations [PDF p. 206]. He even sacked his former place of exile, Cherson [PDF p. 206]. In 711, General Philippicus seized the throne while Justinian was absent [PDF p. 206]. The Heraclian dynasty ended with the murder of Justinian’s son [PDF p. 206].

The subsequent six years were anarchical and disorganized [PDF p. 207]. Philippicus was quickly replaced by Anastasius II, who was then overthrown by Theodosius III [PDF p. 207-208]. During this chaos, the Saracens ravaged frontier cities like Tyana and Antioch-in-Pisidia [PDF p. 208]. Caliph Welid prepared a massive invasion to besiege Constantinople [PDF p. 208]. Leo the Isaurian, general of the Anatolic theme, deposed Theodosius in 717 [PDF p. 209]. He faced a Saracen army and fleet led by Moslemah and Suleiman [PDF p. 210-211]. Leo used Greek fire to disrupt the naval blockade effectively [PDF p. 211]. A severe winter decimated the besiegers, causing many deaths from cold and disease [PDF p. 212]. Bulgarian attacks on the Saracen rear forced Moslemah to retreat in 718 [PDF p. 213]. This victory saved the empire from destruction and established Leo’s authority [PDF p. 214].

Leo then initiated the Iconoclastic movement against image worship [PDF p. 215-216]. He argued that such practices were superstitious and influenced by Islam [PDF p. 217-218]. His policies faced violent resistance from monks and the populace [PDF p. 219]. Despite riots, Leo maintained army support to enforce his reforms [PDF p. 220]. He also reorganized laws and finances, stabilizing the empire [PDF p. 220]. His son Constantine V continued the persecution more brutally [PDF p. 222]. He dissolved monasteries and exiled many monks [PDF p. 223]. Leo IV ruled mildly but died young in 780 [PDF p. 223]. Empress Irene regented for her son Constantine VI [PDF p. 224]. She stopped the persecution to gain popular support [PDF p. 225]. In 797, she blinded and deposed her own son [PDF p. 225]. Nicephorus I overthrew Irene in 802 [PDF p. 226]. He was an Iconoclast but did not persecute opponents harshly [PDF p. 229]. He died fighting the Bulgarians in 811, and his head was made into a cup [PDF p. 229-230]. Michael I succeeded him but lost to the Bulgarians again [PDF p. 231]. Leo V the Armenian took power and attempted a moderate religious policy [PDF p. 231]. He was assassinated in 820 by Michael the Amorian [PDF p. 232-233].

Michael II allowed Crete to fall to Saracens despite failed expeditions to retake it [PDF p. 234]. His son Theophilus resumed harsh Iconoclasm and suffered military defeats [PDF p. 235-236]. He died in 842, leaving a three-year-old son, Michael III [PDF p. 237]. Empress Theodora restored image worship in 843 [PDF p. 238]. She raised her brother Bardas as co-regent [PDF p. 239]. Michael III became a drunkard and murdered Bardas [PDF p. 239]. He appointed Basil the Macedonian as Caesar [PDF p. 239]. Basil assassinated Michael in 867 and founded the Macedonian dynasty [PDF p. 240]. Basil codified laws and persecuted Paulicians [PDF p. 240-241]. Sicily was finally lost to Saracens during his reign [PDF p. 241].

The next eighty years were peaceful under Leo VI and Constantine VII [PDF p. 242]. Both emperors were literary figures rather than warriors [PDF p. 241-242]. Leo wrote a manual on military tactics that praised the Byzantine army [PDF p. 243-245]. Constantine wrote extensively on administration and court ceremonies [PDF p. 246-247]. Literature and art revived significantly during this period [PDF p. 248-250]. The Iconoclastic controversy had inadvertently stimulated painting and manuscript illumination [PDF p. 250]. Constantinople remained a vital commercial hub throughout the ninth century [PDF p. 250].

The source material ends abruptly after discussing commerce in the ninth century [PDF p. 250]. It does not cover later events or provide a conclusion to the narrative [PDF p. 250]. The text relies on monkish chroniclers who may have biased their accounts against Iconoclasts [PDF p. 220]. Some details, such as casualty numbers in Saracen defeats, are presented with uncertainty [PDF p. 213]. The author’s interpretations of religious motivations are explicit but not universally verified by external evidence within the text [PDF p. 217-218].
