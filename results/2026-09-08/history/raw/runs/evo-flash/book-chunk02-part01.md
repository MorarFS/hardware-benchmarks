The Hippodrome’s central spine featured three surviving monuments that outlasted the surrounding walls [PDF p. 51]. The first was a golden tripod dedicated at Delphi in 479 B.C. after the Persian defeat at Plataea [PDF p. 51]. Phocians had stolen its gold centuries earlier, yet inscriptions on the serpent coils remained visible to archaeologists [PDF p. 51]. A square bronze column of more modern work stood nearby, contrasting with the ancient artifacts [PDF p. 51]. The Turks now call this open space the Atmeidan, remembering its equestrian past [PDF p. 51]. Along the eastern wall stood chapels and statues near the Milion milestone [PDF p. 51]. Constantine contributed a porphyry column topped by a bronze Apollo from Hierapolis [PDF p. 51]. He altered this statue into an imperial image by replacing its head with his own features [PDF p. 51]. This practice mirrored Roman papal actions that converted Aurelius statues into St. Peter figures [PDF p. 53]. North of the Hippodrome stood Constantine’s original church, Hagia Sophia [PDF p. 53]. It was a humble basilica, not the famous domed structure known today [PDF p. 53]. This earlier building burned twice and left no physical trace of its form [PDF p. 53]. A wooden gallery connected the church to the palace Royal Gate [PDF p. 53]. This passage allowed the emperor to attend services without crossing public streets [PDF p. 53]. The author compares this structure to the elevated corridor between Florence’s Pitti and Uffizi palaces [PDF p. 53]. These buildings formed the city’s heart, hosting most significant historical events [PDF p. 53]. Other notable sites included the Church of the Holy Apostles and public granaries [PDF p. 54]. An equestrian statue of Constantine stood near the praetorian prefect’s palace [PDF p. 54]. Legends surrounded this statue throughout the Middle Ages [PDF p. 54].

Constantine chose Byzantium as his capital around A.D. 328 or 329 [PDF p. 54]. He consecrated the city on May 11, 330, during a major festival [PDF p. 54]. Christian bishops blessed the palace and held the first service in St. Sophia [PDF p. 55]. Constantine remained unbaptized but insisted the new capital be Christian from its inception [PDF p. 55]. Pagan temples were spared only as architectural remnants, not for worship [PDF p. 55]. Statues of gods adorned public buildings solely as works of art [PDF p. 55]. To populate the city, Constantine invited senators and wealthy provincials to settle there [PDF p. 55]. He granted them seats in his new senate and land for their homes [PDF p. 55]. Court officials and their households formed a large part of the population [PDF p. 55]. Artisans received special privileges, while merchants flocked to the growing commercial hub [PDF p. 55]. The transfer of Egyptian wheat tribute provided free corn to Constantinople’s populace [PDF p. 55]. This policy demoralized citizens but effectively attracted immigrants from across the empire [PDF p. 55]. An edict in 330 A.D. named the city New Rome, though people preferred Constantine’s name [PDF p. 56].

Constantine died on May 22, 337, after receiving baptism on his deathbed [PDF p. 57]. His will divided the realm among sons and nephews, leading to civil wars [PDF p. 57]. Constantius II eventually unified the empire under his sole rule [PDF p. 57]. The author describes Constantius as able but gloomy and suspicious [PDF p. 57]. He initiated the first persecution of Christians by other Christians [PDF p. 57]. His successor Julian attempted to restore pagan worship but failed [PDF p. 57]. Both emperors feared Persian threats from the east, ignoring northern dangers [PDF p. 58]. The Goths had raided Balkan provinces for a century and a half [PDF p. 58]. Constantine defeated them between A.D. 328 and 332, forcing their king to surrender hostages [PDF p. 58]. For forty years, the Goths remained relatively peaceful and settled as farmers [PDF p. 60]. Many converted to Christianity under Bishop Ulfilas, who translated the Bible into Gothic [PDF p. 60]. The author notes this translation remains a precious linguistic relic [PDF p. 60].
