The unwarlike Emperor Valens entered the field in person during the following year. He brought great reinforcements from Asia Minor to aid his struggling forces [PDF p. 65]. His nephew Gratian also marched through Pannonia to assist the Lower Danube regions [PDF p. 65]. This personal intervention by Valens resulted in a catastrophic military disaster for Rome. The Goths forced the Balkan line and approached Adrianople in three hundred seventy-eight A.D. [PDF p. 65]

Valens attacked with an army of sixty thousand men near the Gothic encampment. Contemporaries expected victory because Roman infantry still held a reputation for invincibility [PDF p. 65]. However, the Goths had adopted cavalry tactics while dwelling in South Russia and Roumania. They considered fighting on horseback more honorable than engaging on foot during this period [PDF p. 65]

A great body of Gothic horsemen charged the Roman flank unexpectedly during battle. These riders had been foraging at a distance but rushed to the field upon hearing news. The Roman left wing collapsed under their tremendous impact and drove into the center [PDF p. 66]. Legions became wedged together in hopeless confusion, unable to deploy or flee effectively [PDF p. 66]

The Goths rode into this quivering mass, using lance and sword against helpless enemies. Forty thousand men fell before survivors could break out and follow their cavalry in flight [PDF p. 67]. The Emperor Valens died on the field alongside thirty-five commanders of different corps [PDF p. 67]

This defeat was compared by Ammianus Marcellinus to the slaughter at Cannae historically. The army of the East was almost annihilated and never reorganized on old lines [PDF p. 67]. Victorious Goths subsequently attacked Constantinople, marking its first siege since becoming an imperial city [PDF p. 67]

The attackers abandoned their siege machines upon seeing the vast circuit of walls. They retreated to Thrace after skirmishing with Saracen cavalry near the Golden Gate [PDF p. 67]. The author argues that Constantine’s prescience regarding the city's impregnability was thus justified [PDF p. 68]

Theodosius succeeded Valens and sought to repair the disaster through caution and courage. He destroyed marauding bands without attacking the main Gothic body directly in battle [PDF p. 68]. After Fritigern died, Theodosius made peace with Athanarich by granting land in Thrace [PDF p. 68]

The emperor enlisted Gothic chiefs and their war-bands into his own armies effectively. Within ten years, forty thousand Teutonic horsemen served him at higher pay than Romans [PDF p. 68]

These auxiliaries helped Theodosius win victories over rebel Magnus Maximus in three hundred eighty-five A.D. They also aided in defeating the rebel Eugenius in three hundred ninety-four A.D. [PDF p. 70]

The author interprets this policy as politically dangerous for the Roman Empire significantly. Barbarian auxiliaries were left under their own chiefs rather than mixed with Roman troops [PDF p. 70]. Their loyalty depended on personal ties to Theodosius and the spell of Roman culture [PDF p. 70]

King Athanarich’s visit to Constantinople illustrated this lingering awe of Roman grandeur clearly. He admired the walls, fleets, and disciplined soldiery upon entering the royal city [PDF p. 70]

The death of Theodosius in three hundred ninety-five A.D. ended this stabilizing influence. His weak sons Arcadius and Honorius inherited thrones that were vulnerable to factional strife [PDF p. 70]

The empire’s bureaucratic centralization made the reliance on barbarian troops particularly hazardous then. Civil officials viewed the population as tax sources without rights or local patriotism [PDF p. 71]

A professional army had no sympathy for unarmed provincials due to this separation. Theodosius swamped native elements with Gothic war-bands and handed military posts to Teutons [PDF p. 72]

Stilicho became commander-in-chief in the West under the young Emperor Honorius effectively. He was a barbarian by blood but married to Theodosius’s niece Serena [PDF p. 73]

In the East, the Gothic captain Gainas murdered the minister Rufinus at a review. Arcadius then appointed Eutropius as minister and Gainas as commander-in-chief for the East [PDF p. 73]

Alaric raised the Visigoths in revolt shortly after Theodosius died due to pay disputes. He raided from the Danube to Greece while Stilicho blockaded him in Arcadia [PDF p. 74]

Stilicho allowed Alaric to escape, leading to a peace treaty where Alaric gained land. This arrangement gave Alaric undisputed sway over parts of the Balkan Peninsula for years [PDF p. 74]

Alaric invaded Northern Italy in four hundred one A.D., shifting focus from Constantinople. Stilicho fought hard but was later executed on charges of treason by Honorius [PDF p. 75]
