The Hippodrome’s central spine featured three surviving monuments, including a serpent column dedicated at Delphi in 479 B.C. [PDF p. 51]. The golden tripod was stolen by Phocians centuries earlier, yet the pedestal inscriptions remain visible today [PDF p. 51]. A square bronze column of later workmanship stood nearby, contrasting with the ancient artifacts [PDF p. 51]. These decorations survived while the surrounding walls crumbled into the open space known as the Atmeidan [PDF p. 51]. Along the eastern wall, the Milion served as a key landmark among various chapels and statues [PDF p. 51]. Constantine contributed a porphyry column topped by a bronze Apollo from Hierapolis [PDF p. 51]. The emperor’s face replaced the god’s head, mirroring later papal alterations of Aurelius at Rome [PDF p. 52]. North of the Hippodrome stood an earlier Basilica-shaped St. Sophia, distinct from the current domed structure [PDF p. 53]. This original church burned twice and left no architectural trace of its initial form [PDF p. 53]. A wooden gallery connected the cathedral to the palace Royal Gate, avoiding public streets [PDF p. 53]. The author compares this elevated passage to the corridor linking Florence’s Pitti and Uffizi palaces [PDF p. 53]. These buildings formed Constantinople’s heart, hosting most significant historical events within the city [PDF p. 53]. Constantine designated the Church of the Holy Apostles as his family’s burial site [PDF p. 53]. Outlying structures included granaries, the Golden Gate, and the praetorian prefect’s palace [PDF p. 54]. An equestrian statue of Constantine stood near the prefecture, attracting legends through the Middle Ages [PDF p. 54].

Constantine chose Byzantium as his capital around A.D. 328 or 329 [PDF p. 54]. He consecrated the city on May 11, 330, with Christian bishops blessing the palace and St. Sophia [PDF p. 55]. Although unbaptized, Constantine ensured the new capital remained free of pagan worship practices [PDF p. 55]. Statues in baths and senate houses served as art rather than religious objects [PDF p. 55]. The emperor invited senators and wealthy proprietors to populate the city with new dwellings [PDF p. 55]. Artisans received special privileges, while merchants flocked there for commercial opportunities [PDF p. 55]. Free corn distribution attracted immigrants by transferring Egypt’s wheat tribute from Rome to Constantinople [PDF p. 55]. An edict in 330 A.D. named the city New Rome, though people preferred Constantine’s name [PDF p. 56].

Constantine died on May 22, 337, after receiving baptism on his deathbed [PDF p. 57]. His realm was divided among sons and nephews until civil wars concentrated power under Constantius II [PDF p. 57]. Constantius II focused on ecclesiastical controversies rather than external threats [PDF p. 57]. Julian the Apostate briefly succeeded him, attempting to restore pagan worship without success [PDF p. 58]. Both emperors feared Persian aggression more than northern barbarian incursions [PDF p. 58]. The Goths had been subdued by Constantine between A.D. 328 and 332 [PDF p. 58]. They settled peacefully for forty years, adopting Christianity and farming practices along the Danube [PDF p. 60]. Bishop Ulfilas translated the Bible into Gothic, preserving a vital linguistic relic [PDF p. 60]. The Goths were divided into Visigoths in the south and Ostrogoths in the north [PDF p. 60].

Around A.D. 372, Huns from beyond the Don and Volga invaded Gothic territories [PDF p. 61]. They crushed the Ostrogoths, forcing survivors to flee toward the Danube Delta [PDF p. 61]. The Visigoths under Duke Fritigern retreated to the Roman border in despair [PDF p. 61]. A contemporary writer describes over two hundred thousand Goths pleading for passage across the river [PDF p. 62]. Emperor Valens, ruling the East from Constantinople, faced a difficult decision regarding these refugees [PDF p. 62]. He allowed them to cross if they surrendered arms and provided hostages [PDF p. 63]. Roman officials accepted bribes to let Goths keep their weapons in violation of imperial orders [PDF p. 63]. Governor Lupicinus hoarded food supplies, driving starving Goths to sell children for bread [PDF p. 63]. An affray at Marcianopolis sparked open rebellion when Fritigern escaped an assassination attempt [PDF p. 64]. The ensuing war saw Gothic raids across the Balkans despite initial Roman resistance [PDF p. 64].
