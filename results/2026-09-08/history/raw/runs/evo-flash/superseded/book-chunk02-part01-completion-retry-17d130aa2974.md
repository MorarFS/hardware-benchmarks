The Hippodrome’s central spine featured three surviving monuments that outlasted its crumbling walls [PDF p. 51]. Greeks dedicated the serpent column at Delphi in 479 B.C., though Phocians stole its golden tripod centuries earlier [PDF p. 51]. The Turks now call this open space the Atmeidan, preserving a dim memory of its ancient equestrian use [PDF p. 51]. Along the eastern wall stood the Milion milestone and various statues added by later emperors [PDF p. 51]. Constantine repurposed an Apollo statue from Hierapolis into his own image by replacing its head with imperial features [PDF p. 51]. This act mirrored papal alterations of Aurelius’s statue in Rome, according to the author’s comparison [PDF p. 53]. North of the Hippodrome stood Constantine’s original Hagia Sophia, a humble basilica that burned down twice before leaving no trace [PDF p. 53]. A wooden gallery connected this church directly to the palace Royal Gate for private imperial worship [PDF p. 53]. These structures formed the city’s heart, where most significant historical events occurred within the Augustaeum cluster [PDF p. 53]. Constantine chose Byzantium as his capital around A.D. 328 or 329, though exact dates remain uncertain [PDF p. 54]. He consecrated the new city on May 11, 330, with Christian bishops blessing the partially completed palace [PDF p. 55]. The author notes that Constantine remained unbaptized yet insisted the city be Christian from its inception [PDF p. 55]. Pagan temples survived only as artistic relics in baths and senate houses, devoid of worship functions [PDF p. 55]. To populate the capital, Constantine invited senators and offered privileges to artisans and merchants [PDF p. 55]. He transferred Egypt’s wheat tribute from Rome to Constantinople, leaving Carthage’s grain for the old city [PDF p. 56]. An edict named the city New Rome, but the public persisted in calling it Constantinople [PDF p. 56].

Constantine died peacefully on May 22, A.D. 337, receiving baptism on his deathbed after lingering on Christianity’s verge [PDF p. 57]. His will divided the realm among sons and nephews, but murders concentrated power under Constantius II [PDF p. 57]. The author describes Constantius as capable yet gloomy, initiating the first persecution of Christians by Christians [PDF p. 57]. Julian succeeded him briefly, attempting to restore pagan worship despite his cultured reputation [PDF p. 58]. Both emperors feared Persian threats in the east, ignoring dangers brewing from the north [PDF p. 58]. The Goths had raided Balkan provinces for a century before Constantine defeated them between A.D. 328 and 332 [PDF p. 58]. For forty years they settled peacefully, trading with Romans and adopting Christianity under Bishop Ulfilas’s guidance [PDF p. 60]. The author argues that this period of peace made the empire vulnerable to sudden northern shocks [PDF p. 61].

Around A.D. 372, Huns from beyond the Don burst into Gothic lands, crushing the Ostrogoths first [PDF p. 61]. A contemporary source describes these invaders as shapeless and ferocious nomads skilled in riding and archery [PDF p. 61]. The Visigoths under Fritigern fled to the Danube, fearing slavery more than their eastern kin did [PDF p. 62]. They begged Emperor Valens for permission to cross into Roman territory with over two hundred thousand fighters [PDF p. 62]. Valens, described as stupid and avaricious, agreed if they surrendered arms and hostages [PDF p. 63]. Governor Lupicinus accepted bribes to let Goths keep weapons while starving them through extortionate food prices [PDF p. 63]. This desperation led to an affray at Marcianopolis where Fritigern escaped a planned assassination attempt [PDF p. 64]. The resulting war saw initial Roman successes, including the indecisive battle at Ad Salices near Kustendje [PDF p. 64].

In A.D. 378, Valens marched against the Goths with sixty thousand men near Adrianople [PDF p. 65]. The author emphasizes that Gothic cavalry innovations from South Russia changed warfare dynamics significantly [PDF p. 65]. Roman infantry relied on traditional discipline, expecting victory against barbarian forces despite new threats [PDF p. 65]. Valens attacked the Gothic laager prematurely while their main cavalry was away foraging nearby [PDF p. 66]. The returning Gothic horsemen charged the Roman flank, trampling imperial squadrons and breaking infantry lines [PDF p. 66].
