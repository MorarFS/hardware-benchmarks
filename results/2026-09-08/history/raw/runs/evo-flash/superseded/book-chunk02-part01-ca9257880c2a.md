The Hippodrome’s central spine featured three surviving monuments that outlasted the surrounding walls [PDF p. 51]. The first was a golden tripod dedicated at Delphi in 479 B.C. after the Persian defeat at Plataea [PDF p. 51]. Phocians had stolen its gold six centuries prior, yet inscriptions on its base remained visible to archaeologists [PDF p. 51]. A square bronze column of more modern work stood nearby, contrasting with the ancient artifacts [PDF p. 51]. The Turks now call this open space the Atmeidan, remembering its equestrian past in name only [PDF p. 51]. Along the eastern wall stood chapels and statues, including the Milion milestone at the Augustaeum’s edge [PDF p. 51]. Constantine contributed a porphyry column topped by a bronze Apollo from Hierapolis [PDF p. 51]. The emperor replaced the god’s head with his own features to create an imperial image [PDF p. 51]. This act mirrored later papal changes in Rome, where Aurelius became St. Peter on the Corso column [PDF p. 53]. North of the Hippodrome stood Constantine’s original church of Hagia Sophia [PDF p. 53]. It was a humble basilica, not the famous domed structure that exists today [PDF p. 53]. Fires in the fifth and sixth centuries erased all traces of its original character [PDF p. 53]. A wooden gallery connected the church’s west door to the palace’s Royal Gate [PDF p. 53]. This passage allowed the emperor to attend services without crossing the Chalcoprateia street [PDF p. 53]. The author compares this elevated walkway to the corridor linking Florence’s Pitti and Uffizi palaces [PDF p. 53]. These buildings formed Constantinople’s heart, hosting most significant historical events [PDF p. 53]. Other notable structures included the Church of the Holy Apostles and the Golden Gate [PDF p. 54]. An equestrian statue of Constantine stood near the praetorian prefect’s palace until the Middle Ages [PDF p. 54].

Constantine chose Byzantium as his capital around A.D. 328 or 329 [PDF p. 54]. He consecrated the city on May 11, 330, with Christian bishops blessing the palace [PDF p. 55]. Although unbaptized, Constantine ensured the new city was Christian from its inception [PDF p. 55]. Pagan temples survived only as artistic relics in baths and senate houses [PDF p. 55]. The emperor attracted senators and wealthy proprietors by offering seats in his new senate [PDF p. 55]. Artisans received special privileges, while merchants flocked to the growing commercial hub [PDF p. 55]. Free corn distributions transferred from Rome’s Egyptian tribute to Constantinople’s populace [PDF p. 55]. An edict named the city New Rome, though people preferred calling it Constantinople [PDF p. 56]. Constantine died in A.D. 337 after receiving baptism on his deathbed [PDF p. 57]. His realm passed to sons and nephews, but murders concentrated power under Constantius II [PDF p. 57]. The author describes Constantius as able yet gloomy and suspicious of ecclesiastical matters [PDF p. 57]. Julian succeeded him briefly, attempting unsuccessfully to restore pagan worship [PDF p. 58]. Both emperors feared Persian threats on the eastern frontier more than northern dangers [PDF p. 58].

The Goths had raided Balkan provinces throughout the third century before being pushed back [PDF p. 58]. Constantine defeated them between A.D. 328 and 332, forcing their king to surrender hostages [PDF p. 58]. For forty years, they settled as farmers and traders along the Danube’s northern bank [PDF p. 60]. Many converted to Christianity under Bishop Ulfilas, who translated the Bible into Gothic [PDF p. 60]. The nation split into Visigoths in the south and Ostrogoths in the north [PDF p. 60]. In A.D. 372, Huns from beyond the Don burst westward, crushing the Alans first [PDF p. 61]. Gothic historians describe these invaders as shapeless, fierce riders with barely human forms [PDF p. 61]. The Ostrogoths fell to vassalage, while Visigoths fled south toward the Danube [PDF p. 61]. Duke Fritigern led his people to beg Emperor Valens for permission to cross [PDF p. 62]. A contemporary account notes over two hundred thousand fighting men pleaded on the riverbank [PDF p. 62]. Valens, described as stupid and avaricious, ruled the East from Constantinople [PDF p. 62].
