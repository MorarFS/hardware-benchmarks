The excerpt comprises the front matter of Frederick Douglass’s 1845 autobiography, *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave*, published in Boston by the Anti-Slavery Office. The text includes a preface by William Lloyd Garrison and a letter from Wendell Phillips, both dated May and April 1845, respectively, which serve to authenticate Douglass’s account and advocate for abolition.

William Lloyd Garrison’s preface begins with his personal recollection of meeting Douglass in August 1841 at an anti-slavery convention in Nantucket. Garrison describes Douglass as a stranger to the attendees, having recently escaped slavery from Maryland while residing in New Bedford. Garrison emphasizes the significance of this encounter, portraying it as fortunate for the cause of emancipation and universal liberty. He recounts Douglass’s first speech at the convention with vivid detail, noting the speaker’s commanding physical presence, natural eloquence, and profound emotional impact on the audience. Garrison recalls his own intense reaction, stating he had never hated slavery more intensely than in that moment upon witnessing a man of such high moral and intellectual capacity reduced to "chattel personal" by law.

Garrison describes how a friend from New Bedford persuaded Douglass to speak despite his initial hesitancy and embarrassment. After Douglass apologized for his lack of formal education, citing slavery as a poor school for the intellect, he narrated his history with noble thoughts that Garrison compared favorably to Patrick Henry’s revolutionary eloquence. Garrison then appealed to the audience to protect Douglass from recapture under any law or constitution. The crowd responded unanimously and thunderously with "NO!" to returning him to slavery and "YES!" to protecting him as a resident of Massachusetts.

Following this event, Garrison and John A. Collins, General Agent of the Massachusetts Anti-Slavery Society, encouraged Douglass to become a lecturing agent. Although Douglass initially expressed diffidence, fearing he might do more harm than good, he consented to try. Garrison asserts that Douglass’s subsequent career has exceeded expectations, praising his gentleness, manliness, and rhetorical skills in pathos, wit, and reasoning. Garrison argues that Douglass’s success disproves notions of natural inferiority among African Americans, noting that Charles Lenox Remond similarly represents the free colored population with distinction.

To counter skepticism regarding slave cruelty, Garrison cites Daniel O’Connell, who spoke in Dublin on March 31, 1845, about an American sailor enslaved in Africa for three years who lost his reasoning powers and language, illustrating slavery’s brutalizing effect on any human being. Garrison affirms that Douglass wrote the Narrative himself, calling it highly creditable given his limited opportunities for education. He insists the text is essentially true, containing no malice or exaggeration, and serves as a fair specimen of Maryland slave treatment, which he notes was considered better than in deeper Southern states.

Garrison highlights specific passages, such as Douglass’s soliloquy on the banks of the Chesapeake Bay, as particularly powerful. He addresses skeptics who deny the severity of slavery by pointing to legal injustices: slaves cannot testify against whites, leaving them without legal protection. To support this, Garrison cites the *Baltimore American* (March 17, 1845), which reported a case where a young man named Matthews shot a slave for disobeying an order and remained unmolested. Garrison concludes his preface by condemning religious slaveholders as "man-stealers" and urging readers to adopt the motto: "NO COMPROMISE WITH SLAVERY! NO UNION WITH SLAVEHOLDERS!"

Wendell Phillips’s letter, dated April 22, 1845, follows. He uses the fable of "The Man and the Lion" to celebrate that "lions write history," meaning slaves are now telling their own stories rather than being misrepresented by masters. Phillips expresses satisfaction that Douglass reveals how early enslaved people recognize their rights and the spiritual death caused by slavery, not just physical suffering. He notes the value of Douglass’s perspective coming from Maryland, where slavery is often portrayed as milder, allowing readers to imagine the worse conditions further south. Phillips vouches for Douglass’s truthfulness based on long acquaintance, asserting that the Narrative offers a fair, balanced portrait that acknowledges occasional individual kindness within the deadly system while contrasting the "twilight of rights" at the North with the total deprivation in the South.
