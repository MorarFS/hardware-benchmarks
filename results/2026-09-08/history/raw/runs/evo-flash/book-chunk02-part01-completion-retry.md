The Hippodrome’s central spine featured three surviving monuments that outlasted the surrounding walls [PDF p. 51]. The first was a golden tripod dedicated at Delphi in 479 B.C. after the Persian defeat at Plataea [PDF p. 51]. Phocians had stolen its gold six centuries prior, yet inscriptions on its base remained visible to archaeologists [PDF p. 51]. A square bronze column of more modern work stood nearby, contrasting with the ancient artifacts [PDF p. 51]. The Turks now call this open space the Atmeidan, remembering its equestrian past in name only [PDF p. 51]. Along the eastern wall stood chapels and statues, including the Milion milestone of the empire [PDF p. 51]. Constantine contributed a porphyry column topped by a bronze Apollo from Hierapolis [PDF p. 51]. He altered this statue into an imperial image by replacing its head with his own features [PDF p. 51]. This practice mirrored Roman papal actions, where Aurelius was converted into St. Peter on the Corso [PDF p. 53]. North of the Hippodrome stood Constantine’s original church dedicated to Divine Wisdom [PDF p. 53]. This earlier basilica burned twice and left no trace of its initial architectural character [PDF p. 53]. A wooden gallery connected St. Sophia’s west door to the palace Royal Gate [PDF p. 53]. This passage allowed the emperor to attend services without crossing the busy Chalcoprateia street [PDF p. 53]. The author compares this elevated walkway to the corridor linking Florence’s Pitti and Uffizi palaces [PDF p. 53]. These structures formed the city’s heart, hosting most significant historical events [PDF p. 53]. Other notable buildings included the Church of the Holy Apostles and public granaries along the quays [PDF p. 53]. An equestrian statue of Constantine near the praetorian prefect’s palace became a medieval legend site [PDF p. 54].

Constantine chose Byzantium as his capital around A.D. 328 or 329, though exact dates remain uncertain [PDF p. 54]. He consecrated the city on May 11, 330, with Christian bishops blessing the palace and St. Sophia [PDF p. 55]. Although unbaptized, Constantine ensured the new capital was explicitly Christian from its inception [PDF p. 55]. Pagan temples survived only as artistic relics in baths and senate houses, not for worship [PDF p. 55]. To populate the city, he invited senators and wealthy provincials to establish residences there [PDF p. 55]. Artisans received special privileges, while merchants flocked to the new commercial hub [PDF p. 55]. The transfer of Egyptian wheat tribute provided free corn, a powerful but demoralizing incentive for immigration [PDF p. 55]. An edict in 330 named the city New Rome, though people preferred calling it Constantinople [PDF p. 56].

Constantine died peacefully on May 22, A.D. 337, receiving baptism on his deathbed [PDF p. 57]. His realm was divided among relatives until Constantius II consolidated power through murder and war [PDF p. 57]. Constantius II focused on ecclesiastical disputes rather than external threats, initiating Christian persecution of Christians [PDF p. 57]. His successor Julian attempted to restore paganism but failed due to his misguided zeal [PDF p. 58]. Both emperors feared Persian aggression in the east, overlooking dangers from the north [PDF p. 58]. The Goths had been subdued by Constantine between A.D. 328 and 332, forcing them across the Danube [PDF p. 58]. For forty years they settled peacefully, trading with Romans and adopting Christianity under Bishop Ulfilas [PDF p. 60]. Ulfilas translated the Bible into Gothic, creating a vital linguistic relic of Teutonic tongues [PDF p. 60]. The Goths were divided into Visigoths in the south and Ostrogoths in the north [PDF p. 60].

Around A.D. 372, Huns from beyond the Don and Volga invaded Gothic lands [PDF p. 61]. They crushed the Ostrogoths, who became vassals except for a remnant fleeing southward [PDF p. 61]. The Visigoths, led by Duke Fritigern, retreated to the Danube’s southern bank in despair [PDF p. 61]. A contemporary writer describes over two hundred thousand fighting men pleading for Roman protection [PDF p. 62]. Emperor Valens, ruling the East from Constantinople, faced a difficult choice regarding these refugees [PDF p. 62]. He allowed them to cross if they surrendered arms and provided hostages [PDF p. 63]. Roman officials accepted bribes to let Goths keep weapons, violating imperial orders [PDF p. 63]. Governor Lupicinus hoarded food supplies, forcing starving Goths to sell children for bread [PDF p. 63]. An affray at Marcianopolis led Lupicinus to attempt murdering Fritigern during a banquet [PDF p. 64]. The Goths escaped and launched raids across the Balkans, joined by runaway slaves [PDF p. 64]. Initial Roman resistance held them at bay, though battles like Ad Salices proved indecisive [PDF p. 64].
