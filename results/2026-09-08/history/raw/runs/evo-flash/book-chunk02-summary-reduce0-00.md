The Hippodrome’s central spine featured three surviving monuments that outlasted the surrounding walls [PDF p. 51]. A golden tripod dedicated at Delphi in 479 B.C. stood there after the Persian defeat at Plataea [PDF p. 51]. Phocians had stolen its gold six centuries prior, yet base inscriptions remained visible to archaeologists [PDF p. 51]. Nearby stood a square bronze column of more modern work, contrasting with ancient artifacts [PDF p. 51]. Turks now call this open space the Atmeidan, remembering its equestrian past in name only [PDF p. 51]. Along the eastern wall stood chapels and statues, including the Milion milestone of the empire [PDF p. 51]. Constantine contributed a porphyry column topped by a bronze Apollo from Hierapolis to this area [PDF p. 51]. He altered this statue into an imperial image by replacing its head with his own features [PDF p. 51]. This practice mirrored Roman papal actions, where Aurelius was converted into St. Peter on the Corso [PDF p. 53]. North of the Hippodrome stood Constantine’s original church dedicated to Divine Wisdom [PDF p. 53]. This earlier basilica burned twice and left no trace of its initial architectural character [PDF p. 53]. A wooden gallery connected St. Sophia’s west door to the palace Royal Gate for imperial use [PDF p. 53]. This passage allowed the emperor to attend services without crossing the busy Chalcoprateia street [PDF p. 53]. The author compares this elevated walkway to the corridor linking Florence’s Pitti and Uffizi palaces [PDF p. 53]. These structures formed the city’s heart, hosting most significant historical events for centuries [PDF p. 53].

Constantine chose Byzantium as his capital around A.D. 328 or 329, though exact dates remain uncertain [PDF p. 54]. He consecrated the city on May 11, 330, with Christian bishops blessing the palace and St. Sophia [PDF p. 55]. Although unbaptized, Constantine ensured the new capital was explicitly Christian from its very inception [PDF p. 55]. Pagan temples survived only as artistic relics in baths and senate houses, not for worship [PDF p. 55]. To populate the city, he invited senators and wealthy provincials to establish residences there immediately [PDF p. 55]. Artisans received special privileges, while merchants flocked to the new commercial hub for opportunity [PDF p. 55]. The transfer of Egyptian wheat tribute provided free corn, a powerful but demoralizing incentive for immigration [PDF p. 55]. An edict in 330 named the city New Rome, though people preferred calling it Constantinople [PDF p. 56]. Constantine died peacefully on May 22, A.D. 337, receiving baptism on his deathbed shortly before passing [PDF p. 57]. His realm was divided among relatives until Constantius II consolidated power through murder and war [PDF p. 57]. Constantius II focused on ecclesiastical disputes rather than external threats, initiating Christian persecution of Christians [PDF p. 57]. His successor Julian attempted to restore paganism but failed due to his misguided zeal for old gods [PDF p. 58]. Both emperors feared Persian aggression in the east, overlooking dangers emerging from the northern frontiers [PDF p. 58]. The Goths had been subdued by Constantine between A.D. 328 and 332, forcing them across the Danube [PDF p. 58]. For forty years they settled peacefully, trading with Romans and adopting Christianity under Bishop Ulfilas [PDF p. 60]. Ulfilas translated the Bible into Gothic, creating a vital linguistic relic of Teutonic tongues for history [PDF p. 60]. The Goths were divided into Visigoths in the south and Ostrogoths in the north during this period [PDF p. 60].

Around A.D. 372, Huns from beyond the Don and Volga invaded Gothic lands with sudden force [PDF p. 61]. They crushed the Ostrogoths, who became vassals except for a remnant fleeing southward in panic [PDF p. 61]. The Visigoths, led by Duke Fritigern, retreated to the Danube’s southern bank in utter despair [PDF p. 61]. A contemporary writer describes over two hundred thousand fighting men pleading for Roman protection desperately [PDF p. 62]. Emperor Valens, ruling the East from Constantinople, faced a difficult choice regarding these desperate refugees [PDF p. 62]. He allowed them to cross if they surrendered arms and provided hostages to ensure compliance [PDF p. 63]. Roman officials accepted bribes to let Goths keep weapons, violating imperial orders with impunity [PDF p. 63].
