The final German threat to the Eastern Empire occurred during the reign of Zeno [PDF p. 88]. The Ostrogoths had previously submitted to the Huns ninety years earlier [PDF p. 88]. They regained independence after Attila’s death in A.D. 452 [PDF p. 88]. These Goths replaced the Huns as the primary danger along the Danube [PDF p. 88]. Most settled in Pannonia, bordering Eastern Roman districts [PDF p. 88]. Two chiefs named Theodoric raided Macedonia and Moesia for over twenty years [PDF p. 88]. Zeno attempted to divide them by offering titles and pensions [PDF p. 88]. This strategy failed because the two leaders united against him [PDF p. 88]. One Theodoric accused Rome of using Goths to destroy other Goths [PDF p. 89]. His speech convinced his rival that Roman aid was illusory [PDF p. 89]. They reconciled and fought Zeno together starting in A.D. 479 [PDF p. 89]. Theodoric the One-Eyed died accidentally two years later [PDF p. 89]. His horse threw him against a spear fixed at his tent door [PDF p. 89]. The surviving Theodoric continued to plague the empire until A.D. 488 [PDF p. 89].

Zeno devised a plan to remove this threat by offering Italy to Theodoric [PDF p. 90]. Odoacer had deposed Romulus Augustulus in A.D. 476 [PDF p. 89]. He refused to appoint a new puppet emperor for the West [PDF p. 89]. The Roman Senate asked Zeno to recognize Odoacer as their ruler [PDF p. 89]. Zeno instead advised them to acknowledge Julius Nepos as emperor [PDF p. 90]. Odoacer rejected this advice and proclaimed himself king in Italy [PDF p. 90]. He still nominally recognized the Constantinopolitan emperor as his suzerain [PDF p. 90]. Theodoric accepted Zeno’s offer to conquer Italy from Odoacer [PDF p. 90]. He received the titles of patrician and consul before departing [PDF p. 90]. The Goths defeated Odoacer and established a kingdom at Ravenna [PDF p. 90]. Theodoric professed loyalty to the Eastern emperor throughout his reign [PDF p. 90]. This conquest theoretically reunited the East and West under one ruler [PDF p. 90]. However, Zeno’s actual power in the Western realm remained purely nominal [PDF p. 90].

The Emperor Anastasius died in A.D. 518 at age eighty-eight [PDF p. 91]. Justinus, his body-guard commander, succeeded him with Senate and army support [PDF p. 91]. Justinus was an uncultured soldier who could barely sign his name [PDF p. 91]. He adopted his nephew Justinian as his heir [PDF p. 91]. Justinian was educated in law, finance, theology, and architecture [PDF p. 92]. He showed little personal interest in military matters [PDF p. 92]. In A.D. 526, he announced his intention to marry the actress Theodora [PDF p. 92]. This union scandalized respectable society but pleased gossipmongers [PDF p. 92]. The author notes that libellous accounts of her early life are likely exaggerated [PDF p. 92]. Roman law forbade senators from marrying actresses, so Justinian repealed it [PDF p. 93]. His mother and uncle opposed the marriage but eventually relented [PDF p. 93]. Theodora was considered the most beautiful woman of her age [PDF p. 93]. She served as a trusted councillor and saved her husband’s throne once [PDF p. 93].

Justinus died in A.D. 527, leaving Justinian sole emperor for thirty-eight years [PDF p. 96]. The empire was prosperous when he assumed full power [PDF p. 96]. Anastasius had left a treasury containing over three hundred thousand pounds of gold [PDF p. 97]. The army consisted largely of native subjects rather than barbarians [PDF p. 97]. Heavy cavalry replaced infantry as the primary military force [PDF p. 98]. Foreign auxiliaries like Huns and Herules still served in large numbers [PDF p. 98]. The financial system remained weak despite the available gold reserves [PDF p. 98]. Tax farming and arbitrary customs dues harmed trade significantly [PDF p. 98]. Justinian’s minister John of Cappadocia raised revenue through harsh extortion [PDF p. 98]. This policy impoverished provinces permanently for future generations [PDF p. 99].
