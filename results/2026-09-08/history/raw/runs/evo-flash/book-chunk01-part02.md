The narrative begins with the legendary founding of Byzantium by Dorian Greeks from Megara [PDF p. 27]. These settlers arrived two thousand five hundred and fifty-eight years prior to the author's writing [PDF p. 27]. They established a stockade on a headland between the Golden Horn and the sea [PDF p. 27]. The text describes their motivation as commercial expansion toward the Black Sea [PDF p. 28]. Legends of the Golden Fleece and Amazons drew them eastward [PDF p. 28]. They did not find these mythical lands but discovered valuable trade routes [PDF p. 28]. The Greeks renamed the inhospitable sea to reflect its friendly ports [PDF p. 29]. Byzantium was founded seventeen years after Chalcedon on the opposite shore [PDF p. 29]. An oracle advised building over against the city of the blind [PDF p. 29]. This referred to the Chalcedonians who chose a less suitable site [PDF p. 29]. The location offered superior military and commercial advantages [PDF p. 29]. It served as a fortress for Europe or Asia depending on perspective [PDF p. 30]. The city was naturally impregnable due to water defenses and walls [PDF p. 30]. Early history shows it never fell by storm but only through famine or treachery [PDF p. 30]. It controlled Black Sea trade completely, taxing passing vessels [PDF p. 30]. Local fisheries and Thracian trade further enriched the settlement [PDF p. 31]. The city badge featured a tunny fish and an ox [PDF p. 31]. Byzantium maintained independence for three hundred years except for Persian occupation [PDF p. 31]. Darius built a bridge of boats near the site during his campaigns [PDF p. 31]. During the Ionic Revolt, the rebel Histiaeus held the city briefly [PDF p. 31]. He imposed heavy tolls on ships passing through the strait [PDF p. 31]. Greek forces finally liberated Byzantium after the battles of Salamis and Mycale [PDF p. 31]. The siege ended in B.C. 479 when the garrison starved out [PDF p. 32]. This event marked the beginning of Athenian naval dominance in the region [PDF p. 32].

Byzantium twice rebelled against Athens during the fifth century B.C. [PDF p. 32]. It surrendered voluntarily in 439 B.C. and fell by treachery in 408 B.C. [PDF p. 32]. The Athenians imposed heavy indemnities but did not destroy the city [PDF p. 32]. Commercial profits quickly repaired these war losses [PDF p. 32]. Internal history remains obscure, though they used iron for small change [PDF p. 32]. Their alphabet contained a unique letter shape that confused other Greeks [PDF p. 32]. The author notes ancient chroniclers described the Byzantines as luxurious and gluttonous [PDF p. 33]. One anecdote claims militia members struck work during a siege for food [PDF p. 33]. A comic writer suggested their bodies became gelatinous from eating tunny fish [PDF p. 33]. The author dismisses these tales as neighborly envy of Byzantine prosperity [PDF p. 33]. Philip of Macedon failed to capture the city in B.C. 339 [PDF p. 33]. A sudden light revealed his night escalade, which citizens viewed as divine aid [PDF p. 33]. They adopted the crescent and star badge to commemorate this event [PDF p. 33]. This symbol later became associated with Ottoman rulers [PDF p. 33]. Byzantium eventually submitted to Alexander the Great after Philip’s death [PDF p. 34]. It passed through the hands of Demetrius and Lysimachus before regaining freedom [PDF p. 34]. The city allied early with Rome, securing favorable terms as a free confederate state [PDF p. 34]. Emperor Vespasian stripped these privileges in A.D. 71 [PDF p. 34]. Byzantium flourished under the Pax Romana despite losing political autonomy [PDF p. 35].

The golden age ended with the rise of military emperors after Commodus [PDF p. 35]. Byzantium lay between the rival camps of Pescennius Niger and Severus [PDF p. 35]. The city resisted Severus for two years before falling in A.D. 196 [PDF p. 35]. Severus executed magistrates and demolished the massive walls in punishment [PDF p. 35]. Caracalla later restored self-governance, but Gothic incursions continued to disrupt trade [PDF p. 36]. The city was sacked by Gallienus’s soldiers in A.D. 263 [PDF p. 36].
