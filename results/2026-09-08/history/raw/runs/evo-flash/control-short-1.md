Historians operate within a complex ecosystem of evidence where the distinction between an author’s direct account, quoted testimony, and historical inference is not merely semantic but fundamental to establishing credibility and narrative accuracy. Understanding these categories allows scholars to assess bias, verify facts, and construct interpretations that remain faithful to the source material while acknowledging the gaps inherent in historical record-keeping.

An **author’s account** refers to the direct narrative or analytical voice of the historian writing the text. This is the layer where the historian synthesizes information, provides context, and argues a thesis. For example, if a historian writes, "The economic collapse of 1929 was exacerbated by speculative trading practices," this is an authorial account. It represents the historian’s interpretation of available data. While authoritative, it is inherently subjective because it reflects the historian’s selection of facts and analytical framework. Readers must recognize that this voice is constructing a story from fragments, not simply reporting raw reality.

It consists of direct speech or written statements from individuals who witnessed events or participated in them.
