Constantine evaluated several potential sites for his new imperial capital before selecting Byzantium [PDF p. 43]. Nicomedia offered an eligible location and had previously served as an imperial residence [PDF p. 43]. However, Constantine wished to avoid eclipsing the memory of Diocletian in that city [PDF p. 43]. Christians associated Nicomedia with the persecutions under Diocletian and Galerius [PDF p. 43]. Ilium possessed only legendary glories and lacked a suitable harbor for naval command [PDF p. 43]. Byzantium was thoroughly familiar to Constantine from his military campaigns in the region [PDF p. 43]. The emperor recognized its significant military advantages during his months stationed nearby [PDF p. 43]. Despite this logic, the Roman world reacted with surprise to the choice of a provincial fortress [PDF p. 43].

Once decided, Constantine marked the city limits through solemn processions in traditional Roman style [PDF p. 44]. A later legend describes him tracing fortifications with his spear while walking westward [PDF p. 44]. His attendants questioned the vast scale of the plan as he moved further from old gates [PDF p. 44]. Constantine claimed an invisible guide directed his steps until the boundary was complete [PDF p. 44]. He included all seven hills on the peninsula between the Propontis and Golden Horn [PDF p. 44]. The site of his former siege tent became the marketplace for the new foundation [PDF p. 44]. There he erected the Milion, a golden milestone measuring distances across the eastern world [PDF p. 45]. This structure housed statues of Constantine and his mother Helena within a temple-like building [PDF p. 45].

The imperial palace occupied the south-eastern part of old Byzantium on cleared land [PDF p. 45]. A wall separated this vast precinct from the rest of the city near the Lighthouse [PDF p. 45]. The Augustaeum served as a central forum paved with marble and surrounded by buildings [PDF p. 45]. Three structures connected by colonnades stood between the palace and this open space [PDF p. 45]. The Baths of Zeuxippus were enlarged using ancient statues from Greece and Asia [PDF p. 47]. These included masterpieces like the Athene of Lyndus and the Zeus of Dodona [PDF p. 47]. Constantine also established a Senate House modeled on that of Old Rome [PDF p. 47]. He attracted senatorial families eastward through gifts of pensions and houses [PDF p. 47]. The Patriarch’s Palace lay nearby but remained overshadowed by the imperial residence [PDF p. 48]. This proximity prevented the bishop from gaining independent spiritual authority like his Roman counterpart [PDF p. 48].

The Hippodrome dominated the western side of the Augustaeum with its massive dimensions [PDF p. 48]. Chariot races between the Blues and Greens factions became a central feature of civic life [PDF p. 48]. This rivalry extended beyond sports into religious and political conflicts throughout history [PDF p. 48]. The emperor’s box, or Kathisma, allowed frequent public appearances from his throne [PDF p. 50]. Notable events included the crowning of rebel Hypatius by an impromptu mob [PDF p. 50]. Justinian II later sat there with defeated rivals bound beneath his footstool [PDF p. 50]. The central spina displayed monuments including an Egyptian obelisk and a brazen serpent [PDF p. 50].

Specific details about the Senate House are missing because it was destroyed twice within a century [PDF p. 47]. The author interprets the physical layout as evidence of Constantine’s desire to control religious authority [PDF p. 48]. Uncertainty remains regarding the exact extent of private property cleared for the palace grounds [PDF p. 45].
