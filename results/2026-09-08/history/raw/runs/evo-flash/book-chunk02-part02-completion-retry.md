The unwarlike Emperor Valens entered the field in person during the following year. He brought great reinforcements from Asia Minor to aid his struggling forces [PDF p. 65]. His nephew Gratian also marched through Pannonia to assist the Lower Danube regions [PDF p. 65]. This personal intervention by Valens resulted in a catastrophic military disaster for Rome. The Goths forced the Balkan line and approached Adrianople in three hundred seventy-eight A.D. [PDF p. 65]

Valens attacked with an army of sixty thousand men near the Gothic encampment. Contemporaries expected victory because Roman infantry still held a reputation for invincibility [PDF p. 65]. However, the Goths had adopted cavalry tactics while dwelling in South Russia and Roumania. They considered fighting on horseback more honorable than engaging on foot during this period [PDF p. 65]

A great body of Gothic horsemen charged the Roman flank unexpectedly during battle. These riders had been foraging at a distance but rushed to the field upon hearing news [PDF p. 66]. The Roman left wing collapsed under the tremendous impact of the mounted assault. Legions and cohorts were pushed together in hopeless confusion by the overwhelming pressure [PDF p. 66]

Roman cavalry abandoned the infantry, leaving them unable to deploy or flee effectively. Soldiers could not raise their arms due to the dense packing of the massed troops. Forty thousand men fell before survivors broke out in a headlong flight from the field [PDF p. 67]. The Emperor and thirty-five commanders died on the battlefield during this fearful defeat [PDF p. 67]

The historian Ammianus Marcellinus compared this slaughter to the ancient disaster at Cannae. The army of the East was almost annihilated and never reorganized on old lines [PDF p. 67]. Victorious Goths subsequently attacked Constantinople but failed due to its vast walls and riches. They abandoned their siege machines and retreated back into Thrace after seeing the defenses [PDF p. 67]

Theodosius succeeded Valens and repaired the disaster through caution and courage combined. He destroyed marauding bands while avoiding direct conflict with the main Gothic body. Peace followed the death of Fritigern, as Theodosius negotiated with his successor Athanarich [PDF p. 68]. The Emperor granted land in Thrace and enlisted Gothic chiefs into his armies [PDF p. 68]

Within ten years, forty thousand Teutonic horsemen served under higher pay than Romans. These auxiliaries won victories for Theodosius against rebel forces in the West later on [PDF p. 69]. Yet this policy placed imperial command effectively in barbarian hands according to the author. It relied solely on personal loyalty and the lingering prestige of Roman culture [PDF p. 70]

The Gothic historian Jornandes recorded King Athanarich’s awe at Constantinople’s grandeur. The king admired the walls, fleets, and disciplined soldiery within the imperial city [PDF p. 70]. This impression did not last long after Theodosius died in three hundred ninety-five A.D. His weak sons Arcadius and Honorius inherited a dangerous political situation [PDF p. 70]

The empire suffered from centralized bureaucracy that suppressed local patriotism and rights. Civil populations were viewed merely as tax sources by the administrative caste officials. Landowners faced restrictions on moving or enlisting without leaving heirs to pay rates [PDF p. 71]. A professional army emerged with no sympathy for the unarmed provincial civilians it protected [PDF p. 72]

Theodosius had swamped native military elements with wholesale enlistment of Gothic war-bands. He handed chief posts to Teutons who were often alien in blood and loyalty. The author argues this created a dangerous guardian class over a timid population [PDF p. 72]

After Theodosius died, factions fought between German and Roman parties at both courts. Stilicho dominated the West as commander-in-chief for the young Emperor Honorius. In the East, Arcadius was weak and controlled by ministers like Rufinus initially [PDF p. 73]. A Gothic captain named Gainas murdered Rufinus before compelling further appointments for himself [PDF p. 73]

Alaric led a Visigothic revolt citing unpaid arrears to the auxiliaries as pretext. He raided from the Danube to Greece while Stilicho blockaded him in Arcadia. The Teutonic minister allowed Alaric to escape, leading to peace and land grants [PDF p. 74]

Alaric ruled parts of the Balkans with undisputed sway for five years thereafter. A German kingdom seemed likely to establish itself permanently south of the Danube then. However, Alaric invaded Italy in four hundred one instead of attacking Constantinople again [PDF p. 75]. Stilicho fought hard but was executed by Honorius on charges of treason shortly after [PDF p. 75]

With Stilicho gone, Alaric moved the Visigoths into Italy to plunder at will. They left the Balkan Peninsula and entered the history of Spain eventually. Meanwhile, Gainas fell out with the eunuch Eutropius in Constantinople’s court politics. He instigated an insurrection under Tribigild to remove his rival from power [PDF p. 76]

Eutropius fled to St. Sophia where Patriarch John Chrysostom protected him temporarily. The preacher used the fallen minister as a sermon illustration on vanity and fate. Arcadius banished Eutropius, but Gainas had him returned and beheaded despite promises [PDF p. 76]

Gainas brought his army to Constantinople to overawe the emperor completely. The author notes that a sack of the city appeared likely at this moment. The text ends abruptly before describing the final outcome of this specific threat. It leaves unresolved how the immediate danger to the capital was ultimately managed [PDF p. 76]
