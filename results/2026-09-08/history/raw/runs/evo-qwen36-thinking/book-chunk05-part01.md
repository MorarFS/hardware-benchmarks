Justinian then executed his former rivals while inflicting widespread terror upon citizens [PDF p. 205]. His brutal reign terminated in 711 when General Philippicus seized power [PDF p. 205]. Philippicus murdered the former emperor’s young son to end the Heraclian dynasty [PDF p. 205]. Six years of administrative collapse followed before Leo the Isaurian restored order in 717 [PDF p. 207].

Leo confronted a massive Saracen expedition that besieged the imperial capital [PDF p. 211]. The siege lasted from 717 to 718 [PDF p. 211]. Byzantine fire ships and harsh winter conditions devastated the invading army [PDF p. 212]. The author argues that Leo deserved greater historical recognition than Charles Martel achieved elsewhere [PDF p. 214]. Following this triumph, Leo initiated an aggressive campaign against religious icon veneration [PDF p. 215]. The author attributes this movement to declining classical education and growing Islamic influence [PDF p. 216]. Leo faced violent popular resistance and provincial revolts, yet he maintained army support [PDF p. 219]. His son Constantine V escalated the persecution by forcibly suppressing monastic communities [PDF p. 223]. Religious tensions culminated when Empress Irene blinded her son Constantine VI to seize control [PDF p. 225]. Irene ruled until her own deposition by treasurer Nicephorus in 802 [PDF p. 225]. Nicephorus died during a Bulgarian campaign, triggering a series of short military reigns [PDF p. 230]. These conflicts eventually placed Basil the Macedonian on the throne in 867 [PDF p. 230]. Basil codified imperial law and persecuted heretical groups before losing Sicily permanently [PDF p. 240].
