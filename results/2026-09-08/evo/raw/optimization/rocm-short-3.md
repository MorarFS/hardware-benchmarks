Following the death of Theodora, Antonina and her husband lost imperial favor, experiencing a misfortune in their declining years similar to that of the Marlboroughs [p. 101]. 

The year 532, which marked the end of the Persian War, also saw the rise of a significant danger to the Emperor’s power: the "Blues" and "Greens." These factions of the Byzantine Circus had grown increasingly powerful throughout the fifth century, interfering in politics and religious controversies [p. 101]. By 530, being a "Green" implied being a Monophysite and a partisan of the late Emperor Anastasius’s house, whereas the "Blues" posed as orthodox and as partisans of the house of Justinus [p. 101]. The author notes that while these groups had evolved into political parties, they retained "traces of their low sporting origin," characterized by rougher elements prone to riot and mischief [p. 101].

In January 532, serious rioting broke out. Although Justinian typically favored the Blue faction, he ordered the execution of leaders from both sides [p. 101]. After four rioters were beheaded before a monastery, an attempt to hang three others failed twice due to the bungling of the executioners and the menaces of the mob; the criminals eventually sought sanctuary in a monastery [p. 102].

This incident triggered six days of desperate rioting. The Blues and Greens united under the watchword *"Nika!"* ("conquer"), demanding the deposition of the unpopular finance minister, John of Cappadocia, and the city prefect, Eudemius [p. 102]. The author describes Justinian as "weak enough" to promise the dismissal of these officials, but the mob remained uncontrollable. The unrest was further fomented by partisans of the late emperor who called for Justinian’s deposition in favor of Hypatius, the nephew of Anastasius [p. 102]. Because the garrison had been sent to the Persian War, the city was nearly empty of troops, leaving the Emperor to rely on only 4,000 Imperial Guards, some German auxiliaries, and a regiment [p. 102].
