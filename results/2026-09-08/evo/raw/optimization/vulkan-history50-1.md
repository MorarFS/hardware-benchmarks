In his history of the Byzantine Empire, C.W.C. Oman seeks to move beyond the "depressing and monotonous" reputation established by historians like Gibbon, who characterized the empire's successors as "vicious weaklings" (PDF p. 13). Instead, Oman aims to highlight the empire's role in preserving learning and resisting the Saracens (PDF p. 13).

The narrative begins with the foundation of Byzantium by Greek colonists from Megara, who settled on the headland of the Golden Horn (PDF p. 27). The author notes that the city's strategic location made it both a formidable "frontier fortress" and a commercial powerhouse that commanded Black Sea trade (PDF p. 29-30). Throughout its early history, Byzantium maintained independence, surviving Persian rule and Athenian conquests (PDF p. 31-32). However, the city faced significant instability during the Roman era, including a brutal siege by the Emperor Severus in A.D. 196, which resulted in the slaughter of its magistrates and the destruction of its walls (PDF p. 35).

A pivotal turning point occurred when Constantine the Great selected Byzantium as the site for a new imperial capital (PDF p. 38). Oman describes Constantine as a "typical Roman" with "hard, cold, steady, unwearying energy" who recognized the need for a more accessible military and administrative center than Rome (PDF p. 40-42). The foundation of Constantinople was marked by grand scale; legend suggests Constantine traced the city's vast boundaries with his spear (PDF p. 43). The new capital featured a sophisticated topography, including the Augustaeum forum, the imperial palace, and the Great Hippodrome (PDF p. 45-46). The Hippodrome became a central site for social and political life, characterized by the intense and often dangerous rivalry between the "Blues" and the "Greens" (PDF p. 48-49).
