In his work *The Byzantine Empire*, C.W.C. Oman challenges the historical view, popularized by Edward Gibbon, that the East-Roman Empire was merely a period of "corrupt and decadent" weakness (PDF p. 13). Instead, Oman argues that Byzantium played a vital role in holding back the Saracens and preserving learning during the Dark Ages (PDF p. 13).

The history of the empire begins with the foundation of Byzantium by Greek colonists from Megara approximately 2,558 years before the author's time (PDF p. 27). The city’s strategic location on the Bosphorus made it an "almost impregnable" fortress and a commercial powerhouse that commanded Black Sea trade (PDF p. 30). After centuries of navigating Persian rule, Athenian conflicts, and Macedonian conquest, the city eventually formed a "free and confederate" alliance with Rome (PDF p. 33). However, this autonomy ended under Emperor Vespasian, when Byzantium was relegated to a provincial town (PDF p. 34).

The city suffered significant instability during the third-century military upheavals, including a brutal siege by Severus in A.D. 196 that resulted in the execution of its magistrates and the destruction of its walls (PDF p. 35). Despite these setbacks, the site's importance led to its revitalization. Following his victory over Licinius in A.D. 323, Constantine the Great selected Byzantium as the new imperial capital (PDF p. 38). 

Oman describes Constantine as a "typical Roman" with "hard, cold, steady, unwearying energy" who sought a more convenient administrative and military center than Rome (PDF p. 40-41). The resulting city, Constantinople, was designed with immense scale, featuring the Milion, a massive imperial palace, the Augustaeum forum, and the Hippodrome (PDF p. 44-48). The Hippodrome became a central site for social and political life, characterized by the intense and often dangerous rivalry between the "Blues" and the "Greens" (PDF p. 48).
