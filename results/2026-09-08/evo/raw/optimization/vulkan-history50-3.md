In his work *The Byzantine Empire*, C.W.C. Oman challenges the historical view, popularized by Edward Gibbon, that the East-Roman Empire was merely a "corrupt and decadent" successor to Rome (PDF p. 13). Instead, Oman argues that Byzantium played a vital role in preserving learning during the Dark Ages and resisting Saracen advances (PDF p. 13).

The history of the empire begins with the foundation of Byzantium by Greek colonists from Megara approximately 2,558 years before the author's writing (PDF p. 27). The city’s strategic location on the Bosphorus made it a formidable military fortress and a commercial powerhouse that commanded Black Sea trade (PDF p. 29-30). Despite periods of Persian rule and conflicts with Athens, the city maintained significant independence (PDF p. 31-32). After falling under Roman influence and eventually becoming a provincial town under Vespasian, the city's fortunes shifted during the era of military emperors (PDF p. 34-35).

A pivotal turning point occurred when Constantine the Great, seeking a more accessible and defensible administrative center than Rome, selected Byzantium as his new capital (PDF p. 41-43). Constantine established Constantinople with great energy, marking its boundaries with a massive scale that included the seven hills of the peninsula (PDF p. 43-44). The new city featured grand architecture, including the Augustaeum, the imperial palace, and the Senate House (PDF p. 45-47). A central feature of social and political life was the Hippodrome, where the intense rivalry between the "Blue" and "Green" factions often escalated into civil unrest and political sedition (PDF p. 48-49). The Hippodrome also served as a stage for imperial spectacle, including the crowning of rebels and the public humiliation of vanquished emperors (PDF p. 49-50).
