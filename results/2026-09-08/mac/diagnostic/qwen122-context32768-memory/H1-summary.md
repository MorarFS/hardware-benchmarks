The narrative begins by describing how the Huns, an enormous horde from beyond the Don and Volga, burst into lands north of the Euxine around A.D. 372 [PDF p. 61]. They almost exterminated the Alans and crushed the Ostrogoths, who were compelled to become vassals [PDF p. 61]. The Visigoths, under Duke Fritigern, retreated in dismay to the Danube, finding surrender to the Huns more dreadful than slavery to Romans because they were more civilized and Christian [PDF p. 61]. In A.D. 376, they begged Emperor Valens for permission to cross the river [PDF p. 62]. The author evaluates Valens as a stupid, cowardly, and avaricious prince who ruled the East while his brother held the West [PDF p. 62]. Valens eventually allowed the Goths to settle as subject-allies if they surrendered arms and gave hostages [PDF p. 63]. However, Roman officials accepted bribes to let the Goths keep their weapons, and Governor Lupicinus withheld food, selling it at exorbitant prices [PDF p. 63]. Driven by hunger, the Goths sold their children for slaves, leading to desperation and a violent affray that sparked a nationwide revolt [PDF p. 63]. Fighting continued until A.D. 378, when Valens attacked the Goths near Adrianople with an army of 60,000 men [PDF p. 65]. The author notes that the Goths had adopted cavalry tactics from the Ukraine, which proved decisive [PDF p. 65]. A Gothic cavalry charge overwhelmed the Roman flank, causing confusion and crushing the infantry [PDF p. 66]. Forty thousand men fell before the survivors fled, leaving the Emperor and many commanders dead [PDF p. 67]. The author calls this the most fearful defeat since Cannae, noting the Eastern army was never reorganized on old lines [PDF p. 67]. Although the Goths advanced to Constantinople, their courage failed before the vast walls, and they retreated [PDF p. 67]. Thus, Constantine's foresight in building the fortress-capital was vindicated forty years after his death [PDF p. 67].
