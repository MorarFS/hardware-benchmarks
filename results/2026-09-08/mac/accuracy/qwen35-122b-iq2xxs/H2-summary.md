The text begins by documenting the decay of the Latin tongue in the Eastern Roman Empire, noting that Ammianus Marcellinus wrote in the ruling language despite his Greek origins [PDF p. 169]. By the seventh century, this Roman element vanished as emperors adopted Greek titles and officials like Procopius became ignorant of Latin [PDF p. 169]. The author attributes this linguistic shift to the political division of the empire and the devastation of Latin-speaking Balkan provinces by Goths and Slavs [PDF p. 170]. Consequently, the last native Latin author was the African poet Flavius Corippus [PDF p. 170].

The narrative then transitions to the growing Christian influence on social life, observing that while Constantine's reign left administration largely pagan, Theodosius I later initiated state suppression of moral sins [PDF p. 171]. The author credits the monk Telemachus with ending gladiatorial games in A.D. 404 through his martyrdom [PDF p. 171]. Christianity also abolished infanticide and improved the status of slaves by recognizing their souls, leading to legislation by Justinian that protected mixed marriages and criminalized slave prostitution [PDF p. 173].

However, the author evaluates the rise of ascetic monasticism as a dangerous development that weakened the state by encouraging citizens to neglect civic duties [PDF p. 174]. This apathy led pagans to blame Christianity for the empire's collapse, prompting Orosius to defend the faith [PDF p. 176]. The text concludes by noting that while open paganism ended with Justinian closing the schools of Athens in 529, many educated men remained indifferent Christians who retained pagan lifestyles [PDF p. 176].
