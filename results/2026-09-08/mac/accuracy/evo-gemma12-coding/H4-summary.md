The author characterizes the final seventy-five years of the Byzantine Empire as a mere piece of local history rather than a significant thread in the history of Christendom [PDF p. 358]. The author argues that the empire ceased to function as a bulwark against the Ottomans after 1370 and that the Paleologi prolonged the empire through base subservience [PDF p. 358]. During the reign of John Paleologus, the emperor was vexed by his children; after his eldest son was blinded, John left the throne to his second son Manuel [PDF p. 359]. Manuel later exploited a period of Ottoman civil war to regain territories, but the empire remained a vassal state [PDF p. 362]. The author describes the later years as a period of unredeemed gloom [PDF p. 365]. Under John VI, the empire was a dilapidated ruin with a shrinking population and a military of mostly foreign mercenaries [PDF p. 366]. The emperor’s attempt to win Western aid through conversion to Catholicism yielded only a small sum of money and three hundred mercenaries [PDF p. 367].
