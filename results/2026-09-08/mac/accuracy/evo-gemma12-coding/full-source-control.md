The Hunnic invasion of 372 forced the Ostrogoths into vassalage and drove the Visigoths toward the Danube (p. 61). A contemporary writer describes the desperate plea of the Goths, who sought asylum from the "murderous savagery" of the Huns (p. 62). The emperor Valens, whom the author characterizes as a "stupid, cowardly, and avaricious prince," granted passage on the condition that the Goths surrender their arms and provide hostages (p. 62). However, the Goths retained their weapons through bribes and were later subjected to extortion by the governor Lupicinus, who withheld food and sold grain at exorbitant prices (p. 63). This desperation led to a bloody skirmish at Marcianopolis and a year of warfare along the Danube, where the author claims the "old barbarian spirit" emerged from the veneer of civilization (p. 64).

The conflict culminated in 378 when the Goths defeated the imperial army at Adrianople (p. 65). The author attributes this to the Goths' adoption of heavy cavalry and the failure of the infantry to withstand a flank attack by mounted men (p. 66). The battle is compared to the massacre at Cannae, and the emperor and many commanders were killed (p. 67). The Goths later besieged Constantinople but retreated when the city's defenses appeared inaccessible (p. 67). The author concludes that Constantine’s earlier foresight in building the fortress proved prescient (p. 67).

By the seventh century, the eastern empire had largely abandoned Latin in favor of Greek (p. 169). The author identifies the decline of Latin with the loss of the western half of the empire and the barbarian devastation of the eastern provinces (p. 170). While the emperor still used Latin titles, the educated class was increasingly ignorant of the language (p. 169). The author notes that the only significant Latin-speaking populations remained in Africa and the Italian Exarchate (p. 170).

The adoption of Christianity fundamentally altered social life by banning gladiatorial shows and ending infanticide (p. 171). The author argues that the belief in the immortal soul led to the legal protection of slaves and the banning of their prostitution (p. 173). However, the author also criticizes the rise of ascetic monasticism as a "repulsively selfish" retreat from civic duty that left the empire vulnerable to barbarian invasion (p. 174). The author concludes that the empire’s later decline was a result of this apathy, which the pagan minority accused of ruining the state (p. 176).
