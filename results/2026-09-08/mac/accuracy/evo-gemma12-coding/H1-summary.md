The author begins by characterizing the Huns as a savage horde that nearly exterminated the Alans and crushed the Ostrogoths [PDF p. 61]. The author then reports a Gothic historian’s description of the Huns as shapeless, fierce beasts [PDF p. 61]. The Visigoths, fleeing the Huns and seeking refuge from the threat of slavery, petitioned the Roman Empire for passage [PDF p. 62]. The author evaluates Emperor Valens as a stupid, cowardly, and avaricious ruler who ultimately allowed the Goths to cross the Danube in exchange for hostages and surrendered arms [PDF p. 62, 63]. After the Goths retained their weapons and were subjected to extortion by the governor Lupicinus, a skirmish at Marcianopolis led Fritigern to declare war on Rome [PDF p. 63, 64]. The author claims the Goths’ barbarian spirit was only thinly covered by a veneer of civilization [PDF p. 64]. The conflict culminated in the battle of Adrianople, where the Goths used cavalry to crush the Roman infantry [PDF p. 65, 66]. The author compares the slaughter to Cannae and concludes that Constantine’s foresight was proven when the Goths later abandoned a siege of Constantinople [PDF p. 67].
