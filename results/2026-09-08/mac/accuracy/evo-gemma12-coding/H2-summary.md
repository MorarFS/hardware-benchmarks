The author begins by tracing the decline of Latin in the East, where the language became increasingly rare among educated men by the sixth century [PDF p. 169]. The author attributes this to the practical solution of the empire's division and the barbarian devastation of Latin-speaking regions, leaving only remote areas like Africa and the Italian Exarchate as holdouts [PDF p. 170]. The text then shifts to the internal transformation of the state, which the author describes as becoming more Christian at heart while retaining old official titles [PDF p. 169]. The author credits the belief in the individual human soul for improving the conditions of women, infants, and slaves, and for ending infanticide and gladiatorial shows [PDF p. 171, 173]. The passage concludes by warning that ascetic monasticism became a dangerous anti-social attitude that led many to view barbarian invasions as divine judgment rather than national calamities [PDF p. 174, 175]. The author notes that while paganism was formally ended by Justinian, a widespread indifference prevailed among the literary and official classes [PDF p. 176].
