The author characterizes the Huns as a savage horde that nearly exterminated the Alans and crushed the Ostrogoths [PDF p. 61]. A Gothic historian is reported to describe them as shapeless, fierce beasts [PDF p. 61]. Seeking refuge from the threat of slavery, the Visigoths petitioned Rome for passage [PDF p. 62]. The author evaluates Emperor Valens as a stupid, cowardly, and avaricious ruler who allowed the Guns to cross the Danube in exchange for hostages and surrendered arms [PDF p. 62, 63]. After the Goths retained their weapons and were subjected to extortion by the governor Lupicinus, a skirmish at Marcianopolis led Fritigern to declare war on Rome [PDF p. 63, 64]. The author claims the Goths’ barbarian spirit was only thinly covered by a veneer of civilization [PDF p. 64]. The conflict culminated in the battle of Adrianople, where the Goths used cavalry to crush the Roman infantry [PDF p. 65, 66]. The author compares the slaughter to Cannae and concludes that Constantine’s foresight was proven when the Goths later abandoned a siege of Constantinople [PDF p. 67].

The author traces the decline of Latin in the East, where the language became increasingly rare among educated men by the sixth century [PDF p. 169]. This is attributed to the practical solution of the empire's division and the barbarian devastation of Latin-speaking regions, leaving only remote areas like Africa and the Italian Exarchate as holdouts [PDF p. 170]. The state became more Christian at heart while retaining old official titles [PDF p. 169]. The author credits the belief in the individual human soul for improving the conditions of women, infants, and slaves, and for ending infanticide and gladiatorial shows [PDF p. 171, 173]. The passage warns that ascetic monasticism became a dangerous anti-social attitude that led many to view barbarian invasions as divine judgment rather than national calamities [PDF p. 174, 175]. While paganism was formally ended by Justinian, a widespread indifference prevailed among the literary and official classes [PDF p. 176].

The eighty years following Basil the Macedonian's death were the most unevented and monotonous in imperial history [PDF p. 241]. The author characterizes the two literary emperors as mild, inoffensive men who devoted themselves to the pen rather than the sword [PDF p. 241]. Leo's reign was only interrupted by a failed Bulgarian invasion and a disgraceful raid on Thessalonica in 904, which the author notes was a surprise attack that the army could have stopped had it lasted longer [PDF p. 242]. Constantine was kept in the background by the usurper Romanus until a popular revolt removed the usurpers and allowed him to rule for twenty years [PDF p. 243]. The author evaluates Leo's military manual as a valuable and interesting work that provides a perfect picture of the army [PDF p. 244]. Constantine is described as a careful and methodical compiler whose works on administration and court ceremony reflect his life as a peaceful emperor [PDF p. 246, 247]. The author concludes that their literary devotion signals the end of a long period of intellectual dearth in Byzantine history [PDF p. 247].

The author characterizes the final seventy-five years of the Byzantine Empire as a mere piece of local history rather than a significant thread in the history of Christendom [PDF p. 358]. The empire ceased to function as a bulwark against the Ottomans after 1370 and the Paleologi prolonged the empire through base subservience [PDF p. 358]. During the reign of John Paleologus, the emperor was vexed by his children; after his eldest son was blinded, John left the throne to his second son Manuel [PDF p. 359]. Manuel later exploited a period of Ottoman civil war to regain territories, but the empire remained a vassal state [PDF p. 362]. The author describes the later years as a period of unredeemed gloom [PDF p. 365]. Under John VI, the empire was a dilapidated ruin with a shrinking population and a military of mostly foreign mercenaries [PDF p. 366]. The emperor's attempt to win Western aid through conversion to Catholicism yielded only a small sum of money and three hundred mercenaries [PDF p. 367].
