The author evaluates the final seventy-five years of the Byzantine Empire as a piece of local history [PDF p. 358]. He judges that the Paleologi protracted the empire's life through base subservience to the Turks [PDF p. 358]. During the reign of John Paleologus, his son Andronicus rebelled twice, once allying with Saoudji [PDF p. 359]. Consequently, John left the throne to his second son, Manuel II, in 1391 [PDF p. 359].

Manuel II initially lacked the resources to rebel [PDF p. 360]. However, after Timour defeated Sultan Bayezid in 1402, Manuel exploited the resulting Ottoman civil war to recover territories, including Thessalonica and Black Sea seaports [PDF p. 360-361]. The author notes that Europe failed to use this opportunity to expel the Ottomans because King Sigismund was occupied with the Hussites and Hungarian-Serbian tensions persisted [PDF p. 362-363]. By 1421, Mohammed the Unifier consolidated Ottoman power [PDF p. 362]. Manuel's subsequent support of Ottoman claimants led Murad II to besiege Constantinople [PDF p. 363]. Though the city resisted, Manuel eventually accepted vassalage in 1422 [PDF p. 363-365].

Manuel's successor, John VI, ruled a state of exhaustion and debility [PDF p. 365-366]. After Thessalonica was annexed by the Ottomans in 1430, John VI converted to Roman Catholicism in 1439 to seek aid [PDF p. 367]. The author judges this a poor return, as Pope Eugenius IV provided only money and three hundred mercenaries [PDF p. 367].
