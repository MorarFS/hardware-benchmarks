The author judges the eighty years following Basil the Macedonian's death as the most monotonous in imperial history [PDF p. 241]. This period was defined by the reigns of Leo the Wise and Constantine Porphyrogenitus, whom the author describes as inoffensive men of literature [PDF p. 241]. Because the empire faced few foreign wars, these unenterprising rulers remained in the palace rather than seeking conquest [PDF p. 242]. Leo's reign included a failed invasion of Bulgaria and a disgraceful Saracen raid on Thessalonica in 904 [PDF p. 242].

Constantine Porphyrogenitus spent his early years under guardians and the usurper Romanus Lecapenus [PDF p. 243]. He eventually took power after a popular uprising removed the sons of Romanus [PDF p. 243]. The author evaluates Constantine as weak and ineffective but not tyrannical [PDF p. 243]. Both emperors produced significant literary works [PDF p. 243]. Leo wrote a valuable manual on the Art of War, which the author claims provides a perfect picture of the Byzantine army [PDF p. 244]. Constantine authored works on the Themes, imperial administration, and court ceremonies [PDF p. 246]. The author concludes that this literary focus signals the end of a long period of intellectual dearth [PDF p. 247].
