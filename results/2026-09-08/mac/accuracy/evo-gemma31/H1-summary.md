Around A.D. 372, the Huns entered lands north of the Euxine, nearly exterminating the Alans and crushing the Ostrogoths [PDF p. 61]. The Huns then pressed the Visigoths toward the Danube [PDF p. 61]. The author evaluates the Visigoths as more civilized and Christian than their brethren, making the prospect of slavery to the Huns intolerable [PDF p. 61-62]. In A.D. 376, the Visigoths requested permission to cross into the Roman Empire [PDF p. 62]. The author describes the Eastern Emperor Valens as stupid, cowardly, and avaricious [PDF p. 62]. Valens permitted the Goths to settle as subject allies if they surrendered arms and gave hostages [PDF p. 62-63].

However, Roman officials accepted bribes to let Goths keep their weapons, and Governor Lupicinus extorted the immigrants by withholding food [PDF p. 63]. This desperation led to a conflict in Marcianopolis, where Lupicinus attempted to slay Fritigern, prompting a Gothic revolt [PDF p. 63-64]. After a year of fighting, Valens personally led an army against the Goths in 378 A.D. [PDF p. 64-65]. The Goths, having adopted cavalry warfare, annihilated the Roman army at Adrianople, killing Valens [PDF p. 65-67]. The Goths subsequently attacked Constantinople but retreated after seeing its vast walls [PDF p. 67].
