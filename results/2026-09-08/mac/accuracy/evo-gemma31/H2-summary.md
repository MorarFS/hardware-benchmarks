Latin usage in the East declined due to the empire's division and barbarian incursions that decimated Latin-speaking populations [PDF p. 170]. The author notes that while official titles and imperial greetings persisted, Latin knowledge became rare among the educated by the sixth century [PDF p. 169]. Justinian was the final emperor to speak Latin as a mother tongue [PDF p. 169].

Simultaneously, the empire became more Christian. The author claims the Christian spirit fully penetrated material life forty years after Constantine [PDF p. 171]. This shift led to the abolition of gladiatorial shows following the death of the monk Telemachus in A.D. 404 [PDF p. 171] and the criminalization of infanticide [PDF p. 172]. Christianity also modified slavery by teaching that slaves possessed immortal souls [PDF p. 173].

However, the author evaluates the rise of ascetic monasticism as positively dangerous [PDF p. 174]. He argues that thousands of citizens neglected their state duties to save their own souls, viewing barbarian invasions as divine judgments [PDF p. 174, 175]. This apathetic attitude led the surviving heathen minority to accuse Christianity of ruining the state [PDF p. 176]. By the end of the fifth century, paganism had practically disappeared [PDF p. 176].
