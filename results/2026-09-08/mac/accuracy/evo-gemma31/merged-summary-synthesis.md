Around A.D. 372, the Huns entered lands north of the Euxine, where they crushed the Ostrogoths and nearly exterminated the Alans [PDF p. 61]. This movement pressed the Visigoths toward the Danube [PDF p. 61]. The author evaluates the Visigoths as more Christian and civilized than their brethren, which made the prospect of slavery to the Huns intolerable [PDF p. 61-62]. In A.D. 376, the Visigoths requested entry into the Roman Empire [PDF p. 62]. The author describes the Eastern Emperor Valens as avaricious, cowardly, and stupid [PDF p. 62]. Valens allowed the Goths to settle as subject allies on the condition that they provided hostages and surrendered their arms [PDF p. 62-63].

Conflict arose when Governor Lupicinus extorted immigrants by withholding food and Roman officials accepted bribes to allow the Goths to keep their weapons [PDF p. 63]. This desperation led to a revolt in Marcianopolis after Lupicinus attempted to slay Fritigern [PDF p. 63-64]. In 378 A.D., Valens led an army against the Goths, who had adopted cavalry warfare [PDF p. 64-65]. The Goths annihilated the Roman army and killed Valens at Adrianople [PDF p. 65-67]. The Goths subsequently attacked Constantinople but retreated upon seeing the city's vast walls [PDF p. 67].

Latin usage declined in the East due to barbarian incursions that decimated Latin speakers and the division of the empire [PDF p. 170]. The author notes that Latin knowledge became rare among the educated by the sixth century, though imperial greetings and official titles persisted [PDF p. 169]. Justinian was the final emperor to speak Latin as a mother tongue [PDF p. 169]. Simultaneously, the author claims the Christian spirit fully penetrated material life forty years after Constantine [PDF p. 171]. This shift resulted in the criminalization of infanticide [PDF p. 172] and the abolition of gladiatorial shows following the death of the monk Telemachus in A.D. 404 [PDF p. 171]. Christianity also taught that slaves possessed immortal souls [PDF p. 173]. The author evaluates the rise of ascetic monasticism as positively dangerous, arguing that citizens neglected state duties to save their souls and viewed barbarian invasions as divine judgments [PDF p. 174-175]. This apathy led the surviving heathen minority to accuse Christianity of ruining the state [PDF p. 176]. Paganism had practically disappeared by the end of the fifth century [PDF p. 176].

The author judges the eighty years following the death of Basil the Macedonian as the most monotonous period in imperial history [PDF p. 241]. This era featured Leo the Wise and Constantine Porphyrogenitus, whom the author describes as inoffensive men of literature [PDF p. 241]. Because there were few foreign wars, these unenterprising rulers remained in the palace [PDF p. 242]. Leo's reign saw a disgraceful Saracen raid on Thessalonica in 904 and a failed invasion of Bulgaria [PDF p. 242]. Constantine Porphyrogenitus spent his early years under the usurper Romanus Lecapenus and guardians [PDF p. 243]. He took power after a popular uprising removed the sons of Romanus [PDF p. 243]. The author evaluates Constantine as ineffective and weak, though not tyrannical [PDF p. 243]. Both emperors produced literary works [PDF p. 243]. Leo wrote a manual on the Art of War that the author claims provides a perfect picture of the Byzantine army [PDF p. 244]. Constantine wrote on court ceremonies, imperial administration, and the Themes [PDF p. 246]. The author concludes that this focus signals the end of a long intellectual dearth [PDF p. 247].

The author evaluates the final seventy-five years of the empire as a piece of local history [PDF p. 358]. He judges that the Paleologi used base subservience to the Turks to protract the empire's life [PDF p. 358]. During the reign of John Paleologus, his son Andronicus rebelled twice, once allying with Saoudji [PDF p. 359]. John left the throne to his second son, Manuel II, in 1391 [PDF p. 359]. Manuel II initially lacked resources to rebel [PDF p. 360]. However, after Timour defeated Sultan Bayezid in 1402, Manuel exploited the Ottoman civil war to recover Black Sea seaports and Thessalonica [PDF p. 360-361]. The author notes that King Sigismund's occupation with the Hussites and persisting Hungarian-Serbian tensions prevented Europe from using this opportunity to expel the Ottomans [PDF p. 362-363]. By 1421, Mohammed the Unifier consolidated power [PDF p. 362]. Manuel's support of Ottoman claimants led Murad II to besiege Constantinople [PDF p. 363]. Manuel accepted vassalage in 1422 [PDF p. 363-365]. His successor, John VI, ruled a state of debility and exhaustion [PDF p. 365-366]. After the Ottomans annexed Thessalonica in 1430, John VI converted to Roman Catholicism in 1439 to seek aid [PDF p. 367]. The author judges this a poor return, as Pope Eugenius IV provided only three hundred mercenaries and money [PDF p. 367].
