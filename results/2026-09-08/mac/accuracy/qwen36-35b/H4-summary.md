The author argues that the Byzantine Empire ceased to be a vital bulwark for Christendom after 1370, asserting that its prolonged existence resulted from the Paleologi’s subservience to the Turks [PDF p. 358]. The text suggests that an earlier fall of Constantinople would have only marginally altered European history, potentially affecting Genoese and Venetian commerce or delaying the Renaissance [PDF p. 359]. Under John Paleologus, internal strife weakened the dynasty, as his son Andronicus rebelled and was blinded, leading John to bypass him in favor of Manuel II [PDF p. 359]. Manuel II demonstrated limited capacity but lacked the resources for rebellion, relying instead on the Ottoman civil war triggered by Tamerlane’s victory in 1402 [PDF p. 360]. He exploited this chaos to regain territories, yet the author notes that Europe missed a rare opportunity to expel the Ottomans due to Hungarian conflicts with the Hussites and poor relations with Serbia [PDF p. 362]. When Murad II unified the Ottoman realm, he reclaimed these lands and besieged Constantinople, forcing Manuel into renewed vassalage [PDF p. 363]. The author describes the subsequent decades as a period of unredeemed gloom, characterized by the city’s physical decay and economic dependence on Italian merchants [PDF p. 365]. John VI. maintained peace but lost Thessalonica to the Ottomans after his brother sold it to Venice [PDF p. 367]. Finally, John VIII. traveled to Italy to secure aid through religious union with Rome, but the author evaluates his efforts as futile, noting that Pope Eugenius IV. lacked the power to provide significant assistance, leaving the empire isolated and doomed [PDF p. 367].
