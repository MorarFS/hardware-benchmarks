The author characterizes the eighty years following Basil the Macedonian’s death as the most monotonous period in imperial history, dominated by the literary reigns of Leo VI and Constantine VII [PDF p. 241]. While these emperors were mild men of letters rather than military conquerors, the era experienced relative peace compared to other periods [PDF p. 242]. The Caliphate fragmented in the East, and northern neighbors settled down, leaving only minor piratical raids as threats [PDF p. 242]. Although the capture of Thessalonica by Saracens in 904 was disgraceful, the author notes it resulted from surprise rather than systemic failure [PDF p. 242]. Constantine VII’s rise to power was delayed by regents, including his uncle Alexander and the usurper Romanus Lecapenus [PDF p. 243]. Constantine only assumed full control after popular outrage removed the Lecapenus family, allowing him to rule for twenty years as a weak but non-tyrannical monarch [PDF p. 243]. The author explicitly praises Leo’s military manual as invaluable for detailing Byzantine organization, noting the army’s high discipline and care for wounded soldiers [PDF p. 244]. Constantine’s works, particularly his treatises on administration and court ceremonies, reveal him as a methodical compiler rather than an original thinker [PDF p. 246]. The author argues that these literary achievements signal the end of the intellectual dearth that plagued the empire from Justinian’s death through the Heraclian dynasty [PDF p. 247]. By the ninth century, a robust literary class had emerged, exemplified by the learned patriarch Photius [PDF p. 247].
