The author argues that Constantine’s foresight in establishing Constantinople proved vital as the Huns displaced the Goths [PDF p. 61]. After crushing the Ostrogoths, the Huns forced the Visigoths to seek refuge across the Danube in 376 [PDF p. 62]. The Eastern Emperor Valens, described by the author as stupid and cowardly, permitted their entry under strict conditions [PDF p. 63]. However, Roman officials exploited the starving immigrants through extortion, leading to a violent outbreak at Marcianopolis [PDF p. 64]. This triggered a year of fierce fighting along the Balkans [PDF p. 65].

The author notes that the Goths had adopted cavalry tactics, a significant shift from traditional infantry warfare [PDF p. 66]. At the Battle of Adrianople in 378, Valens attacked the Gothic camp with sixty thousand men [PDF p. 66]. A sudden charge by Gothic cavalry overwhelmed the Roman left flank, causing total collapse [PDF p. 67]. The author emphasizes that the Roman infantry was crushed in a dense press, unable to maneuver or fight effectively [PDF p. 67]. Forty thousand Romans died, including the Emperor and high commanders [PDF p. 67].

The author characterizes this defeat as the most fearful loss since Cannae [PDF p. 67]. The Eastern army was annihilated and never reorganized [PDF p. 67]. The victorious Goths marched toward Constantinople but abandoned the siege upon seeing its massive walls [PDF p. 67]. The author concludes that this event validated Constantine’s original strategic vision for the capital [PDF p. 67].
