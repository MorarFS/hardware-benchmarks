The narrative begins with the Huns’ westward expansion around A.D. 372, which crushed the Ostrogoths and forced the Visigoths to seek refuge within the Roman Empire (p. 61). Pressed against the Danube, the Visigoths, numbering nearly 200,000, petitioned Emperor Valens for asylum in 376 (p. 62). Valens, described as cowardly and avaricious, agreed to their entry on the condition that they surrender their arms and provide hostages, intending to settle them as subject allies (p. 63). However, corruption among Roman officials, particularly Governor Lupicinus, led to severe extortion and starvation among the immigrants, who were forced to sell their children for bread (p. 63). This desperation culminated in a banquet at Marcianopolis where Lupicinus attempted to assassinate the Gothic leader Fritigern, prompting the Goths to revolt and launch a year of fierce fighting along the Danube (p. 64).

The conflict escalated into the Battle of Adrianople in 378, where Emperor Valens led a large army against the Goths (p. 65). The battle marked a turning point in military history, as the Goths, having adopted cavalry tactics, utilized a sudden charge by their mounted forces to flank and crush the Roman infantry (p. 66). The Roman army was annihilated, with Valens and many high-ranking commanders killed, and the defeat is compared to the disaster at Cannae (p. 67). Although the victorious Goths approached Constantinople, they abandoned the siege due to the city’s formidable walls and retreated to Thrace, validating Constantine’s earlier strategic foresight regarding the capital’s defenses (p. 67).

In the subsequent centuries, the Eastern Roman Empire underwent significant cultural and social transformations. The Latin language gradually declined in favor of Greek, a process accelerated by barbarian invasions that decimated the Latin-speaking populations in the Balkans (p. 169–170). Simultaneously, Christianity reshaped social structures by abolishing practices such as infanticide and modifying the harshness of slavery, though it also fostered a rise in ascetic monasticism (p. 171–175). This monastic movement drew many men away from civic duties, leading some contemporaries to argue that Christianity weakened the state’s ability to resist barbarian invasions (p. 175–176). Despite these internal shifts, the empire maintained its administrative traditions and military organization, as evidenced by the literary works of Emperor Leo VI, who detailed the structure and tactics of the Byzantine army and navy (p. 241–246).

By the late fourteenth century, the empire had shrunk to a rump state centered on Constantinople, relying on diplomacy and occasional opportunism to survive Ottoman expansion (p. 358). Emperor Manuel II briefly regained territory during the Ottoman civil war following Tamerlane’s defeat of Bayezid I in 1402, but these gains were largely reversed after the Ottomans reunified under Mehmed I (p. 360–362). The empire’s final decades were marked by extreme poverty and military weakness, with Constantinople’s population dwindling and its defenses relying on mercenaries (p. 365–366). In a final attempt to secure aid, Emperor John VIII traveled to Italy and agreed to church union with Rome in 1439, but received only minimal support from a weakened papacy, leaving the empire isolated and vulnerable to its eventual fall (p. 367).
