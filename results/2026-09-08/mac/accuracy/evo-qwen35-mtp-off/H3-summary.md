The author characterizes the eighty years following Basil the Macedonian’s death as the most uneventful period in imperial history [PDF p. 241]. This era was dominated by Leo the Wise and Constantine Porphyrogenitus, who were mild literary figures rather than military conquerors [PDF p. 241]. The author argues that these emperors lacked the strength to protect their thrones had the times been harder, yet the period from 880 to 960 experienced fewer foreign wars than any other corresponding era [PDF p. 242]. The Caliphate fragmented in the East, while northern neighbors settled down, leaving only minor piratical raids as threats [PDF p. 242]. Although the capture of Thessalonica by Saracens in 904 was disgraceful, the author notes it occurred by surprise and the invaders fled quickly [PDF p. 242]. Constantine VII spent his youth under regents, including Romanus Lecapenus, before emerging to rule after popular wrath removed the usurpers’ sons [PDF p. 243]. The author describes Constantine as weak but not tyrannical, noting that many abler men made worse rulers [PDF p. 243]. The primary achievements of both emperors were their literary works [PDF p. 243]. Leo’s manual on war provides a detailed picture of the organized Byzantine army, which the author praises for its modern-like equipment and care for the wounded [PDF p. 244]. Constantine’s writings on administration and court ceremonies reveal him as a methodical compiler rather than an original thinker [PDF p. 246]. The author concludes that these literary efforts signaled the end of the intellectual dearth that had plagued Byzantium since the seventh century [PDF p. 247].
