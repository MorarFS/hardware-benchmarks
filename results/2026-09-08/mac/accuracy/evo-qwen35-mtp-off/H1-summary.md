The author argues that Constantine’s foresight in establishing Constantinople proved vital as the Huns displaced the Goths, forcing the Visigoths to seek refuge in the Roman Empire [PDF p. 61]. In 376, the desperate Visigoths, numbering nearly two hundred thousand, petitioned Emperor Valens to cross the Danube [PDF p. 62]. Valens, described by the author as stupid and cowardly, agreed to their settlement as subject allies, provided they surrendered hostages and arms [PDF p. 63]. However, Roman officials, particularly Governor Lupicinus, accepted bribes to allow the Goths to keep their weapons and subsequently engaged in extortion, selling food at exorbitant prices [PDF p. 63]. This starvation drove the Goths to desperation, culminating in a banquet incident where Lupicinus attempted to assassinate their leader, Fritigern [PDF p. 64]. Fritigern escaped and rallied his people, initiating a year of fierce fighting along the Danube [PDF p. 64]. The conflict escalated when Valens personally led a large army against the Goths near Adrianople in 378 [PDF p. 65]. The author notes that the Goths had adopted cavalry tactics, which proved decisive when their horsemen arrived to flank the Roman forces [PDF p. 66]. The resulting battle was a catastrophic defeat for Rome, with forty thousand soldiers killed and the Emperor slain [PDF p. 67]. Although the Goths approached Constantinople, they abandoned the siege due to the city’s formidable walls [PDF p. 67]. The author concludes that this disaster marked the first attack on the capital since its founding, validating Constantine’s original strategic vision [PDF p. 67].
