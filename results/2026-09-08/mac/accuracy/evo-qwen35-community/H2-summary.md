The author argues that Latin decayed in the East due to the practical separation from the West and barbarian devastation [PDF p. 170]. By the seventh century, Latin was vanishing, though imperial titles persisted [PDF p. 169]. Justinian was the last emperor to speak Latin as a mother tongue [PDF p. 169]. The author attributes this linguistic shift to the predominance of Greek and the decimation of Latin-speaking populations by Slavs and other groups [PDF p. 170].

Simultaneously, the empire became increasingly Christian [PDF p. 171]. The author notes that Christian influence on state machinery began slowly, accelerating under Theodosius I [PDF p. 171]. The author explicitly praises the end of gladiatorial games and infanticide as positive outcomes of Christian morality [PDF p. 171, 173]. The teaching that slaves possessed immortal souls modified slavery’s evils, though the institution persisted [PDF p. 173]. The author traces these improvements to the belief in the individual soul’s importance [PDF p. 174].

However, the author evaluates the rise of ascetic monasticism as harmful to the state [PDF p. 174]. The author claims monks neglected civic duties, interpreting barbarian invasions as divine judgments rather than national calamities [PDF p. 175]. This apathy angered surviving pagans, who blamed Christianity for the empire’s ruin [PDF p. 176]. The author contrasts this with governing classes who preserved Roman statecraft traditions [PDF p. 176]. Finally, the author observes that while open paganism ended, indifference prevailed among educated Christians in the sixth century [PDF p. 176].
