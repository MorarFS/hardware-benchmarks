The author characterizes the eighty years following Basil the Macedonian’s death as the most monotonous in imperial history, dominated by the literary pursuits of Leo VI and Constantine VII [PDF p. 241]. While these emperors were mild men of letters rather than warriors, the period from 880 to 960 was remarkably free from major foreign wars [PDF p. 242]. The Caliphate was fragmenting in the East, and Western powers were disintegrating, leaving only minor piratical threats from Russians and Saracens [PDF p. 242]. Although the Saracen capture of Thessalonica in 904 was disgraceful, the author notes it occurred by surprise and caused no lasting damage [PDF p. 242]. Constantine VII eventually assumed power after the fall of the Lecapenus family, ruling for twenty years as a weak but non-tyrannical monarch [PDF p. 243].

The author evaluates the emperors’ primary achievements as their literary works, particularly Leo’s manual on the Art of War [PDF p. 243]. This text reveals a highly organized Byzantine army with specialized uniforms, engineers, and medical support, reflecting confidence in its ability to defeat eastern foes [PDF p. 244]. The author praises the fleet’s use of Greek fire and its role in policing the Aegean, though it could not fully suppress piracy [PDF p. 245]. Constantine’s contributions included invaluable administrative manuals and a biography of his grandfather [PDF p. 246]. The author interprets Constantine’s elaborate focus on court ceremonies as compensation for his lack of real power during his youth [PDF p. 247]. Finally, the author argues that the literary output of these emperors signals the end of the intellectual dearth that had plagued Byzantium since the seventh century [PDF p. 247].
