The first excerpt details the catastrophic consequences of Gothic migration and Roman mismanagement in the fourth century. The author argues that Constantine’s establishment of Constantinople proved prescient as Hunnic pressure displaced the Goths toward the Roman border. The Huns crushed the Ostrogoths and drove the Visigoths, led by Fritigern, against the Danube. Desperate, the Visigoths petitioned Emperor Valens for asylum. The author characterizes Valens as a stupid, cowardly, and avaricious ruler who lacked the military prowess of his brother Valentinian. Valens permitted the Goths to cross the Danube in exchange for hostages and their weapons. However, Roman officials accepted bribes to allow the Goths to keep their arms, and Governor Lupicinus hoarded food to sell at exorbitant prices. Starvation drove the Goths to desperation, leading to a violent clash at Marcianopolis where Lupicinus attempted to assassinate Fritigern. This incident ignited a year of fierce fighting along the Danube. The author notes that the Goths had adopted cavalry tactics, which proved decisive against the Roman infantry. In 378 A.D., Valens attacked the Gothic camp near Adrianople with sixty thousand men. A sudden charge by Gothic cavalry overwhelmed the Roman left flank, causing the entire army to collapse into a confused mass. The author describes the battle as a fearful disaster comparable to Cannae, resulting in the death of forty thousand Romans and the Emperor himself. Although the Goths marched on Constantinople, they abandoned the siege due to the city’s formidable walls. The author concludes that this event marked the first attack on the capital since its founding, validating Constantine’s original strategic vision.

The second excerpt examines the linguistic and religious transformations within the empire during the seventh century. The author argues that Latin decayed in the East due to the practical separation from the West and barbarian devastation. By the seventh century, Latin was vanishing, though imperial titles persisted. Justinian was the last emperor to speak Latin as a mother tongue. The author attributes this linguistic shift to the predominance of Greek and the decimation of Latin-speaking populations by Slavs and other groups. Simultaneously, the empire became increasingly Christian. The author notes that Christian influence on state machinery began slowly, accelerating under Theodosius I. The author explicitly praises the end of gladiatorial games and infanticide as positive outcomes of Christian morality. The teaching that slaves possessed immortal souls modified slavery’s evils, though the institution persisted. The author traces these improvements to the belief in the individual soul’s importance. However, the author evaluates the rise of ascetic monasticism as harmful to the state. The author claims monks neglected civic duties, interpreting barbarian invasions as divine judgments rather than national calamities. This apathy angered surviving pagans, who blamed Christianity for the empire’s ruin. The author contrasts this with governing classes who preserved Roman statecraft traditions. Finally, the author observes that while open paganism ended, indifference prevailed among educated Christians in the sixth century.

The third excerpt describes the period following Basil the Macedonian’s death as monotonous but culturally significant. The author characterizes the eighty years following Basil the Macedonian’s death as the most monotonous in imperial history, dominated by the literary pursuits of Leo VI and Constantine VII. While these emperors were mild men of letters rather than warriors, the period from 880 to 960 was remarkably free from major foreign wars. The Caliphate was fragmenting in the East, and Western powers were disintegrating, leaving only minor piratical threats from Russians and Saracens. Although the Saracen capture of Thessalonica in 904 was disgraceful, the author notes it occurred by surprise and caused no lasting damage. Constantine VII eventually assumed power after the fall of the Lecapenus family, ruling for twenty years as a weak but non-tyrannical monarch. The author evaluates the emperors’ primary achievements as their literary works, particularly Leo’s manual on the Art of War. This text reveals a highly organized Byzantine army with specialized uniforms, engineers, and medical support, reflecting confidence in its ability to defeat eastern foes. The author praises the fleet’s use of Greek fire and its role in policing the Aegean, though it could not fully suppress piracy. Constantine’s contributions included invaluable administrative manuals and a biography of his grandfather. The author interprets Constantine’s elaborate focus on court ceremonies as compensation for his lack of real power during his youth. Finally, the author argues that the literary output of these emperors signals the end of the intellectual dearth that had plagued Byzantium since the seventh century.

The fourth excerpt analyzes the final decline of the Byzantine Empire in the late fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries. The author argues that the Byzantine Empire ceased to be a vital bulwark for Christendom after 1370, rendering its prolonged existence unjustified. The text suggests that an earlier fall of Constantinople would have only slightly altered European history by affecting Genoese and Venetian commerce or delaying the Renaissance. During the reign of John Paleologus, internal strife weakened the state, as his son Andronicus rebelled and was blinded, leading John to bypass him in favor of Manuel. Manuel II demonstrated some capacity but lacked the resources to resist the Turks until the arrival of Tamerlane in 1402. This crisis allowed Manuel to exploit Ottoman civil wars, selling his support to Turkish pretenders and temporarily recovering significant territories. However, the author notes that Europe missed a rare opportunity to expel the Ottomans because Hungary was distracted by Hussite wars and lacked unity with the Servians. When Mohammed unified the Ottoman realm, Manuel allied with him to retain his gains, but the subsequent rise of Murad II ended this respite. Murad attacked Constantinople, forcing Manuel to cede most recovered lands and return to vassalage. The author describes the empire’s condition as one of unredeemed gloom, with the capital reduced to ruins and its economy controlled by Italian merchants. John VI. avoided conflict but lost Thessalonica when his brother sold it to Venice, prompting Ottoman annexation. The final emperor, John VIII, traveled to Italy to secure aid through religious union with Rome. The author evaluates this effort as futile, noting that Pope Eugenius IV was too weak to provide meaningful assistance, leaving the empire to its inevitable fate.
