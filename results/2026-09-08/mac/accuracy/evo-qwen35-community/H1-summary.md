The author argues that Constantine’s foresight in establishing Constantinople proved prescient as the Huns displaced the Goths, forcing them toward the Roman border [PDF p. 61]. The Huns crushed the Ostrogoths and drove the Visigoths, led by Fritigern, against the Danube [PDF p. 61]. Desperate, the Visigoths petitioned Emperor Valens for asylum [PDF p. 62]. The author characterizes Valens as a stupid, cowardly, and avaricious ruler who lacked the military prowess of his brother Valentinian [PDF p. 62]. Valens permitted the Goths to cross the Danube in exchange for hostages and their weapons [PDF p. 63]. However, Roman officials accepted bribes to allow the Goths to keep their arms, and Governor Lupicinus hoarded food to sell at exorbitant prices [PDF p. 63]. Starvation drove the Goths to desperation, leading to a violent clash at Marcianopolis where Lupicinus attempted to assassinate Fritigern [PDF p. 64]. This incident ignited a year of fierce fighting along the Danube [PDF p. 64]. The author notes that the Goths had adopted cavalry tactics, which proved decisive against the Roman infantry [PDF p. 65]. In 378 A.D., Valens attacked the Gothic camp near Adrianople with sixty thousand men [PDF p. 66]. A sudden charge by Gothic cavalry overwhelmed the Roman left flank, causing the entire army to collapse into a confused mass [PDF p. 66]. The author describes the battle as a fearful disaster comparable to Cannae, resulting in the death of forty thousand Romans and the Emperor himself [PDF p. 67]. Although the Goths marched on Constantinople, they abandoned the siege due to the city’s formidable walls [PDF p. 67]. The author concludes that this event marked the first attack on the capital since its founding, validating Constantine’s original strategic vision [PDF p. 67].
