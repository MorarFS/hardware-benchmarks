The author argues that the Byzantine Empire ceased to be a vital bulwark for Christendom after 1370, rendering its prolonged existence unjustified [PDF p. 358]. The text suggests that an earlier fall of Constantinople would have only slightly altered European history by affecting Genoese and Venetian commerce or delaying the Renaissance [PDF p. 359]. During the reign of John Paleologus, internal strife weakened the state, as his son Andronicus rebelled and was blinded, leading John to bypass him in favor of Manuel [PDF p. 359]. Manuel II demonstrated some capacity but lacked the resources to resist the Turks until the arrival of Tamerlane in 1402 [PDF p. 360]. This crisis allowed Manuel to exploit Ottoman civil wars, selling his support to Turkish pretenders and temporarily recovering significant territories [PDF p. 361]. However, the author notes that Europe missed a rare opportunity to expel the Ottomans because Hungary was distracted by Hussite wars and lacked unity with the Servians [PDF p. 362]. When Mohammed unified the Ottoman realm, Manuel allied with him to retain his gains, but the subsequent rise of Murad II ended this respite [PDF p. 363]. Murad attacked Constantinople, forcing Manuel to cede most recovered lands and return to vassalage [PDF p. 365]. The author describes the empire’s condition as one of unredeemed gloom, with the capital reduced to ruins and its economy controlled by Italian merchants [PDF p. 366]. John VI. avoided conflict but lost Thessalonica when his brother sold it to Venice, prompting Ottoman annexation [PDF p. 367]. The final emperor, John VIII, traveled to Italy to secure aid through religious union with Rome [PDF p. 367]. The author evaluates this effort as futile, noting that Pope Eugenius IV was too weak to provide meaningful assistance, leaving the empire to its inevitable fate [PDF p. 367].
