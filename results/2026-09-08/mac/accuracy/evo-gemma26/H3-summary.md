The eighty years following the death of Basil the Macedonian were the most monotonous in the empire's history [PDF p. 241]. The author characterizes the era as being defined by the reigns of Leo the Wise and Constantine Porphyrogenitus, whom the author describes as mild, inoffensive men of literature [PDF p. 241]. Because the period from 880 to 960 was relatively free from foreign wars, these unenterprising emperors focused on writing rather than military conquest [PDF p. 242]. Leo's reign included an unsuccessful Bulgarian invasion and a disgraceful Saracen raid on Thessalonica in 904 [PDF p. 242]. Constantine Porphyrogenitus, who inherited the throne at age seven, spent much of his youth under the tutelage of guardians, including the usurper Romanus Lecapenus [PDF p. 243]. Constantine eventually secured his rule after a popular uprising against the sons of Romanus [PDF p. 243].

The author evaluates the emperors' literary works as their chief achievements [PDF p. 243]. Leo's military manual provides a detailed picture of a highly organized Byzantine army and fleet [PDF p. 244, 246]. Constantine's works, including treatises on administration and court ceremonies, reveal him to be a methodical compiler [PDF p. 245, 246]. The author concludes that the literary output of these two emperors signaled an end to previous intellectual destitution [PDF p. 246].
