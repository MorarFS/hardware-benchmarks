The author characterizes the final seventy-five years of the Byzantine Empire as a mere piece of local history that no longer serves as an important thread in Christendom [PDF p. 358]. The era began with the reign of John Paleologus, whose long life was vexed by the rebellion of his son, Andronicus [PDF p. 359]. Following John's death in 1391, Manuel II ascended the throne [PDF p. 359]. Manuel briefly expanded his influence during the Ottoman civil wars following Timour's defeat of Sultan Bayezid in 1402 [PDF p. 360]. However, the author notes that Europe failed to seize this opportunity to expel the Ottomans because King Sigismund of Hungary was preoccupied with the Hussite wars [PDF p. 362].

After the unification of the Ottoman realm by Mohammed, Manuel faced the ambitious Murad II [PDF p. 362]. Although Manuel attempted to support Ottoman pretenders, Murad II besieged Constantinople and eventually forced Manuel to cede most dominions, returning the empire to vassalage in 1422 [PDF p. 363]. The author describes the subsequent reign of John VI as a period of unredeemed gloom [PDF p. 365]. Despite John's attempt to secure Western aid through conversion to Roman Catholicism at the Council of Florence, the author evaluates his efforts as yielding only a poor return [PDF p. 367].
