The Latin language declined in the East as the Roman element vanished by the seventh century [PDF p. 169]. The author notes that while Roman titles survived, they became mere names [PDF p. 169]. This decay resulted from the division of the empire and the loss of Latin-speaking provinces to barbarian incursions and Slavic migrations [PDF p. 170]. By A.D. 620, less than one tenth of the inhabitants east of the Adriatic spoke Latin [PDF p. 170].

Simultaneously, the empire became increasingly Christian [PDF p. 171]. The author observes that Christianity improved the conditions of vulnerable groups, such as infants and slaves, by emphasizing the importance of the individual soul [PDF p. 172, 173]. However, the author evaluates the rise of ascetic monasticism as a "positively dangerous" development [PDF p. 174]. This movement caused many to neglect civic duties in favor of personal salvation, which the author claims hampered the empire's defense against barbarians [PDF p. 175]. While the pagan minority accused Christianity of ruining the state, the author credits the governing classes with preserving Roman statecraft [PDF p. 175, 176]. By 529, Justinian formally ended pagan teaching by closing the schools of Athens [PDF p. 176].
