The author observes that the Latin tongue began to decay as the Roman element in the East rapidly vanished by the seventh century [PDF p. 169]. This decline originated from the practical solution of maintaining continuity between Rome and the East following the empire's division, which caused the Latin element to yield to Greek [PDF p. 170]. The process accelerated because the Latin-speaking Eastern provinces suffered most from barbarian decimation, leaving only remote districts like Africa and the Italian Exarchate with considerable Latin-speaking populations [PDF p. 170].

Simultaneously, the empire grew more Christian, with the Christian spirit fully penetrating the material sphere forty years after Constantine's death [PDF p. 171]. The author notes that Christianity modified social life by ending infanticide and altering the status of slaves, who were now regarded as moral beings with immortal souls [PDF p. 171, 173]. However, the author evaluates the rise of ascetic monasticism as positively dangerous [PDF p. 174]. He argues that this movement caused many citizens to neglect their duties to the State, leading to an apathetic attitude toward barbarian invasions [PDF p. 175].
