The author asserts that the final seventy-five years of the Byzantine Empire were merely local history, as the empire ceased to function as the bulwark of Christendom after 1370 [PDF p. 358]. The author evaluates the Paleologi dynasty as having protracted the empire's life through base subservience to the Turks long after its justification vanished [PDF p. 358]. During the reign of John Paleologus, the empire suffered from the undutiful behavior of his children, specifically his son Andronicus, who rebelled and was eventually blinded [PDF p. 359]. John subsequently left the throne to his second son, Manuel II [PDF p. 360].

Manuel experienced a brief opportunity for liberation in 1402 when the Tartars defeated Sultan Bayezid, leading to a Turkish civil war [PDF p. 360]. Manuel sold his aid to Prince Suleiman for significant territories, briefly ruling what the author calls an empire [PDF p. 361]. However, the author notes that Europe failed to seize the opportunity to rid itself of the Ottomans because King Sigismund was distracted by a religious war in Bohemia [PDF p. 362]. Following the rise of Murad II, Manuel lost his gains and the empire returned to vassalage [PDF p. 363]. The author describes the final thirty years as a period of unredeemed gloom, characterized by exhaustion, poverty, and the loss of Thessalonica [PDF p. 365, 367].
