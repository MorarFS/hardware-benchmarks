The author notes that Constantine demonstrated prescience by establishing Constantinople as a central fortress for the Balkan Peninsula [PDF p. 61]. Around A.D. 372, the Huns invaded from the north, nearly exterminating the Alans before crushing the Ostrogoths into vassalage [PDF p. 61]. The Huns then pressured the Visigoths, who fled toward the Danube seeking refuge from the "murderous savagery" of the nomads [PDF p. 61, 62]. These Visigoths, described by a historian as "shapeless black collops of flesh" hiding "ferocity of the wild beast," requested entry into the Roman Empire [PDF p. 61].

The author characterizes the Eastern Emperor Valens as a "stupid, cowardly, and avaricious prince" who granted the Goths entry on the condition they surrender arms and provide hostages [PDF p. 62]. However, Roman officials accepted bribes to let the Goths keep weapons, and the governor Lupicinus extorted food from the starving immigrants [PDF p. 62, 63]. This desperation led to a violent uprising and a year of warfare [PDF p. 63, 64]. The author observes that the Goths soon revealed their "old barbarian spirit" through fierce raids [PDF p. 64].

The conflict culminated in 378 A.D. at Adrianople, where the Goths utilized superior cavalry tactics to annihilate Valens' army [PDF p. 65, 66]. The author describes this as the most fearful defeat since Cannae, noting the army was never reorganized on old lines [PDF p. 67]. Although the Goths reached Constantinople, they failed to breach its walls, confirming Constantine's foresight [PDF p. 67].
