The Huns, described as an enormous Tartar horde from beyond the Don and Volga, burst into the lands north of the Euxine around A.D. 372 and moved westward (p. 61). This invasion nearly exterminated the Alans before the Huns crushed the Ostrogoths, forcing them to become vassals (p. 61). The Huns then pressed against the Visigoths, who fled with their families and possessions toward the Danube (p. 61). The author notes that the Visigoths were more civilized and Christian than their eastern brethren, making the prospect of slavery to the Huns intolerable (p. 62). Consequently, the Visigoths sought permission from the Emperor to cross the border (p. 62).

At this time, the Roman Empire was divided between the West, ruled by the general Valentinian, and the East, ruled by the prince Valens (p. 62). The author characterizes Valens as a stupid, cowardly, and avaricious prince who was slothful and timid (p. 62). Valens faced a dilemma regarding the 200,000 desperate Goths: he could either refuse them passage or admit them with an obligation to provide land (p. 62). He chose the latter, requiring the Goths to surrender their arms and provide hostages (p. 62). However, once in Moesia, the Goths retained their weapons through bribes, and the governor Lupicinus extorted food from them at exorbitant prices (p. 63). This desperation led to a violent affray when starving Goths tried to pillage a market, prompting Lupicinus to order the execution of Fritigern and his guests (p. 64).

A year of fighting followed along the Danube, during which the Goths displayed a barbarian spirit that the author suggests was only thinly covered by a veneer of civilization (p. 64). In 378 A.D., Valens personally led an army of 60,000 against the Goths near Adrianople (p. 65). Although the Roman legions were traditionally considered superior to barbarians, the Goths had adopted horse-back fighting (p. 65). The Goths launched a flank attack that rolled up the Roman infantry into a hopeless, suffocating press (p. 66). The author describes the resulting slaughter as the most fearful defeat suffered by a Roman army since Cannae (p. 67). The victorious Goths then besieged Constantinople, but their courage failed when they viewed the city's vast walls and riches (p. 67).

By the seventh century, the Latin tongue was rapidly vanishing in the East as the population learned the Greek of their conquerors (p. 169). The author attributes this to the division of the empire and the fact that Latin-speaking provinces suffered most from barbarian invasions (p. 170). Simultaneously, the empire became more Christian in heart, with the State beginning to suppress moral sin and abolish gladiatorial shows (p. 171). Christianity also modified the institution of slavery by teaching that slaves possessed immortal souls (p. 173). However, the author notes that the rise of ascetic monasticism was positively dangerous, as thousands of men who should have borne the burdens of the State instead retired to save their own souls (p. 174). The author judges this apathetic attitude as a disease on the body politic, as many Christians interpreted barbarian invasions as divine judgments rather than national calamities (p. 175).
