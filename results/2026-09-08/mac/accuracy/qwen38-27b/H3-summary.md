The author characterizes the eighty years following Basil the Macedonian’s death as the empire’s most uneventful period, dominated by the reigns of Leo the Wise and Constantine Porphyrogenitus [PDF p. 241]. While Basil was a capable but uneducated adventurer, his successors were mild, literary men who prioritized writing over military conquest [PDF p. 241]. The author notes that the period 880–960 was relatively peaceful, with external threats limited to vexatious but non-dangerous raids by Russians and Saracens [PDF p. 242]. Leo’s twenty-six-year reign was marked by a failed Bulgarian invasion and the disgraceful 904 Saracen capture of Thessalonica, which the author qualifies as a surprise attack that could have been prevented with better timing [PDF p. 242].

Constantine, who became emperor at seven, spent decades under the regency of Alexander and later the usurper Romanus Lecapenus [PDF p. 243]. He only gained full power near age forty after popular outrage forced Romanus’s sons into monasteries [PDF p. 243]. The author describes Constantine as weak but not tyrannical [PDF p. 243]. Both emperors produced significant literary works. Leo’s *Tactica* provides a detailed picture of the Byzantine army, which the author praises for its modern-like organization, uniformity, and care for wounded soldiers [PDF p. 244]. The author also highlights Leo’s strategic advice, recommending decisive action against eastern foes but caution against western Franks and Lombards [PDF p. 245].

Constantine’s works, including *On the Themes* and *On the Administration of the Empire*, are deemed invaluable for historical data [PDF p. 246]. The author evaluates Constantine as a methodical compiler rather than an originative thinker, noting his focus on court ceremonies compensated for his lack of real power [PDF p. 246]. Finally, the author argues that these emperors’ literary output signals the end of Byzantine intellectual decline, which had persisted since the Heraclian dynasty [PDF p. 247].
