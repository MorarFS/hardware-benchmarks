The author argues that Constantine’s construction of Constantinople proved prescient when the Huns, a Tartar horde, invaded the Balkans around A.D. 372 [PDF p. 61]. The Huns nearly exterminated the Alans and crushed the Ostrogoths, forcing them into vassalage [PDF p. 61]. The Visigoths, led by Fritigern, retreated to the Danube, where they pleaded for asylum from the Roman Emperor [PDF p. 61]. Valens, whom the author describes as stupid and cowardly, permitted their entry in exchange for hostages and surrendered arms [PDF p. 62]. However, Roman officials violated these terms by accepting bribes to let the Goths keep their weapons [PDF p. 63]. Governor Lupicinus further exacerbated tensions by hoarding food and selling it at exorbitant prices, driving the Goths to desperation [PDF p. 63]. A minor affray at Marcianopolis escalated when Lupicinus ordered the assassination of Fritigern, prompting the Goths to revolt [PDF p. 64].

Following a year of destructive raids, Valens personally led an army of 60,000 men against the Goths in 378 [PDF p. 65]. The author notes that the Goths had adopted cavalry tactics, giving them a tactical advantage [PDF p. 65]. At Adrianople, a Gothic cavalry charge broke the Roman flank, causing the infantry to collapse into a dense, confused mass [PDF p. 66]. The resulting slaughter killed forty thousand Romans, including Valens and his senior commanders [PDF p. 67]. The author identifies this as the worst Roman defeat since Cannae [PDF p. 67]. The victorious Goths advanced on Constantinople but abandoned their siege after seeing the city’s formidable walls, confirming the strategic value of Constantine’s fortress [PDF p. 67].
