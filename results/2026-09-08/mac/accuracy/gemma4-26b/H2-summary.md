The Latin language underwent a gradual decay in the East, a process the author attributes to the practical division of the empire and the decimation of Latin-speaking provinces by barbarians [PDF p. 170]. While Latin-speaking populations once comprised a quarter of provincials east of the Adriatic in A.D. 400, they fell to less than a tenth by A.D. 620 [PDF p. 170]. The author notes that Justinian was the final emperor to speak Latin as a mother tongue [PDF p. 169].

Simultaneously, the empire became increasingly Christian, a shift the author evaluates as having profound social consequences [PDF p. 171]. Christianity modified the treatment of vulnerable groups, such as infants and slaves, by emphasizing the importance of the individual soul [PDF p. 172, 173]. However, the author characterizes the rise of ascetic monasticism as a "positively dangerous" development [PDF p. 174]. The author judges that this movement caused many to neglect civic duties in favor of a "repulsively selfish" pursuit of salvation, which hampered the empire's defense against barbarians [PDF p. 175]. While paganism effectively disappeared by the end of the fifth century, the author observes that a widespread indifference prevailed among the educated classes [PDF p. 176].
