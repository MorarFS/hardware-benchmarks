Around A.D. 372, the Huns migrated westward from beyond the Don and Volga, nearly exterminating the Alans before attacking the Goths (61). The Ostrogoths attempted to defend the Dniester but were crushed and forced into vassalage, leaving only a remnant to flee toward the Danube Delta (61). Subsequently, the Huns attacked the Visigoths, who, under Duke Fritigern, retreated toward the Danube with their families and livestock (61). The author notes that the Visigoths found surrender to the Huns more dreadful than their eastern brethren because they were more civilized and Christian, making the prospect of slavery intolerable (62). In A.D. 376, the Visigoths requested permission from the Emperor Valens to cross the river (62). The author characterizes Valens as a stupid, cowardly, and avaricious prince who ruled the East while his brother Valentinian held the West (62). Valens eventually decided to allow the Goths to settle as subject-allies provided they surrendered their arms and gave hostages (63).

Although the Goths crossed the Danube, they refused to disarm in Moesia, offering bribes to Roman officials to retain their weapons (63). Conflict escalated when the governor, Lupicinus, withheld food supplies to sell them at exorbitant prices, driving the Goths to barter slaves and even children for bread (63). This desperation led to a violent affray at Marcianopolis, where Fritigern escaped a Roman attempt to slay him and called his followers to arms (64). Following a year of fighting, the Goths utilized a newly acquired skill for fighting on horseback (65). In A.D. 378, Valens attacked the Gothic camp near Adrianople with 60,000 men, but a Gothic cavalry charge decimated the Roman flanks and infantry (66). The author describes the resulting slaughter as the most fearful defeat for a Roman army since Cannae, noting that the Roman army was almost annihilated and never reorganized on its old lines (67).

The decline of the Latin language in the East was driven by the division of the empire and the subsequent loss of provinces to barbarians (170). By the seventh century, the Roman element was vanishing, and by A.D. 620, less than a tenth of the inhabitants east of the Adriatic spoke Latin (170). Simultaneously, the empire became increasingly Christian, which modified social practices (171). Christianity helped end infanticide and modified the evils of slavery, as seen in Justinian's legislation regarding intermarriage and the criminalization of a master's prostitution of a slave (173). However, the author judges that the rise of ascetic monasticism was "positively dangerous" to the state, as thousands of men abandoned their civic duties to seek individual salvation (174). This apathetic attitude toward national calamities led some to view barbarian invasions as divine judgments (175).

During the ninth and tenth centuries, the empire experienced a period of relative stability under the "literary emperors" Leo the Wise and Constantine Porphyrogenitus (241). Leo's reign was marked by the capture of Thessalonica by Saracen pirates, an incident the author calls disgraceful (242). Constantine VII, after overcoming the usurpation of Romanus Lecapenus, focused on writing, including a manual on court ceremonies (243). The author observes that Constantine's preoccupation with ceremony was characteristic of a peaceful emperor lacking real power (246). By the end of the ninth century, the intellectual destitution that had long prevailed began to end, evidenced by a growing literary class (247).
