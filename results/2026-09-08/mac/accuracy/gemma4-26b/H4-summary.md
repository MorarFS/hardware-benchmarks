The author characterizes the final seventy-five years of the Byzantine Empire as a "mere piece of local history" that no longer significantly impacted Christendom [PDF p. 358]. The narrative begins with the reign of John Paleologus, which the author describes as a "long and unhonoured" period marked by his son Andronicus's rebellions [PDF p. 359]. Following John's death in 1391, Manuel II ascended the throne [PDF p. 359]. Although Manuel showed "some signs of capacity," he lacked the resources to challenge the Turks [PDF p. 360].

A temporary opportunity for liberation arose in 1402 when Timour defeated Sultan Bayezid at Angora, causing Ottoman civil war [PDF p. 360]. Manuel exploited this instability by selling aid to Suleiman in exchange for significant territories [PDF p. 361]. However, the author notes that Europe failed to seize this "unparalleled opportunity" due to the Hungarian King Sigismund's preoccupation with the Hussites and a lack of cooperation between Servians and Magyars [PDF p. 362].

The empire's decline accelerated under Murad II, who besieged Constantinople and eventually forced Manuel to cede most dominions [PDF p. 363]. After Manuel's death, John VI ruled an empire the author describes as being in a state of "exhaustion and debility" [PDF p. 365]. John VI attempted to secure Western aid by converting to Roman Catholicism at the Council of Florence, but the author evaluates this as a failure, as he received only minimal financial and military support from a discredited papacy [PDF p. 367].
