Around A.D. 372, the Huns moved westward, nearly exterminating the Alans and crushing the Ostrogoths [PDF p. 61]. The Visigoths, fleeing the Huns, reached the Danube and requested permission from the Emperor to cross the border [PDF p. 62]. The author characterizes the Eastern Emperor, Valens, as a stupid, cowardly, and avaricious prince [PDF p. 62]. Valens permitted the Goths to settle as subject-allies provided they surrendered their arms and gave hostages [PDF p. 63]. However, Roman officials accepted bribes to allow the Goths to keep their weapons, and the governor Lupicinus engaged in shameless extortion by withholding food [PDF p. 63]. This desperation led to a violent outbreak at Marcianopolis, prompting Fritigern to lead the Goths in war against Rome [PDF p. 63].

In A.D. 378, Valens engaged the Goths near Adrianople [PDF p. 65]. While the Roman infantry was traditionally considered superior, the Goths had adopted horse-back fighting [PDF p. 65]. A Gothic cavalry charge crushed the Roman flanks and compressed the infantry into a helpless, suffocating mass [PDF p. 66]. The battle resulted in the death of Valens and forty thousand men, an event the author describes as a fearful defeat comparable to Cannae [PDF p. 66, 67]. Although the Goths approached Constantinople, they failed to attack the city walls [PDF p. 67].
