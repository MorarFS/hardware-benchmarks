The author argues that the final seventy-five years of the Byzantine Empire were merely local history, no longer central to Christendom [PDF p. 358]. He asserts that the Paleologi prolonged the state’s life through subservience to the Turks, even after its justification had vanished [PDF p. 358]. The author contends that an earlier fall in 1370 would have had minimal impact, aside from straining Venetian and Genoese commerce and slightly diminishing the Renaissance [PDF p. 358-359].

During John Paleologus’s reign, his son Andronicus rebelled, leading to a failed blinding and John’s decision to pass the throne to Manuel II [PDF p. 359]. Manuel II, though capable, lacked resources to resist the Ottomans [PDF p. 360]. However, the Tartar invasion of 1402 and the subsequent Ottoman civil war provided a rare opportunity [PDF p. 360]. Manuel sold his aid to Sultan Suleiman, gaining Thessalonica and other territories [PDF p. 361-362]. The author notes that Europe missed this chance to expel the Ottomans because King Sigismund was preoccupied with the Hussites, and Serbs and Hungarians lacked cooperation [PDF p. 362-363].

When Mohammed I reunified the Ottoman realm in 1421, Manuel retained his gains [PDF p. 362]. However, his son Murad II later besieged Constantinople, forcing Manuel to cede most of his lands in 1422 [PDF p. 363-365]. Manuel died in 1425, leaving behind a state of exhaustion and poverty [PDF p. 365-366]. His son John VI. ruled passively, losing Thessalonica to the Ottomans in 1430 after his brother sold it to Venice [PDF p. 366-367]. John VI. then traveled to Italy, converting to Catholicism in 1439 to seek aid, but received only minor financial and military support from a discredited papacy [PDF p. 367].
