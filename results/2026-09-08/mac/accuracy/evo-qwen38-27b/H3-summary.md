The author characterizes the eighty years following Basil the Macedonian’s death as the most uneventful period in imperial history, dominated by the reigns of Leo the Wise and Constantine Porphyrogenitus [PDF p. 241]. While Basil was a capable but uneducated adventurer, his successors were mild, literary men who preferred the pen to the sword [PDF p. 241]. The author notes that the period 880–960 was relatively peaceful because the Caliphate was breaking up, the Carolingian empire had collapsed, and northern neighbors were converting to Christianity [PDF p. 242]. Consequently, Leo and Constantine remained in the palace, avoiding military campaigns despite opportunities for conquest [PDF p. 242]. Leo’s reign was marked by a failed invasion of Bulgaria and the disgraceful Saracen capture of Thessalonica in 904, which the author qualifies as a surprise attack that could have been prevented with better timing [PDF p. 242]. Constantine, who became emperor at seven, spent decades under the regency of Alexander and later Romanus Lecapenus [PDF p. 243]. He only gained full power after the popular revolt against Romanus’s sons, and the author describes him as weak but not tyrannical [PDF p. 243]. Their primary achievements were literary. Leo’s manual on the Art of War provides a detailed picture of the Byzantine army, which the author praises for its organization and care for wounded soldiers [PDF p. 244]. The author also highlights Leo’s tactical advice, noting his confidence against eastern foes but caution against western enemies [PDF p. 245]. Constantine’s works, particularly those on themes and administration, are deemed invaluable to historians [PDF p. 246]. The author concludes that these emperors’ literary output signals the end of intellectual destitution, marking a gradual recovery of Byzantine culture from the dark ages of the seventh and eighth centuries [PDF p. 247].
