The author argues that the Byzantine Empire's decline after 1370 was inevitable.  Murad I's failure to capture Constantinople in 1370 did not significantly alter the course of events in Eastern Europe because the empire had already lost its function as a bulwark against the Ottomans. The author states that the Paleologi emperors, through their subservience to the Turks, prolonged the empire's existence beyond its natural lifespan.  

John Paleologus' reign was marked by his son Andronicus's rebellion and the subsequent blinding of the son.  John's later years were spent trying to secure his succession, ultimately choosing his second son Manuel over Andronicus. Manuel, despite his limited resources, saw an opportunity to regain lost territory during the Ottoman civil war following Timur's defeat of Bayezid I in 1402. Manuel allied with Suleiman, one of Bayezid's sons, and regained control of Thessalonica and other territories. However, this success was short-lived.  

The author notes that Europe missed a chance to expel the Ottomans during this period of instability.  Sigismund of Hungary, who could have led a crusade, was preoccupied with the Hussite Wars in Bohemia.  By 1421, Mohammed, Bayezid's youngest son, had unified the Ottoman Empire and turned his attention back to Constantinople.  Manuel's attempt to support rival claimants to the Ottoman throne against Mohammed backfired, leading to a siege of Constantinople in 1422.  The city was saved, but Manuel was forced to cede most of his remaining territories.  

John VI, Manuel's successor, reigned peacefully but ineffectually.  He attempted to secure aid from the West by converting to Roman Catholicism and attending the Councils of Ferrara and Florence.  However, the papacy was weak and unable to offer meaningful assistance.  The author concludes that John VI's reign was marked by the loss of Thessalonica to the Ottomans in 1430, highlighting the empire's continued decline.
