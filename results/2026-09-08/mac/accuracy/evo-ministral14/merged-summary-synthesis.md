The Huns, a large Tartar horde from beyond the Don and Volga, invaded the lands north of the Euxine around A.D. 372, nearly exterminating the Alans and then attacking the Ostrogoths, who were described by a Gothic historian as having "faces that can hardly be called faces rather shapeless black collops of flesh with little points instead of eyes" and being "little in stature, but lithe and active, skilful in riding, broad shouldered, good at the bow, stiff-necked and proud" [PDF p. 61]. The Ostrogoths were crushed and became vassals of the Huns, except for a remnant who fled southward to the Wallachian shore. The Huns then fell on the Visigoths, who, under their Duke Fritigern, retreated to the Danube, where they asked the Roman Emperor for permission to cross [PDF p. 61-62]. The Roman Empire was divided at this time, with the East ruled by Valens, a "stupid, cowardly, and avaricious prince" [PDF p. 62]. Valens, faced with the desperate Visigoths, chose to allow them to cross the Danube and settle within the empire, provided they gave hostages and surrendered their arms. The Goths accepted the terms, but disputes arose when the Roman officials, including Lupicinus, extorted food and supplies from them, leading to starvation and desperation among the Goths [PDF p. 62-63]. A chance affray at Marcianopolis, where Lupicinus ordered the seizure and slaying of Fritigern and his nobles, sparked a year of desperate fighting along the Danube and the northern slope of the Balkans. The Goths, half-starved and enraged, showed their old barbarian spirit, sacking towns and harrying the countryside. Despite initial setbacks, the Roman armies held the Goths at bay, though the struggle was fierce and involved runaway slaves and outcasts among the provincial population [PDF p. 64]. The following year, Emperor Valens, driven into the field by the clamors of his subjects, led a splendid army of 60,000 men against the Goths. However, the Goths, who had adopted cavalry tactics during their sojourn in the plains of South Russia and Roumania, ambushed the Roman army at Adrianople. The Roman left flank was overwhelmed, and the infantry was pushed into hopeless confusion. The Roman cavalry fled, leaving the infantry to be cut down. Forty thousand men fell, including the Emperor, the Grand Masters of the Infantry and Cavalry, the Count of the Palace, and thirty-five commanders of different corps [PDF p. 65-67]. The battle of Adrianople was a catastrophic defeat for the Roman army, comparable to the battle of Cannae. The army of the East was almost annihilated and never reorganized on the old Roman lines. The victorious Goths, after a vain assault on Adrianople, pressed rapidly towards Constantinople but ultimately retreated, realizing the city's vast defenses were inaccessible. Thus, Constantine's foresight in building his fortress-capital was vindicated, though it faced its first attack forty years after his death [PDF p. 67].

The decline of Latin as a dominant language in the Eastern Roman Empire is evident from the seventh century onward, marking a shift from the language of the ruling race to Greek. This transition is illustrated by figures like Ammianus Marcellinus, who, despite being born in Greece, wrote in Latin, the language of the ruling race. By the sixth century, Latin proficiency had become rare even among educated men, with Johannes Lydus attributing his career advancement to his rare skill in Latin, while Procopius, a notable writer, struggled with the language. The last emperor to speak Latin as his mother tongue was Justinian, with his successors being more fluent in Greek [PDF p. 169-170]. The decline of Latin was accelerated by the division of the empire and the barbarian invasions, which decimated Latin-speaking populations in the Balkans. By A.D. 620, only a tenth of the population east of the Adriatic spoke Latin, with the remaining Latin speakers concentrated in Africa and the Italian Exarchate. The last notable Latin author was Flavius Corippus, an African, who wrote a panegyric to Justinus II [PDF p. 170]. Simultaneously, the empire became more Christian, with the state assuming a more active role in suppressing moral and legal crimes. The practice of infanticide was criminalized, and slavery was reformed, with Justinian's legislation reflecting a more humane approach. However, the rise of ascetic monasticism, which spread rapidly, had negative consequences for the state, as it led to a neglect of civic duties and an apathetic attitude towards national calamities. This was contrasted by the heathen minority, which accused Christianity of being anti-social. Despite this, the governing classes continued to uphold Roman traditions, fighting against barbarian invasions, famine, and pestilence [PDF p. 171-176].

The eighty years following Basil the Macedonian’s death (A.D. 880–960) were marked by the uneventful reigns of Leo the Wise and his grandson Constantine Porphyrogenitus, both of whom were described by the author as mild, unenterprising men of literature rather than strong rulers [PDF p. 241]. The period was relatively peaceful, with the empire facing only minor threats such as Russian and Saracen pirate raids, rather than large-scale foreign wars [PDF p. 242]. Leo’s reign was notable for a failed Bulgarian invasion and the Saracen capture of Thessalonica in 904, though the author suggests the city’s fall was due to surprise rather than the empire’s weakness [PDF p. 242]. Constantine, who inherited the throne at age seven, faced prolonged guardianship and later a usurpation by Romanus Lecapenus, whose sons were overthrown by popular unrest, allowing Constantine to rule for twenty years [PDF p. 243]. Both emperors left significant literary works. Leo’s *Tactica* provided detailed military organization, tactics, and strategy, reflecting the Byzantine army’s strength and discipline, particularly against non-Western enemies [PDF p. 244–245]. Constantine’s works, including *On the Themes* and *On the Administration of the Empire*, offered valuable historical and administrative insights, though the author notes his preference for ceremonial and detail over originality [PDF p. 246–247]. The author also credits them with revitalizing Byzantine literature, which had declined during the earlier Isaurian dynasty but flourished under their rule [PDF p. 247].

The final decades of the Byzantine Empire, from 1370 to 1453, marked a period of decline and irrelevance in the broader history of Christendom, according to the author. The empire, once a bulwark against the Ottoman advance, had lost its strategic significance by 1370, with its survival prolonged only by the subservience of the Paleologi dynasty to the Turks [PDF p. 358]. John Paleologus, the aging emperor, faced internal strife from his son Andronicus, who rebelled twice and was blinded—though not effectively—by his father, following the example set by the Ottoman Sultan Murad I [PDF p. 359]. John’s death in 1391 passed the throne to his second son, Manuel II, who ruled over a diminished empire of Constantinople, Thessalonica, and the Peloponnesus, lacking the resources to resist Ottoman dominance [PDF p. 360]. A brief opportunity arose in 1402 when the Ottoman Empire fractured due to civil wars following the defeat of Sultan Bayezid by Tamerlane. Manuel exploited this chaos, selling his military support to Suleiman, an Ottoman pretender, in exchange for territorial gains, including Thessalonica and Black Sea ports [PDF p. 361]. However, Mohammed, the youngest son of Bayezid, reunified the Ottomans by 1421, leaving Manuel’s empire once again in a state of vassalage [PDF p. 362]. The author notes that Europe missed a critical chance to expel the Ottomans, as Hungary’s focus on the Hussite Wars prevented a coordinated effort [PDF p. 363]. Manuel’s later years saw renewed Ottoman aggression under Murad II, culminating in a failed siege of Constantinople in 1422, where the city’s walls held despite advanced Ottoman weaponry [PDF p. 364]. Manuel died in 1425, leaving his son John VI to rule over a further weakened empire. John’s reign was marked by stagnation, with Constantinople in ruins, its population impoverished, and its military strength reduced to mercenaries [PDF p. 366]. The empire’s final humiliation came in 1430 when John’s brother Andronicus sold Thessalonica to the Venetians, leading to its annexation by the Ottomans [PDF p. 367]. John’s desperate attempt to secure Western aid by converting to Catholicism in 1439 yielded little, as the papacy was weakened and unable to support the empire meaningfully [PDF p. 367].
