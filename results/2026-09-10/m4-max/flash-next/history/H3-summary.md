The eighty years following Basil the Macedonian’s death were the most uneventful in imperial history, occupied entirely by the reigns of Leo the Wise and Constantine Porphyrogenitus [PDF p. 241]. These successors were easy-going literary men who devoted themselves to writing rather than military enterprise [PDF p. 241]. The period 880–960 was less troubled by foreign wars than any other, with only vexatious raids from Russians and Saracens [PDF p. 242]. Leo’s reign was diversified only by a failed Bulgarian invasion and a disgraceful Saracen raid on Thessalonica [PDF p. 242]. Constantine, Leo’s grandson, was seven when he inherited the throne and spent years under guardians, including the usurper Romanus Lecapenus [PDF p. 243]. Popular wrath eventually swept away Romanus’s sons, allowing Constantine to administer the empire for twenty years [PDF p. 243]. Leo’s chief achievement was a manual on the Art of War, which provided a perfect picture of the Byzantine army and advised decisive action against most foes, excepting the Franks and Lombards [PDF p. 244]. The fleet’s main object was policing seas, though it could not suppress petty piracy [PDF p. 245]. Constantine’s works were more interesting, including a biography of Basil and a manual on Court Ceremonies [PDF p. 246]. He was a careful compiler rather than an originative genius [PDF p. 247]. The author notes that the darkest age in literary history was 600–750, but by the ninth century, a larger literary class existed [PDF p. 247].
