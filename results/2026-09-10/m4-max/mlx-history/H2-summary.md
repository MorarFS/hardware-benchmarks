The passage discusses the decline of the Latin language in the Eastern Roman Empire, attributing it to the growing influence of Greek and the fragmentation of the empire. The author notes that by the seventh century, the Latin element was vanishing, as evidenced by the Emperor's shift from Latin to Greek and the decline of Latin-speaking officials. The author attributes this decline to the practical disconnection between Rome and the East following the division of the empire and the barbarian invasions, which disproportionately affected Latin-speaking regions. The author cites the example of Flavius Corippus, the last notable Latin author, as an African, highlighting the shift. The passage also touches on the transformation of the empire's character, noting its increasing Christianity and the impact of Christian teachings on social practices such as the abolition of infanticide and the reevaluation of slavery. The author further discusses the effects of Christianity on the state, including the rise of monasticism and its potential negative impact on civic duty. The author attributes the empire's survival to the persistence of Roman statecraft and the decline of paganism by the end of the fifth century, though a form of indifference persisted among the educated classes.
