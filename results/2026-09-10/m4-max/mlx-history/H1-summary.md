The passage begins by highlighting the unexpected events that validated Constantine's foresight in establishing Constantinople as the central military hub of the Balkans. In 372 AD, the Huns, a vast horde from beyond the Don and Volga, invaded the region, overwhelming the Alans and then the Ostrogoths. The Ostrogoths, after a desperate defense, were forced to become vassals of the Huns, while a remnant fled to the Danube. The Huns then turned on the Visigoths, who, under Duke Fritigern, retreated to the Danube, facing the prospect of slavery to the Huns. [PDF p. 61]

The Visigoths, now desperate, sought permission to cross the Danube from the Emperor. At this time, the Roman Empire was divided, with Valens ruling the East and Valentinian the West. Valens, described as stupid, cowardly, and avaricious, hesitated to admit the Goths, fearing the consequences of both allowing them in and turning them away. Eventually, he agreed to let them cross under certain conditions, including the surrender of hostages and arms. [PDF p. 62]

However, tensions quickly arose. The Goths, facing hunger and extortion by Roman officials, rebelled. A skirmish at Marcianopolis led to the Goths turning against Rome, sparking a year of fierce fighting along the Danube and the Balkans. Despite the Goths' hardships, they managed to inflict significant damage, echoing the raids of the third century. [PDF p. 63]

The conflict escalated when Valens personally led an army against the Goths in 378 AD, but the battle of Adrianople resulted in a catastrophic defeat for the Romans. The Goths, now fighting on horseback, overwhelmed the Roman infantry, leading to the death of Valens and many Roman commanders. This defeat marked a turning point, as the Eastern Roman army was never reorganized on its former lines. [PDF p. 64-67]
