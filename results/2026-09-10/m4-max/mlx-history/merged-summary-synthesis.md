**Excerpt 1 (p. 61-67):** The text begins by describing the invasion of the Huns into the Balkans in 372 AD, which overwhelmed the Alans and Ostrogoths. The Ostrogoths, after a desperate defense, became vassals of the Huns, while a remnant fled to the Danube. The Huns then turned on the Visigoths, who, under Duke Fritigern, retreated to the Danube, facing the prospect of slavery. The author attributes the Visigoths' retreat to the Huns' overwhelming power and the Visigoths' desperation. The author also notes the Roman Empire's division, with Valens ruling the East and Valentinian the West, and describes Valens as "stupid, cowardly, and avaricious," highlighting his hesitation in allowing the Goths to cross the Danube. The author attributes the subsequent conflict to the Goths' hunger and extortion by Roman officials, leading to a rebellion and a year of fierce fighting along the Danube and Balkans. The author describes the catastrophic defeat of the Romans at Adrianople in 378 AD, where Valens was killed, marking a turning point in the empire's military organization.

**Excerpt 2 (p. 169-176):** The passage discusses the decline of Latin in the Eastern Roman Empire, attributing it to the growing influence of Greek and the fragmentation of the empire. The author notes that by the seventh century, the Latin element was vanishing, as evidenced by the Emperor's shift from Latin to Greek and the decline of Latin-speaking officials. The author attributes this decline to the practical disconnection between Rome and the East following the division of the empire and the barbarian invasions, which disproportionately affected Latin-speaking regions. The author cites the example of Flavius Corippus, the last notable Latin author, as an African, highlighting the shift. The passage also touches on the transformation of the empire's character, noting its increasing Christianity and the impact of Christian teachings on social practices such as the abolition of infanticide and the reevaluation of slavery. The author further discusses the effects of Christianity on the state, including the rise of monasticism and its potential negative impact on civic duty. The author attributes the empire's survival to the persistence of Roman statecraft and the decline of paganism by the end of the fifth century, though a form of indifference persisted among the educated classes.

**Excerpt 3 (p. 358-367):** The passage discusses the decline of the Byzantine Empire from 1370 to 1453, emphasizing its diminishing role in Christendom. The author notes that the fall of Constantinople in 1370 would not have significantly altered the course of events in Eastern Europe, as the empire had already ceased to function as the "bulwark of Christendom against the Ottomite." The author attributes the prolonged existence of the empire to the Paleologi's subservience to the Turks, which the author judges as "base." The text then outlines the reign of John Paleologus, whose long and troubled rule was marked by the rebellions of his children, particularly his son Andronicus, who twice attempted to seize the throne. Manuel II, who succeeded John, ruled over a diminished empire, with limited military and financial resources. The author evaluates his reign as "above the average of the Paleologi" but notes the limited scope of his authority. A turning point came in 1402 with the Ottoman civil war, which the author describes as an opportunity for the empire to regain independence. Manuel exploited this by allying with the Ottoman contender Mohammed, securing territories in Europe and Asia. However, the author criticizes the failure of European powers, particularly Hungary, to capitalize on this opportunity. The passage concludes with the reign of Manuel II, who died in 1448, marking the end of the last independent Byzantine sovereign. The author describes the empire's state as one of "unredeemed gloom," noting its economic and military decline. The text also details the efforts of John VI to seek Western aid, culminating in his conversion to Roman Catholicism and attendance at the Council of Florence in 1439, which the author judges as a largely unsuccessful endeavor.
