The author argues that Constantine’s foresight in building Constantinople proved prescient when the Huns invaded around A.D. 372, crushing the Alans and Ostrogoths before threatening the Visigoths [PDF p. 61]. Driven by fear of Hunnic savagery, the Visigoths under Fritigern begged Emperor Valens for refuge in A.D. 376 [PDF p. 62]. The author characterizes Valens as a stupid and cowardly ruler who ultimately permitted the Goths to cross the Danube as subject-allies, provided they surrendered hostages and arms [PDF p. 63]. However, Roman officials accepted bribes to let the Goths keep their weapons, while Governor Lupicinus withheld food and demanded exorbitant prices, driving the starving refugees to desperation [PDF p. 63]. This extortion sparked a revolt after a violent clash at Marcianopolis, leading to a year of fierce fighting where the Goths, now adopting cavalry tactics, raided the Balkans [PDF p. 64]. In A.D. 378, Valens personally led a massive army against the Goths near Adrianople, expecting a routine victory based on the legions’ historic reputation [PDF p. 65]. The author notes that the Goths had become skilled horsemen, and their sudden cavalry charge shattered the Roman lines, causing a chaotic press that killed forty thousand men and the Emperor himself [PDF p. 66]. Although the Goths subsequently attacked Constantinople, the author states their courage failed before the city’s massive walls, validating Constantine’s earlier strategic planning [PDF p. 67].
