The author argues that the Byzantine Empire ceased to be a vital bulwark for Christendom after 1370, a duty that shifted to Servians and Hungarians [PDF p. 358]. The Paleologi family prolonged the empire's existence through base subservience to the Turks [PDF p. 358]. Emperor John Paleologus faced internal strife when his son Andronicus rebelled and allied with Murad I's son, leading to Andronicus being blinded before he rebelled again [PDF p. 359]. Upon John's death in 1391, the throne passed to his second son, Manuel II, who possessed limited dominions and resources [PDF p. 359]. A brief opportunity for liberation arose in 1402 when Tamerlane defeated Sultan Bayezid, causing Ottoman civil war [PDF p. 360]. Manuel capitalized on this by selling aid to pretenders, regaining territories like Thessalonica and Black Sea ports [PDF p. 361]. However, Mohammed the Unifier eventually reunited the Ottoman realm by 1421, and Manuel's alliance with him preserved his gains temporarily [PDF p. 362]. Europe failed to exploit the Ottoman weakness because Sigismund of Hungary was distracted by Hussite wars, and no crusade was formed due to Serb-Magyar animosity [PDF p. 362]. When Murad II attacked in 1422, Manuel's support for pretenders failed, and the city survived only due to a miraculous vision and internal Ottoman trouble [PDF p. 363]. Manuel died in 1425, leaving a gloomy legacy to his son John VI, who ruled a depleted empire where commerce belonged to Italians and walls were in ruins [PDF p. 365]. John VI lost Thessalonica to the Ottomans in 1430 after his brother sold it to Venice [PDF p. 366]. Finally, John traveled to Florence in 1438 to seek aid by converting to Catholicism, but received only minimal military support because Pope Eugenius IV was politically weak [PDF p. 366].
