The text begins by documenting the decay of Latin in the East, noting that Ammianus Marcellinus wrote in the ruling tongue despite his Greek origins [PDF p. 169]. By the seventh century, the Roman element vanished as emperors adopted Greek titles and officials like Procopius became ignorant of Latin [PDF p. 169]. The author attributes this linguistic shift to the political division of the empire and the devastation of Latin-speaking Balkan provinces by Goths and Slavs [PDF p. 170]. Consequently, only remote African and Italian territories retained significant Latin populations, while the last native Latin author, Flavius Corippus, was an African [PDF p. 170].

Simultaneously, the empire grew more Christian, though administrative machinery remained pagan until forty years after Constantine's death [PDF p. 171]. The author evaluates the abolition of gladiatorial shows as the result of a single monk's martyrdom rather than state policy [PDF p. 171]. Christianity also ended infanticide and improved slavery by teaching the spiritual equality of all souls [PDF p. 173]. However, the author judges that this focus on individual salvation created dangerous apathy, as monks abandoned civic duties during barbarian invasions [PDF p. 174]. This attitude led pagans to blame Christianity for the state's ruin, prompting Orosius to defend the faith [PDF p. 176]. Although paganism disappeared as an active force by 529, the author observes that many educated men remained nominal Christians with indifferent lives [PDF p. 176].
