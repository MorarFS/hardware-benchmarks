The eighty years following the death of Basil the Macedonian were the most monotonous in the empire's history [PDF p. 241]. The author characterizes the era by the reigns of Leo the Wise and Constantine Porphyrogenitus, describing them as easy-going, unenterprising men of literature who were a strange issue from the "upstart" Basil [PDF p. 241]. During the period of 880–960, the empire faced few foreign wars, experiencing only vexatious but non-dangerous piratical raids from Russians and Saracens [PDF p. 242]. Leo's twenty-six-year reign included an unsuccessful invasion of Bulgaria and a "disgraceful" Saracen raid on Thessalonica in 904 [PDF p. 242].

Constantine VII ascended the throne at age seven under various guardians, including the usurper Romanus Lecapenus, who kept the rightful heir in the background until Constantine reached his majority [PDF p. 243]. Constantine eventually regained power after a popular uprising against the sons of Romanus [PDF p. 243]. The author evaluates the primary achievements of both emperors as their books [PDF p. 244]. Leo's military manual provides a detailed picture of a highly organized Byzantine army and fleet [PDF p. 244, 246]. Constantine's works, including treatises on administration and court ceremonies, reveal him to be a methodical compiler [PDF p. 246]. The author concludes that these literary emperors signaled an end to previous intellectual destitution [PDF p. 247].
