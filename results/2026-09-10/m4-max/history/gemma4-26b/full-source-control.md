Around A.D. 372, the Huns migrated westward from beyond the Don and Volga, nearly exterminating the Alans before attacking the Goths (61). The author notes that the Ostrogoths attempted to defend the Dniester against these "savages," whom a Gothic historian described as having "shapeless black collops of flesh" rather than faces (61). The Ostrogoths were eventually crushed and forced into vassalage, leaving a remnant to flee toward the Danube Delta (61). Subsequently, the Huns attacked the Visigoths, who, under Duke Fritigern, retreated toward the Danube (61). The author observes that the Visigoths found surrender to the Huns more dreadful than their eastern brethren because they were more civilized and Christian, making the prospect of slavery intolerable (62). In A.D. 376, the Visigoths requested permission from the Emperor Valens to cross the river (62). The author characterizes Valens as a "stupid, cowardly, and avaricious prince" who ruled the East while his brother Valentinian held the West (62). Valens eventually decided to allow the Goths to settle as subject-allies if they surrendered their arms and provided hostages (63).

Although the Goths accepted these terms, tensions rose in Moesia when Roman officials failed to disarm them and instead accepted bribes to let them keep their weapons (63). Furthermore, the governor Lupicinus is reported to have withheld food supplies to extort high prices, driving the Goths to desperation and the sale of children for food (63). This led to a conflict in Marcianopolis where, following a skirmish between starving Goths and Roman soldiers, Lupicinus attempted to slay Fritigern at a banquet (64). Fritigern escaped and raised an army, leading to a year of fighting characterized by the author as a repetition of third-century raids (64). While Roman armies initially checked the Visigoths, the conflict escalated when Valens personally led reinforcements into the field (65). In A.D. 378, the Goths utilized cavalry tactics learned in Russia to launch a flank attack at Adrianople (65). The resulting battle was a "fearful disaster" that the author compares to the slaughter at Cannae, resulting in the death of Valens and forty thousand men (66, 67). Although the Goths approached Constantinople, they failed to take the city due to its vast walls (67).

The decline of the Latin language in the East was driven by the division of the empire and the subsequent loss of Latin-speaking provinces to barbarian incursions and Slavic migrations (170). By the seventh century, the Roman element was vanishing, and by A.D. 620, less than a tenth of the inhabitants east of the Adriatic spoke Latin (169, 170). Simultaneously, the empire became increasingly Christian, a shift that the author suggests modified social practices, such as the end of infanticide and the modification of slavery (173). However, the author judges that the rise of ascetic monasticism was "positively dangerous" to the state, as it led thousands of men to abandon their civic duties to focus on individual salvation (174). This apathetic attitude toward national calamities was viewed by the surviving pagan minority as the ruin of the state (176).

Between A.D. 880 and 960, the empire experienced a period of relative stability under the "literary emperors" Leo VI and Constantine VII (242). These rulers were described as "easy-going, and inoffensive men of literature" who focused on writing rather than military conquest (242). Leo's *Tactica* provided a detailed account of Byzantine military organization, including the use of heavy cavalry and specialized medical trains (244, 245). Constantine VII produced various administrative and ceremonial manuals, though the author notes he was a "methodical compiler" rather than an original thinker (246). Despite this intellectual recovery, the later empire faced terminal decline (247). By the time of John Paleologus, the empire was characterized by extreme exhaustion, with Constantinople's population reduced to one hundred thousand and much of its wealth controlled by Italians (340). John's attempt to secure Western aid through conversion to Roman Catholicism at the Council of Florence yielded only minimal military and financial support (341).
