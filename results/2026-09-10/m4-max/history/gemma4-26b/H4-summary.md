The author characterizes the final seventy-five years of the Byzantine Empire as a "mere piece of local history" that no longer significantly impacted Christendom [PDF p. 358]. The narrative begins with the reign of John Paleologus, which the author describes as a "long and unhonoured" period marked by his son Andronicus's rebellions [PDF p. 359]. Following John's death in 1391, Manuel II ascended the throne [PDF p. 359]. Although Manuel showed "some signs of capacity," he lacked the resources to challenge the Turks [PDF p. 360].

A temporary opportunity for liberation arose in 1402 when Timour defeated Sultan Bayezid at Angora, causing Ottoman civil war [PDF p. 360]. Manuel exploited this instability by selling aid to Suleiman for a "heavy price," briefly regaining significant territories [PDF p. 361]. However, the author notes that Mohammed, a "man of genius," eventually reunited the Ottoman realm by 1421 [PDF p. 362]. After Manuel's death, his son John VI presided over a period of "unredeemed gloom" [PDF p. 365]. The author describes a city in "exhaustion and debility," characterized by ruins, poverty, and a population of one hundred thousand [PDF p. 366]. John VI attempted to secure Western aid by converting to Roman Catholicism at the Council of Florence in 1439, but the author evaluates this as a failure, as he received only minimal money and three hundred mercenaries [PDF p. 367].
