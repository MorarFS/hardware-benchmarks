Around A.D. 372, the Huns moved westward, nearly exterminating the Alans and crushing the Ostrogoths [PDF p. 61]. The Visigoths, fleeing the Huns, reached the Danube and requested permission from the Emperor to cross the border [PDF p. 62]. The author characterizes the Eastern Emperor, Valens, as a stupid, cowardly, and avaricious prince [PDF p. 62]. Valens permitted the Goths to settle as subject-allies provided they surrendered their arms and gave hostages [PDF p. 63]. However, Roman officials accepted bribes to allow the Goths to keep their weapons, and the governor Lupicinus engaged in shameless extortion by withholding food [PDF p. 63]. This desperation led to a conflict at Marcianopolis, after which Fritigern led the Goths in a year of fighting against Rome [PDF p. 63].

In A.D. 378, Valens personally led an army against the Goths near Adrianople [PDF p. 65]. The author notes that while Roman infantry were traditionally superior, the Goths had adopted horse-back fighting [PDF p. 65]. During the battle, a Gothic cavalry charge crushed the Roman flanks and infantry, resulting in the death of forty thousand men and the Emperor [PDF p. 66]. The author describes this as the most fearful Roman defeat since Cannae [PDF p. 67]. Although the Goths approached Constantinople, they failed to attack the city walls and retreated [PDF p. 67].
