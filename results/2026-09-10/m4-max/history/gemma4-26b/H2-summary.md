The Latin language underwent a gradual decay in the East, a process the author attributes to the practical division of the empire and the decimation of Latin-speaking provinces by barbarians [PDF p. 170]. While Latin-speaking populations once comprised a quarter of provincials east of the Adriatic in A.D. 400, they fell to less than a tenth by A.D. 620 [PDF p. 170]. The author notes that Justinian was the final emperor to speak Latin as a mother tongue [PDF p. 169].

Simultaneously, the empire became increasingly Christian, a shift the author evaluates as having profoundly modified social life [PDF p. 171]. Christianity ended infanticide through state intervention and modified slavery by recognizing the slave as a human with an immortal soul [PDF p. 172, 173]. However, the author offers a critical judgment of ascetic monasticism, labeling it a "positively dangerous" development that caused men to neglect their civic duties [PDF p. 174]. This "repulsively selfish" focus on individual salvation led many to view barbarian invasions as divine judgments rather than national calamities [PDF p. 175]. Ultimately, while paganism practically disappeared by the end of the fifth century, the author observes that widespread indifference prevailed among the educated classes [PDF p. 176].
