**Beginning (ca. A.D. 372-376)**: The Huns, a massive Tartar horde, invaded lands north of the Euxine, decimating the Alans and then targeting the Goths [PDF p. 61]. The Ostrogoths were crushed, becoming Hun vassals, while a Visigothic remnant fled to the Danube's Wallachian shore [PDF p. 61]. Pressed by the Huns, the Visigoths, under Duke Fritigern, sought permission from the Roman Emperor Valens to cross the Danube, promising loyalty in exchange for refuge [PDF p. 62].

**Middle (A.D. 376)**: Valens, described as "stupid, cowardly, and avaricious" [PDF p. 62], reluctantly agreed, demanding hostages and disarmament. However, Roman officials, notably Governor Lupicinus, exploited the Goths, leading to starvation and desperation [PDF p. 63]. Tensions escalated, culminating in a deadly affray at Marcianopolis, where Goths, including Fritigern, fought back, sparking open rebellion [PDF p. 63].

**Ending (A.D. 378)**: The ensuing war saw the Goths, now mounted warriors, clash with Roman armies along the Danube and Balkans [PDF p. 64-65]. Valens's personal intervention ended in disaster at the Battle of Adrianople, where Gothic cavalry decimated the Roman army, killing Valens and annihilating nearly 40,000 men [PDF p. 66-67]. The Goths then marched towards Constantinople but were awed by its formidable walls, abandoning their assault [PDF p. 67]. The author evaluates this sequence as a testament to Constantine's foresight in building the fortified capital, which withstood the Goths' advance [PDF p. 67]. This defeat marked a significant blow to Roman military prestige and highlighted the growing challenge posed by barbarian groups.
