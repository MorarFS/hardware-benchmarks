**Beginning (Late Antiquity, East Roman Empire)**: The Latin language was once prevalent in the Balkan peninsula, but by the seventh century, it was rapidly vanishing (PDF p. 169). Although Roman titles and the Emperor's style initially persisted, little more than the names survived (PDF p. 169). The last emperor to speak Latin as his mother tongue was Justinian, with all his successors being more skilled in Greek (PDF p. 169). This decline was partly due to the division of the empire and the increasing predominance of Greek in the East (PDF p. 170).

**Middle (5th-6th centuries, Social and Religious Shifts)**: As the empire lost its Roman character, it grew more Christian (PDF p. 171). Christianity significantly impacted social life, ending practices like infanticide (PDF p. 172) and modifying the evils of slavery (PDF p. 173). However, the rise of ascetic monasticism, which started in Egypt and spread throughout the empire, had detrimental effects on the state (PDF p. 174). Thousands of men, who should have been contributing to the state, retired into monasteries or hermit's caves, focusing solely on saving their own souls (PDF p. 174). This trend was not only unhelpful to society but also posed a danger to the state by reducing the number of active citizens.

**Ending (6th century, Consequences and Legacy)**: The apathetic attitude of many Christians towards the empire's afflictions, viewing them as divine judgment rather than national calamities, was met with criticism from the remaining pagan minority (PDF p. 175). Despite this, the governing classes continued to fight against the empire's ills, preserving Roman statecraft traditions (PDF p. 175). By the end of the fifth century, paganism had practically disappeared as an active force, with Justinian formally ending the teaching of pagan philosophy in 529 (PDF p. 176). However, many in the sixth century, particularly among the literary and official classes, were Christians in name only, with little genuine Christian influence on their lives (PDF p. 176). The author evaluates this period as one where the empire faced significant challenges, both from external pressures and internal shifts in societal values and religious beliefs.
