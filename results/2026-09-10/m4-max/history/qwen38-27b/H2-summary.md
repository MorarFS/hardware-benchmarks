The passage begins by detailing the decline of Latin in the Eastern Empire. The author notes that while Greek was more familiar to many, a large Latin-speaking population remained in the Balkans until the seventh century [PDF p. 169]. By then, Roman titles persisted in name only, and Latin knowledge became rare even among educated men, as evidenced by Johannes Lydus and Procopius [PDF p. 169]. Justinian was the last emperor to speak Latin natively [PDF p. 169]. The author attributes this linguistic shift to the political separation of East and West after Constantine’s sons divided the empire and Odoacer took Italy in 476 [PDF p. 170]. Barbarian invasions, particularly by Slavs, accelerated the loss of Latin speakers in the Balkans, reducing their proportion from a quarter in 400 to a tenth in 620 [PDF p. 170].

Christianity also abolished infanticide and improved the status of slaves, teaching that they possessed immortal souls [PDF p. 173]. Justinian’s laws further mitigated slavery’s evils by permitting mixed marriages and criminalizing the prostitution of slaves [PDF p. 173].

The ending focuses on the negative consequences of asceticism. The author contends that monasticism, which spread rapidly from Egypt, weakened the state by removing citizens from public duties [PDF p. 174]. Unlike later monasticism, fifth-century monks did not preserve literature or serve as missionaries [PDF p. 175]. This apathy toward barbarian invasions, viewed as divine judgment, angered the pagan minority, who blamed Christianity for the empire’s decline [PDF p. 176]. The author concludes that the empire survived because governing classes maintained Roman statecraft traditions despite widespread religious indifference among the educated [PDF p. 176].
