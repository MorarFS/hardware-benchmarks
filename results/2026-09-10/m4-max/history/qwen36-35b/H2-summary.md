The author argues that Latin decayed in the East due to practical separation from the West and barbarian devastation [PDF p. 170]. By the seventh century, Latin was rare, with Justinian being the last emperor to speak it natively [PDF p. 169]. The author attributes this linguistic shift to the division of the empire and the extermination of Latin-speaking populations by Slavs [PDF p. 170]. Simultaneously, the empire became increasingly Christian [PDF p. 171]. The author notes that Christian influence on state machinery began slowly, accelerating under Theodosius I [PDF p. 171]. The author highlights that gladiatorial games ended after a monk’s death in 404, not by state decree [PDF p. 171]. Christianity also abolished infanticide and modified slavery by emphasizing the soul’s equality [PDF p. 173]. The author credits these social improvements to the Christian belief in individual worth [PDF p. 174]. However, the author explicitly criticizes ascetic monasticism as harmful to the state [PDF p. 174]. The author claims monks neglected civic duties and viewed barbarian invasions as divine judgments rather than national crises [PDF p. 175]. This apathy angered pagans, who blamed Christianity for the empire’s ruin [PDF p. 176]. The author praises governing classes for maintaining Roman statecraft despite these challenges [PDF p. 176]. Finally, the author observes that while open paganism ended, indifference persisted among educated Christians [PDF p. 176].
