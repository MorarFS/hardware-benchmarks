The author argues that Constantine’s strategic foresight in establishing Constantinople proved vital as the Huns displaced the Goths [PDF p. 61]. After crushing the Ostrogoths, the Huns forced the Visigoths to seek refuge across the Danube in 376 [PDF p. 62]. The Eastern Emperor Valens, described by the author as stupid and cowardly, permitted their entry under strict conditions [PDF p. 63]. However, Roman officials, particularly Governor Lupicinus, engaged in extortion and withheld food supplies, driving the starving Goths to desperation [PDF p. 63]. A banquet incident at Marcianopolis escalated into open rebellion when Lupicinus attempted to assassinate Gothic leaders [PDF p. 64].

The author notes that the Goths, now fighting with renewed barbarian ferocity, initially held Roman forces at bay [PDF p. 64]. In 378, Emperor Valens launched a major offensive against the Gothic camp near Adrianople [PDF p. 65]. The author emphasizes that the Goths had adopted cavalry tactics, which proved decisive [PDF p. 65]. A sudden cavalry charge overwhelmed the Roman flank, causing the imperial army to collapse into a confused mass [PDF p. 66]. The resulting slaughter killed forty thousand Romans, including Valens himself [PDF p. 67].

The author characterizes this defeat as the most severe loss for Rome since Cannae [PDF p. 67]. Although the Goths advanced toward Constantinople, they abandoned the siege due to the city’s formidable walls [PDF p. 67]. The author concludes that this event validated Constantine’s earlier architectural and military planning [PDF p. 67].
